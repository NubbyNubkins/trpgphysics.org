Part I. The physics we know, from the beginning

Everything here is established physics, tested many times. It is told in order, because each idea grows out of the one before, and because the framework keeps all of it.

What physics is, and how anyone knows anything

For a 10-year-old

Close your eyes and imagine you are a detective. You cannot ask nature questions in words. You can only poke it and watch what happens: drop a stone, shine a light, smash two tiny things together. Then you guess a rule, and you use the rule to say what will happen next time. If you are right again and again, the rule is good. If you are wrong even once, and you checked carefully, the rule needs fixing. That is all physics is: careful guessing, tested by watching.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Physics does not prove things the way geometry does. It builds models: pictures with rules, which we trust because they keep predicting correctly. A model is a map, not the territory. Newton's map of gravity was perfect for centuries and is still used to fly spacecraft, yet Einstein's map replaced it where they disagree. A good theory is one that says clearly what would show it wrong, and then survives the attempt. This framework adopts that standard strictly: anything that can be shown wrong within it is labelled as failing.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Every science shares the method: measure, model, predict, test, revise. Physics differs mainly in how far it pushes mathematics, because its subjects (particles, fields, space) are simple enough that their rules can be written exactly. Chemistry, biology and engineering rest on physics, but each adds its own level of description, just as weather forecasting uses fluid dynamics without tracking every molecule.

For a physicist

Modern physics is organised as effective theories valid within a range of scales (Wilson, 1971). Each is complete within its range and breaks down outside it, where a deeper theory takes over and reproduces it as a limit. A theory of everything would be the theory with no outer boundary of validity. The framework presented here claims to be a step toward that: one structure from which the known effective theories follow, with every remaining measured input named.

For a mathematician

A physical theory is a mathematical structure (spaces, symmetries, equations) plus a dictionary connecting some of its quantities to measurements. Predictive power is roughly the number of independent measurements the structure reproduces minus the number of numbers put into the dictionary by hand.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

On falsifiability: Popper, The Logic of Scientific Discovery (1934/1959). On effective theories: Wilson and Kogut, Phys. Rep. 12, 75 (1974); Weinberg, Physica A 96, 327 (1979).

Motion and force: Galileo and Newton

For a 10-year-old

Kick a ball on ice and it slides a long way. Kick it on grass and it stops quickly. Galileo realised that things would keep moving forever if nothing slowed them down. Newton turned that into three simple rules: things keep doing what they are doing unless pushed; a push changes motion, more for light things and less for heavy things; and every push has an equal push back, which is why a rocket goes up when it throws gas down.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Before Galileo, people thought motion needed a constant cause, as a cart needs a horse. Galileo saw that the natural state is steady motion, and only change needs a cause. Newton made "force" the cause of change and showed that the same rules govern a falling apple and the orbiting Moon. This was the first great unification: heaven and earth obey one law.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Newton's second law, force equals mass times acceleration (F = ma), is the workhorse of engineering: bridges, engines, the paths of satellites. Mass here measures resistance to being accelerated (inertia).

For a physicist

Newtonian mechanics: F = dp/dt, with p = mv. It is the low-speed, weak-gravity limit of relativity and the large-action limit of quantum mechanics. Its reformulations by Lagrange (1788) and Hamilton (1833), through the principle of least action, are the language later used by every field theory, including the action on the Equations page.

For a mathematician

In Lagrangian form, a path q(t) is physical when it makes the action S = ∫ L(q, q̇) dt stationary, which gives the Euler–Lagrange equation d/dt (∂L/∂q̇) − ∂L/∂q = 0. For L = ½mq̇² − V(q) this is exactly F = ma with F = −dV/dq.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Galileo, Two New Sciences (1638); Newton, Principia (1687); Lagrange, Mécanique analytique (1788); Hamilton (1834–35).

Energy, and why it is never lost

For a 10-year-old

Lift a ball up a hill and let go: it rolls down faster and faster. The height turned into speed. When it stops, the speed turns into a little heat in the ground. Energy is like money that changes currency but never disappears: height-energy, speed-energy, heat-energy, light-energy, all the same money in different coins.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

The conservation of energy is one of the deepest facts known. In 1918 Emmy Noether showed why: every symmetry of the laws gives a quantity that never changes. Because the laws are the same today as tomorrow, energy is conserved; because they are the same here as there, momentum is conserved; because they are the same in every direction, spin and rotation are conserved. Conservation laws are what symmetry looks like.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

In chemistry, the energy released by burning fuel or stored in a battery is the energy of electric bonds between atoms rearranging. In biology, cells store energy in molecules such as ATP. The total is always balanced: the first law of thermodynamics.

For a physicist

Noether's theorem: for a continuous symmetry of the action there is a conserved current ∂μjμ = 0. Time-translation invariance gives energy conservation. In general relativity energy is conserved locally (∇μTμν = 0) but there is in general no global total for an expanding universe, which is why the framework's "the amount is kept" can only be checked locally.

For a mathematician

If the Lagrangian is invariant under q → q + εK(q), then Q = (∂L/∂q̇)·K is constant along solutions. For time translation the conserved quantity is the Hamiltonian H = pq̇ − L, the energy.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Noether, Nachr. Ges. Wiss. Göttingen, 235 (1918).

Gravity, Newton's version

For a 10-year-old

Everything with weight pulls on everything else. The Earth pulls you down; you pull the Earth up, but it is so heavy it does not notice. The pull gets weaker quickly as things get farther apart: twice as far, four times weaker. That one rule explains why the Moon goes around the Earth and the Earth goes around the Sun.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Newton's gravity acts instantly across empty space, which troubled Newton himself; he wrote that action at a distance without a medium was an absurdity. He gave the rule, not the mechanism. That unease waited two centuries for Einstein, and it is close in spirit to this framework's starting point, that there is no truly empty space for anything to act across.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

F = Gm₁m₂/r². The constant G is tiny (6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ in SI units), which is why gravity between everyday objects is unnoticeable and why it is the hardest force to measure precisely.

For a physicist

Newtonian gravity is the weak-field, slow-motion limit of general relativity: g00 ≈ −(1 + 2Φ/c²), with ∇²Φ = 4πGρ. Gravity's weakness relative to the other forces (about 10⁻³⁶ of the electric force between two protons) is part of the unexplained hierarchy.

For a mathematician

The inverse-square law follows from Gauss's law in three dimensions: the flux of the field through any closed surface is proportional to the enclosed mass, and the area of a sphere grows as r².

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Newton (1687); Cavendish (1798) for the first measurement of G; CODATA 2022 for its value.

Waves: how energy travels without things travelling

For a 10-year-old

Drop a pebble in a pond. Rings spread out, but the water itself just bobs up and down; it does not travel to the edge. A wave is a pattern that moves, carrying energy, while the stuff stays put. Sound is a wave in air, an ocean swell is a wave in water, and, as it turns out, light is a wave too.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Waves show that something can be real and travel without being a thing. When two waves meet they add: crest on crest makes a bigger crest, crest on trough makes calm. This adding, called interference, is the signature of waves, and it will reappear at the heart of quantum physics.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Waves have a wavelength (crest to crest), a frequency (how many pass per second) and a speed (their product). Engineers use interference in noise-cancelling headphones; chemists use it in X-ray crystallography to read the shapes of molecules such as DNA.

For a physicist

The wave equation ∂²u/∂t² = v²∇²u. Linear superposition holds for small amplitudes. Waves arise where energy spreads through a medium whose stiffness resists displacement, which is the framework's reading of why space carries waves: energy spreading through regions of different resistance.

For a mathematician

Solutions are superpositions of sinusoids u = A cos(kx − ωt) with ω = v|k| (Fourier, 1822). Any reasonable shape can be built from such sinusoids.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

d'Alembert (1747); Fourier, Théorie analytique de la chaleur (1822); Young (1803) for interference of light.

Electricity, magnetism and light: Faraday and Maxwell

For a 10-year-old

Rub a balloon on your hair and it sticks to the wall: that is electricity. A magnet grabs a paper clip: that is magnetism. In the 1800s people discovered that they are two faces of one thing. A moving magnet makes electricity (that is how power stations work) and moving electricity makes a magnet. Then came the big surprise: a wiggle of electricity and magnetism travels on its own, at an incredible speed, and that wiggle is light.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Faraday imagined invisible lines of force filling space. Maxwell turned the idea into four equations and found that they predicted waves moving at exactly the measured speed of light. Light, radio, X-rays and heat radiation were all one phenomenon. Fields became as real as matter. The idea of a field filling space is a direct ancestor of the framework's full medium.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Chemistry is almost entirely electromagnetism: atoms hold together because negative electrons are attracted to positive nuclei, and molecules form when electrons are shared. The colours of things, the conduction of nerves, and the workings of every electronic device are electromagnetic.

For a physicist

Maxwell's equations (1865) predict electromagnetic waves with speed c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀). Their symmetry is not Galilean but Lorentzian, which led to special relativity. Written with potentials, they are the simplest gauge theory, with symmetry U(1); the strong and weak forces are generalisations (Yang and Mills, 1954).

For a mathematician

In differential forms Maxwell's equations are dF = 0 and d⋆F = ⋆J, with F = dA. The gauge freedom A → A + dχ leaves F unchanged: the first example of a local symmetry.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Faraday, Experimental Researches (1839–55); Maxwell, Phil. Trans. R. Soc. 155, 459 (1865); Hertz (1888).

Heat, disorder and the direction of time

For a 10-year-old

Put an ice cube in warm lemonade. The ice melts and the lemonade cools until they are the same. It never goes the other way. Heat always spreads from hot to cold. Scientists measure how spread-out energy is with a number called entropy, which you can think of as messiness. In a closed box, messiness only ever goes up.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Boltzmann explained why: there are vastly more messy arrangements than tidy ones, so any change is overwhelmingly likely to go toward mess. This gives time its direction. It also leaves a question: why was the universe so tidy at the start? The framework treats the start as outside its scope, while agreeing that tidy pockets (stars, life) grow by riding the flow toward mess.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

The second law sets the efficiency limit of every engine and explains why living things must eat: they keep their internal order by exporting disorder. Chemical reactions run in the direction that increases total entropy, which is why some reactions happen on their own and others need energy.

For a physicist

S = kB ln W (Boltzmann, 1877). Statistical mechanics derives thermodynamics from microscopic dynamics. Black holes carry entropy proportional to their horizon area (Bekenstein, 1973; Hawking, 1975), the first sign that gravity, information and geometry are connected, and the seed of the entanglement route to gravity the framework uses.

For a mathematician

For N independent two-state systems, W = 2N and S = NkB ln 2: entropy adds for independent parts. The overwhelming dominance of the most probable macrostate grows exponentially with N.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Carnot (1824); Clausius (1865); Boltzmann (1877); Gibbs (1902); Bekenstein, Phys. Rev. D 7, 2333 (1973).

Special relativity: space and time are one

For a 10-year-old

Light always goes the same speed, whether you run toward it or away from it. That sounds impossible, like a car that looks just as fast whether you are standing still or driving beside it. Einstein said: if light's speed never changes, then time and distance must change instead. Fast-moving clocks tick slower. And mass is a kind of frozen energy: E = mc². A tiny bit of mass holds a huge amount of energy.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Einstein took two principles seriously: the laws are the same for everyone moving steadily, and light's speed is the same for everyone. The price was the idea of absolute time. What one observer calls "at the same moment" another does not. Space and time merge into spacetime. This shows that what you measure depends on your perspective, while the underlying reality (spacetime intervals) does not, an idea very close to the framework's rule that every observation is one perspective on one geometry.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Relativity is engineering, not speculation: GPS satellites must correct their clocks by about 38 microseconds per day for relativistic effects, or positions would drift by kilometres. Nuclear energy is E = mc² in action.

For a physicist

Lorentz transformations preserve the interval ds² = −c²dt² + dx² + dy² + dz². Time dilation and length contraction follow with γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²). Energy and momentum form a four-vector with E² = (pc)² + (mc²)². Michelson and Morley (1887) found no motion relative to a luminiferous ether; any medium in a modern theory must therefore have no detectable rest frame, a point the framework addresses directly (see "How the framework meets relativity").

For a mathematician

The Lorentz group O(1,3) is the group of linear maps preserving the quadratic form −t² + x² + y² + z². Physical laws are written as tensor equations, which keeps them valid in every inertial frame.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Einstein, Ann. Phys. 17, 891 (1905) and 18, 639 (1905); Minkowski (1908); Michelson and Morley, Am. J. Sci. 34, 333 (1887).

General relativity: gravity is the shape of spacetime

For a 10-year-old

Imagine a stretched trampoline. Put a bowling ball in the middle and it makes a dip. Roll a marble nearby and it curves around the dip, not because the bowling ball pulls it with a rope, but because the surface is bent. Einstein said gravity works like that: heavy things bend space and time, and other things follow the bends. Even light bends around the Sun.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Einstein's happiest thought was that a person falling freely feels no weight. Gravity is not a force in the usual sense; it is the geometry of spacetime. "Spacetime tells matter how to move; matter tells spacetime how to curve" (Wheeler). This removed Newton's action at a distance: the influence travels through the curved fabric at the speed of light, as gravitational waves.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

General relativity predicted the bending of starlight (measured 1919), the slow shift of Mercury's orbit, black holes, the expansion of the universe, and gravitational waves (detected by LIGO in 2015). Clocks at sea level run measurably slower than clocks on a mountain.

For a physicist

Einstein's field equations Gμν + Λgμν = 8πG Tμν/c⁴. The deflection of light at the Sun's edge is 1.75″, twice the Newtonian value. The framework reads curvature as the resistance of the medium acting equally on time and space, reproduces 1.75″ and the Shapiro delay, and inherits the factor of two from general relativity rather than deriving it independently.

For a mathematician

Spacetime is a four-dimensional Lorentzian manifold; gravity is its metric g. Free particles follow geodesics. The Einstein tensor Gμν = Rμν − ½Rgμν is the unique divergence-free tensor built from the metric and its first two derivatives (Lovelock, 1971), which is why the equation has the form it does.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Einstein, Sitzungsber. Preuss. Akad. Wiss. 844 (1915); Dyson, Eddington and Davidson (1920); Abbott and others (LIGO/Virgo), Phys. Rev. Lett. 116, 061102 (2016).

The quantum: energy comes in packets

For a 10-year-old

Heat a piece of metal and it glows red, then orange, then white. Scientists in 1900 could not explain those colours until Max Planck guessed that light energy comes in tiny packets, like coins instead of a smooth stream. Einstein showed that light really does arrive in packets, now called photons. And Niels Bohr showed that electrons in atoms can only sit on certain steps, like a ladder with no in-between rungs. When an electron jumps down a step, it gives off one packet of light with exactly one colour.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

The quantum discovery was that nature is grainy at small scales: energy, light and motion come in discrete amounts. This was the end of the idea that everything changes smoothly. It explained why atoms are stable, why each element has its own colour fingerprint, and why things have the properties they do.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Every spectroscopy technique in chemistry, from identifying elements in stars to the structure of molecules, rests on quantised energy levels. Lasers, LEDs and solar cells are quantum devices.

For a physicist

E = hν (Planck, 1900; Einstein, 1905). Bohr's model (1913) quantised angular momentum and gave hydrogen's levels En = −13.6 eV/n². De Broglie (1924) gave matter a wavelength λ = h/p. The framework's link value of the particle matrix, (m₁ + m₂)/√(m₁m₂), reproduces atomic binding energies (hydrogen 13.598 eV, positronium 6.803 eV) because it is exactly the reduced-mass factor written as a relationship.

For a mathematician

Planck's constant h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s sets the scale at which the action of a process becomes comparable to one quantum; when action is large compared with h, classical physics is recovered.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Planck, Ann. Phys. 4, 553 (1901); Einstein, Ann. Phys. 17, 132 (1905); Bohr, Phil. Mag. 26, 1 (1913); de Broglie (1924).

Quantum mechanics: waves of possibility, uncertainty and measurement

For a 10-year-old

In the tiny world, a particle is not a little marble in one spot. It is more like a cloud of maybe: maybe here, maybe there, more likely in some places than others. The cloud spreads and ripples like a wave. When you look, you find the particle in one spot, and the cloud tells you the odds of where. And there is a rule: the more exactly you know where something is, the less you can know about how fast it is going. It is not that our tools are clumsy. It is how nature is.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Quantum mechanics (1925–27) describes the state of a system by a wavefunction, a list of possibilities with amplitudes. The amplitudes evolve smoothly and add like waves, so possibilities interfere. When a measurement is made, one result appears, with probability given by the square of the amplitude (Born's rule). Heisenberg's uncertainty principle says certain pairs of properties cannot both be sharp. What the wavefunction is (knowledge, reality, a branch of many worlds) is still debated. The framework reads each measurement as taking one perspective on one geometry: the whole is the wave, the result is one view of it.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Quantum mechanics explains the periodic table: electrons fill orbitals (shapes of probability clouds) according to exclusion and energy, which sets every element's chemistry. Transistors, MRI scanners and the shapes of molecules all follow from it. It is the most precisely tested theory in science.

For a physicist

The Schrödinger equation iħ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ. Observables are Hermitian operators; their eigenvalues are the possible results; the Born rule P = |⟨a|ψ⟩|². Heisenberg: Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2, from the commutator [x̂, p̂] = iħ. Superposition, interference and entanglement follow from linearity and the tensor-product structure. The framework keeps all of this as inherited rules.

For a mathematician

States are unit vectors in a complex Hilbert space; time evolution is a one-parameter unitary group U(t) = e−iĤt/ħ (Stone's theorem). Complex numbers are essential: a theory over the reals cannot reproduce all quantum predictions (Renou and others, 2021). In the framework, the complex numbers of quantum mechanics are the ones left after one imaginary unit of the octonions is fixed by the observer.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Heisenberg, Z. Phys. 33, 879 (1925); Schrödinger, Ann. Phys. 79, 361 (1926); Born, Z. Phys. 37, 863 (1926); Heisenberg, Z. Phys. 43, 172 (1927); Dirac, The Principles of Quantum Mechanics (1930); Renou and others, Nature 600, 625 (2021).

Entanglement and Bell's theorem

For a 10-year-old

Two particles can be born as partners, so that whatever one does, the other does the opposite, no matter how far apart they fly. Measure one here and you instantly know the answer for the other on the Moon. Experiments prove the answers were not secretly decided in advance. Yet you still cannot use this to send a message, because each answer on its own looks random.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Entanglement is the feature Schrödinger called the defining trait of quantum theory. Bell (1964) turned the philosophical worry of Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen into an experiment, and experiments chose quantum mechanics: nature is not made of separate pieces carrying their own local properties. The framework makes this its foundation: relationships come first, and distance is a property of the space that relationships build.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Entanglement is now a technology: quantum cryptography, quantum computers and quantum sensors use it. The 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger for the experiments.

For a physicist

For the singlet state, quantum mechanics gives E(a, b) = −cos θab and a CHSH value up to 2√2, against a local-realistic bound of 2. Loophole-free violations: Hensen and others (2015), Giustina and others (2015), Shalm and others (2015). No-signalling holds because reduced density matrices are unaffected by distant operations.

For a mathematician

A pure state of two systems is entangled when it is not a product |a⟩⊗|b⟩. Its entanglement entropy S = −Tr ρA ln ρA measures how much. For regions of a quantum field, this entropy scales with the boundary area, which is the starting point of the entanglement route to gravity.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Schrödinger, Proc. Camb. Phil. Soc. 31, 555 (1935); Bell (1964); Clauser, Horne, Shimony and Holt, Phys. Rev. Lett. 23, 880 (1969); Aspect and others (1982).

Quantum fields: the vacuum is full, and antimatter

For a 10-year-old

Scientists found that particles are not little things moving through empty space. Each kind of particle is a ripple in a field that fills all of space, like waves on an ocean that covers the whole world. An electron is a ripple in the electron field; light is a ripple in the light field. Even when there are no ripples, the ocean is still there, gently fizzing. And every kind of particle has an opposite twin, antimatter, that can wipe it out in a flash of light.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Quantum field theory joined quantum mechanics with special relativity. Its picture is strikingly close to the framework's: space is never empty, the vacuum is the ground state of fields that fill everything, and particles are excitations of that fullness. Dirac's equation (1928) predicted antimatter before it was seen (Anderson, 1932).

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

PET scanners in hospitals detect the flashes from positrons (anti-electrons) meeting electrons. The vacuum's fizzing is measurable: two metal plates very close together are pushed toward each other (the Casimir effect), and atomic energy levels are shifted slightly (the Lamb shift).

For a physicist

Quantum electrodynamics (Tomonaga, Schwinger, Feynman, Dyson, 1946–49) predicts the electron's magnetic moment to about one part in 10¹², the most precise prediction in science. Renormalisation absorbs infinities into measured parameters; theories where this works with finitely many parameters are renormalisable. Gravity treated this way is not.

For a mathematician

A free field is an infinite set of harmonic oscillators, one per wave vector; particles are their quanta. Each oscillator has zero-point energy ½ħω, and their sum diverges, which is the origin of the vacuum energy problem.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Dirac, Proc. R. Soc. A 117, 610 (1928); Anderson, Phys. Rev. 43, 491 (1933); Feynman, Phys. Rev. 76, 769 (1949); Casimir (1948); Lamb and Retherford (1947).

The Standard Model and the Higgs

For a 10-year-old

Scientists made a family tree of every kind of tiny bubble of matter and every kind of force carrier. There are six quarks, six leptons (like the electron and the neutrino), the force carriers (the photon for light, the gluons for the strong grip, the W and Z for the weak nudge), and the Higgs, which gives things their weight by making them wade through a kind of invisible syrup. This family tree is called the Standard Model, and it has passed every test for fifty years.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

The Standard Model is humanity's most successful theory of matter. Its structure is dictated by symmetry: once the symmetries are chosen, the forces follow. But the symmetries themselves, the number of families, the particle charges and about 19 numbers are chosen to fit experiment. It is like a superb machine delivered without the blueprint explaining why it was built this way. The framework aims at that blueprint.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

The Higgs boson was found at CERN's Large Hadron Collider in 2012 at 125 GeV. The Higgs field gives mass to the W and Z bosons and to the quarks and electrons, but not most of the mass of everyday matter, which comes from the strong-force energy inside protons and neutrons.

For a physicist

Gauge group SU(3)c × SU(2)L × U(1)Y; three generations of 15 Weyl fermions (16 with a right-handed neutrino); the Higgs doublet with electroweak symmetry breaking (Englert and Brout; Higgs; Guralnik, Hagen and Kibble, 1964). The electroweak theory (Glashow, 1961; Weinberg, 1967; Salam, 1968) and quantum chromodynamics (Gross and Wilczek; Politzer, 1973). The framework derives the gauge group and one generation's charges from the relationship matrix; the Higgs value and mass remain measured.

For a mathematician

The Standard Model is a Yang–Mills theory with compact gauge group G and chiral fermions in a representation free of gauge anomalies. The global form of the group is (SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1))/ℤ₆, which is the form obtained from the exceptional Jordan algebra.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

ATLAS and CMS, Phys. Lett. B 716, 1 and 30 (2012); PDG 2024.

Atoms, chemistry, nuclei and stars

For a 10-year-old

An atom is like a tiny solar system, but stranger: a heavy centre made of protons and neutrons, with electrons in clouds around it. Two electrons can never be in exactly the same state, so they stack into layers, and those layers decide how atoms join to make water, salt, sugar and you. Deep in the Sun, the centres of hydrogen atoms are squeezed so hard that they fuse into helium, and a little mass becomes the sunlight that warms your face.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

All of chemistry follows from quantum mechanics plus one rule, Pauli's exclusion principle. The richness of the material world, from crystals to cells, is the electric force and exclusion acting on a few kinds of particles. Stars are balances: gravity pulling in, pressure from fusion pushing out. That balance of push and pull, holding a shape, is exactly what the framework calls containment.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Chemists know the periodic table as the pattern of electron shells. Nuclear physics sets which isotopes are stable; the balance formula of Weizsäcker (1935) gives nuclear binding to within a few MeV across almost three thousand nuclei. Stellar fusion (Bethe, 1939) made every element heavier than helium; the iron in your blood was made in stars.

For a physicist

The framework describes every bound object as a bubble whose edge is the balance point between containment and leakage. For hadrons this is the MIT bag model (Chodos, Jaffe, Johnson, Thorn and Weisskopf, 1974), with the bubble pressure B = 60 (57–64) MeV/fm³ fixed by the proton and Δ masses. The edge radius scales as (B + P)−1/4, so packed nucleons change shape slightly (the EMC effect, 1983). The same B gives a maximum neutron-star mass of 2.0–2.15 Suns, against 2.08 ± 0.07 measured, once quarks in the core pair; the pairing gap, about 82–97 MeV, is the prediction.

The universe as a whole

For a 10-year-old

Every galaxy is moving away from every other, like raisins in rising bread. Run the movie backwards and everything was once squeezed together, hot and bright, about 13.8 billion years ago. We can still see the afterglow of that time as a faint glow in every direction. And there are two big mysteries: something invisible (dark matter) holds galaxies together, and something else (dark energy) makes the whole universe stretch faster and faster.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Cosmology is the history of everything. Its standard model, called ΛCDM, fits an astonishing range of data with six numbers, but about 95% of what it describes (dark matter and dark energy) is of unknown nature. The framework replaces the image of an explosion with a still pond in a light drizzle: a flat surface with tiny variations, where a droplet makes rings that spread, and ours need not be the only droplet. It does not try to say what made the drop.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Key observations: the expansion (Hubble, 1929), the cosmic microwave background (Penzias and Wilson, 1965), the abundances of hydrogen, helium and lithium, the cosmic web of galaxies, and the accelerating expansion (Riess and others; Perlmutter and others, 1998).

For a physicist

Part II. Where it breaks

The two great theories of the twentieth century disagree, and a list of facts is explained by neither.

Two great theories that disagree

For a 10-year-old

We now have two amazing rulebooks: one for the very small (quantum) and one for the very big (relativity). Each has passed every test. But they speak different languages. In the small book, everything jitters and is uncertain. In the big book, space is smooth and calm. In places where both matter at once, inside black holes and at the very start of the universe, putting the two books together gives answers like "infinity", which is the universe's way of saying "you made a mistake".

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

The conflict is about what space and time are. In quantum theory time is an external clock and space a fixed stage; in general relativity both are dynamic and part of what is being described. Quantising the shape of spacetime the usual way produces infinities that cannot be absorbed. Many physicists suspect the problem is conceptual: one of the two theories is describing something that is not fundamental.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

For everyday science the clash is invisible: quantum effects of gravity matter only at the Planck length, 1.6 × 10⁻³⁵ m, a hundred billion billion times smaller than a proton. But a theory of everything must work there too.

For a physicist

Perturbative quantum gravity is non-renormalisable: the two-loop divergence of Goroff and Sagnotti (1986) requires a new counterterm, and so on at every order. Further puzzles: the problem of time in canonical gravity (Wheeler–DeWitt equation), black hole information loss (Hawking, 1976), and singularities (Penrose, 1965; Hawking and Penrose, 1970).

For a mathematician

Newton's constant has dimensions of length squared in natural units; couplings with negative mass dimension make perturbation theory non-renormalisable, since each order brings higher powers of momentum.

The numbers nobody can explain

For a 10-year-old

The Standard Model works like a recipe with about two dozen amounts written in by hand: how heavy each particle is, how strong each force is. Nobody knows why these amounts are what they are. Some are wildly different from each other, like a recipe asking for one grain of salt and a mountain of flour.

The attempts so far, and what each taught

For a 10-year-old

Many brilliant people have tried to write one rulebook for everything. Some said all the forces were once one force. Some said every particle has a hidden partner. Some said particles are tiny vibrating strings in a world with extra hidden directions. Some said space is made of tiny loops. Each idea taught us something important, even when it did not finish the job. This framework learned from all of them.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Part III. The framework, from scratch

The framework built step by step, from one picture to the forces and particles, and how it meets every rule that quantum mechanics and relativity say cannot be broken.

Step 1. The full glass: one medium with no gaps

For a 10-year-old

Close your eyes and picture a glass, half water and half air. Is it half empty? No: it is completely full. Every bit of it holds something. Now picture a sealed bag full of water, with marbles floating in it. There is no gap between the water and the marbles. Now the strange part: imagine that the water is space itself. Then there is nowhere in the universe that is truly empty. Everything that happens is the water of space doing something: rippling, swirling, bunching up into bubbles.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

The framework's first idea is that space is a single, completely filled medium. "Empty space" is a misunderstanding: what we call emptiness is the medium at rest. This is not the old ether, a substance sitting in space; it is space itself, and it has no rest frame you could detect by moving through it (see "How the framework meets relativity" below). From this idea come two working rules: relationships in the medium can never combine into nothing (no holes in the glass), and the total amount is kept, though it can only be checked locally, the way a ripple on a pond can be followed but the whole pond cannot be weighed at once.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Physicists already describe the vacuum as full: quantum fields fill all space, and their ground state has measurable effects. A useful material comparison is superfluid helium near absolute zero: a liquid so orderly that waves move through it without friction, and in which some excitations behave like particles. Volovik (2003) showed that many features of relativity and particle physics appear naturally in such a liquid.

For a physicist

Formally the principle has two parts. Rule 1 (no holes): if two relationships a and b satisfy ab = 0, one of them was already zero, which is the division-algebra condition. Rule 2 (amount kept): the norm is multiplicative, |ab| = |a||b|. Together these restrict the inner algebra of the medium (next step). The medium's ground state is Lorentz invariant, as the quantum vacuum is, so it singles out no frame of rest.

For a mathematician

An algebra over ℝ with no zero divisors and a multiplicative positive-definite norm is a normed division algebra. Hurwitz (1898) proved there are exactly four: ℝ, ℂ, ℍ and 𝕆. The two pictures (the full glass and the kept amount) are therefore exactly the axioms of Hurwitz's theorem.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Sources: Hurwitz (1898); Volovik, The Universe in a Helium Droplet (2003); Baez, Bull. AMS 39, 145 (2002).

Where it stands. A starting principle. With established physics it reproduces the flatness and the age of the universe; it does not by itself go beyond standard cosmology.

Step 2. Bubbles: mass is where push meets pull

For a 10-year-old

Blow a soap bubble. The air inside pushes out; the soap film pulls in. Where the two balance, that is where the edge of the bubble sits. Squeeze it gently and the edge moves; let go and it moves back. The framework says every particle is like that: a bubble in the water of space, where a push outward and a pull inward balance. What we call mass, the "weight" of a thing, is the energy held at that balance. When two bubbles touch, their edges squish together like soap bubbles in a sink. A planet is a giant grouping of these balances, bubbles squished together.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Mass has long seemed to appear from nowhere. The framework's idea is that mass is not stuff but a balance: the point where containment (inward pull) and leakage (outward push) are equal. The edge of a particle is not a wall; it is a place where back-and-forth is equal. Splitting an atom does not destroy anything; it changes the balance, and the difference is released. This explains why most of a proton's mass is binding energy, and why fission releases the energy it does.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

For a chemist: about 99% of the mass of the atoms in your body is the energy of quarks and gluons held inside protons and neutrons, not the mass of the quarks themselves. Nuclear binding energies, and the energy of fission (about 170 MeV per uranium nucleus), are changes in this balance energy. The framework's balance picture reproduces the fission energy as 166 MeV against 173 MeV measured.

For a physicist

For hadrons the bubble is the MIT bag (Chodos and others, 1974): quarks inside a region where the vacuum pressure B pushes inward. The framework fixes B = 60 (57–64) MeV/fm³ from the proton and Δ masses alone and writes it as a containment field P with a two-state potential (Friedberg and Lee, 1977), with wall thickness about 0.11 fm. The edge radius goes as (B + P)−1/4 with external pressure P. The same B, with a quark pairing gap of about 82–97 MeV (compact stars modelled as self-bound strange stars; 88–109 MeV for a running strange-quark mass), gives a maximum mass of 2.0–2.15 Suns (measured 2.08 ± 0.07) and tidal deformability consistent with GW170817.

For a mathematician

E(R) = a/R + bR³ with a = Σ(quark kinetic terms) and b = 4πB/3. Setting dE/dR = 0 gives R⁴ = a/3b, so R ∝ B−1/4; adding an outside pressure P replaces B by B + P. The energy at the minimum is (4/3)a/R, which ties mass to the balance point directly.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Sources: Chodos, Jaffe, Johnson, Thorn and Weisskopf (1974); Friedberg and Lee (1977); Ji, Phys. Rev. Lett. 74, 1071 (1995) for the proton mass decomposition; Aubert and others (EMC), Phys. Lett. B 123, 275 (1983).

Where it stands. Holds: the proton's balance energy, fission (166 against 173 MeV), the bubble pressure B, and the neutron-star cap all follow. Strict numbers for the quark pull fail by 16%. For elementary particles the bubble is a reading, since no edge is seen down to 10⁻¹⁹ m.

Step 3. Relationships first, and why they must be octonions

For a 10-year-old

Imagine the numbers you know: 1, 2, 3, and the fractions in between. Mathematicians found bigger kinds of numbers that point in several directions at once: a two-direction kind, a four-direction kind, and an eight-direction kind called octonions. Then a mathematician named Hurwitz proved something amazing: if you want numbers where two real things never multiply to make nothing, and sizes multiply properly, there are only those four kinds, and the biggest has eight directions. No nine, no sixteen. The rules of the full glass pick the octonions.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

The framework says the universe is made of relationships, not of things taken one at a time. What matters is how each part relates to every other. If relationships must obey the full-glass rules, a theorem forces their arithmetic to be one of four systems, and the richest is the octonions. The octonions are strange: the order of multiplying matters, and even the grouping matters. That strangeness turns out to be exactly what is needed to make the forces of nature.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

A small analogy: in chemistry, the angles of bonds in a molecule are not chosen freely; they follow from the geometry of electron orbitals. Here the "bond angles" of relationships follow from the arithmetic that relationships are allowed to have.

For a physicist

The octonions have seven imaginary units e₁ to e₇ whose multiplication follows the Fano plane. Their symmetry group is G₂ (dimension 14). Relations among three parts are described by 3×3 Hermitian octonion matrices, the exceptional Jordan algebra J₃(𝕆) of dimension 27, with symmetry F₄ (dimension 52). This algebra was found by Jordan, von Neumann and Wigner (1934) while searching for the possible algebras of quantum observables.

For a mathematician

An element of J₃(𝕆) is X = ((α, c, b̄), (c̄, β, a), (b, ā, γ)) with α, β, γ real and a, b, c ∈ 𝕆, giving 3 + 3×8 = 27 real dimensions. The Jordan product X∘Y = ½(XY + YX) is commutative and satisfies the Jordan identity only because 3×3 is small enough for alternativity to cancel the associator terms. The framework reads relations among states three at a time with the triple product {X, Y, Z}, in which the middle entry enters as its mirror image (Jordan, 1933; Loos, 1977).

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Sources: Graves (1843) and Cayley (1845) for the octonions; Hurwitz (1898); Jordan, von Neumann and Wigner, Ann. Math. 35, 29 (1934); Freudenthal (1954) and Tits (1966) for F₄; Loos, Bounded Symmetric Domains and Jordan Pairs (1977).

Where it stands. Mathematically exact (Hurwitz's theorem). The physical claim is that the medium's relationships obey the two rules of Step 1.

Step 4. One spot, one facing: the forces, the charges, and the side we see

For a 10-year-old

Hold a toy in front of you and look at it. You only ever see one side. Walk around and you see another side, but never all sides at once. The framework says that is how we see the universe: always from one spot, facing one way. Now here is the magic part. Take the eight-direction octonion pattern, look at it from one spot, facing one way, and count what stays the same. You get exactly 8 strong-force carriers, 3 weak-force carriers and 1 more: the same numbers scientists measure. And because you are facing one way, the weak force only touches things spinning one way. Left-handedness is the side you observe.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Every observation is taken from a perspective, and only one perspective at a time. This is Heisenberg's insight, generalised. In the framework it is a precise act: fixing one direction among the octonions' seven imaginary directions. Once it is fixed, the symmetry that remains is exactly the symmetry of the Standard Model's forces, and the particles of one family appear with exactly their measured charges. The forces are not added to the world; they are what the one structure looks like from inside. Handedness, which the Standard Model simply assumes, is the side from which we look.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Think of a crystal again: from outside, a cube; but stand at one corner and look along one axis, and you see a different, smaller set of symmetries. Which symmetries survive depends on where you stand. The framework uses this to explain why nature has the particular forces it does.

For a physicist

Fixing a complex structure (one imaginary unit) reduces G₂ to SU(3): three colours and eight gluons. Within F₄, also fixing a rank-one idempotent leaves (SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1))/ℤ₆ (Todorov and Dubois-Violette, 2018). The three-way product of the matrix's link states gives the 16 of one generation with Standard Model hypercharges, including the right-handed neutrino, and no mirror states. The far side holds exactly one weak pair of charge ½, which gives ρ = MW²/(MZ² cos²θW) = 1 (measured 1.0003 ± 0.0005 after known corrections). That forces act locally is a theorem of relativity plus quantum mechanics (Weinberg, 1964), inherited.

For a mathematician

𝕆 ≅ ℂ ⊕ ℂ³ as a representation of SU(3) = StabG₂(i). Lie algebra dimensions: 8 (su(3)) + 3 (su(2)) + 1 (u(1)) = 12, a subalgebra of f₄ (52). The quotient by ℤ₆ is fixed by the requirement that all representations appearing are faithful on the stated charges.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Where it stands. Step 5. Gravity from entanglement

For a 10-year-old

Remember the two partner particles that always know about each other? In the framework, those partners are connected through the water of space itself, so there is no distance for a message to cross. Now imagine an enormous number of such connections, everywhere. Gravity is what all those connections add up to: the way the water of space bends because of how its parts are tied together. Gravity is not a separate rope; it is the knitting.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Gravity is the one force the Standard Model leaves out. The framework does not add it as a fourth force; it treats it as a consequence of entanglement, the relationships among all parts of the medium. This is why the framework does not need to quantise gravity: you do not quantise temperature, you quantise the molecules.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

The thermodynamic picture: Jacobson (1995) showed that if the entanglement across any small patch of space is proportional to its area, and heat obeys the usual thermodynamic law, Einstein's equation of gravity follows. Gravity is the equation of state of space.

This also explains why vacuum energy does not gravitate (it drops out), leaving the dark energy to be set separately: on the main line, by the grain and the horizon of space together, with its amount measured. Step 6. Three families, the lepton cone and the quark ladders

For a 10-year-old

Matter comes in three families: light, medium and heavy copies. The framework sees the three as one shape placed three ways. For the electron family, the three masses sit like three points around a cone, tilted at exactly 45 degrees, the angle of perfect balance between "me" and "my relationships". From that one angle and one scale, you can work out the heaviest member, the tau, before weighing it. For the quark families, the pattern is more like a spiral leaning off the cone, and the two quark ladders lean a little against each other. That lean is called the tilt.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

The framework's rule 7 says a particle's relation to itself and its relations to the others are as uncertain as possible relative to each other: the 45° balance. For leptons this gives a striking rule known since the early 1980s (Koide), which the framework reads as perspective rather than coincidence. The quarks do not obey the simple cone, and the framework says so; the lean between the two quark ladders (the tilt) is the measured geometry of how one family is seen from another.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Picture three needles of different lengths, standing on a table at the same angle and spaced evenly around a circle. The lengths are the square roots of the three masses. For the electron, muon and tau the angle is 45°; for the quarks the needles lean more and their circles are not centred on the same axis.

Step 7. The far side: hidden partners, light neutrinos and dark matter

For a 10-year-old

Every family in the pattern has partners on the far side, heavy hidden bubbles that never show up in our experiments directly. One of them is the neutrino's partner. It sits on the other end of a seesaw from the ordinary neutrino: because the partner is so heavy, the neutrino is pushed up so high that it barely weighs anything. When the partner got its weight, early in the universe, it left behind a soft, quiet wave. So many of those waves overlap that they act like an invisible mist with weight: that mist may be the dark matter.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

The framework's matrix has a near side (what we see) and a far side (heavy partners we only see through their effects). The far side explains why neutrinos are so light and supplies a dark matter candidate without adding anything to the structure. It may also explain the excess of matter, if the universe started cold. What the framework cannot yet do is fix the far side's weights; it names this as the one remaining kind of missing rule.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

The seesaw: if a light particle is linked to a very heavy one, the light one's mass is roughly (link)²/(heavy mass). With heavy partners around 10⁶ to 10¹¹ GeV, the neutrinos come out at hundredths of an electronvolt, as measured.

Right-handed neutrinos at 0.6–1.9 × 10⁵, 2.2–6.2 × 10⁸ and 1.3–1.65 × 10¹² GeV (full three-by-three seesaw with the measured mixing). The rules, stated plainly

For a 10-year-old

Here is the whole framework in seven sentences. One: space is a full glass with no holes. Two: the amount in it is kept. Three: forces act right where things are, never by magic at a distance. Four: you always see from one spot, facing one way. Five: the electron family shares one pattern of relationships. Six: nothing extra is added. Seven: a particle's relation to itself and to the others are balanced as evenly as possible.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Each rule comes with an honest note. Rules 1 and 2 are the founding pictures and force the octonions. Rule 3 is inherited from relativity and quantum mechanics, not derived. Rule 4 is the perspective and produces the forces, the charges and handedness. Rule 5 holds for the leptons only. Rule 6, "nothing extra", is too strong as written, because at least one further Higgs piece is needed. Rule 7, with the family symmetry, gives the leptons' 45° balance; it does not hold for quarks.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Beyond the rules, the framework keeps every measured rule of quantum mechanics and relativity: the Born rule, the uncertainty principle, the speed of light, the equivalence principle. It changes what those rules describe, not the rules themselves.

For a physicist

The founding principles, as stated in the paper, are: a single filled medium; contents that always fill it; a resistance acting equally on time and space; containment (push against pull); each particle's own scale of view, ours being the proton's; entanglement as fundamental; and a geometry that keeps transitioning while flat on average. The seven working rules above are how these principles are used in calculation.

For a mathematician

How the framework meets everything quantum mechanics says cannot be

For a 10-year-old

Quantum physics has some strict "you can't" rules. You can't know exactly where something is and how fast it moves at the same time. You can't copy a tiny particle's state perfectly. You can't send a message faster than light, even with entangled partners. And you can't explain the strange results by saying the answers were secretly written down in advance. The framework breaks none of these rules. It keeps them all, and gives a picture of why they are true: we always see one side of one connected shape.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Any new picture of the world must respect the no-go theorems that experiments have confirmed. Here is how the framework meets each one. Uncertainty: complementary views of one geometry cannot be taken at once; that is the meaning of perspective. Bell's theorem: it rules out local hidden variables; the framework has none, because relationship comes before location. Kochen–Specker (contextuality): results depend on the whole measurement context, which is exactly what a perspective is. No signalling: entangled correlations carry no message, because each side alone sees randomness; the framework keeps this. No cloning: an unknown state cannot be copied; the framework adds no copying process. Unitarity: probabilities always add to one; kept. Exclusion: two identical fermions cannot share a state; in the framework this is the one kind of "nothing" that the three-way rule allows, the same state twice.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

In practice: every calculation the framework makes for an experiment uses ordinary quantum mechanics and quantum field theory. It does not propose a new equation of motion for quantum systems. Its additions concern which particles and forces exist and why, not how quantum states evolve.

For a physicist

Heisenberg (1927); Bell (1964); Kochen and Specker (1967); no-signalling (Ghirardi, Rimini and Weber, 1980); no-cloning (Wootters and Zurek, 1982; Dieks, 1982); unitarity; Pauli (1925) and the spin-statistics theorem (Pauli, 1940). The framework retains the Hilbert-space formalism over ℂ, which in its reading is the complex slice of the octonions fixed by the observer; non-associativity is confined to the internal (charge) structure and never enters the state space, so none of these theorems is affected.

For a mathematician

The no-go theorems are theorems about complex Hilbert spaces and their tensor products. Since the framework's state space is exactly such a space once the perspective is fixed, each theorem holds verbatim. In the three-way product {X, Y, Z}, {X, X, Y} = 0 can occur for nonzero X only for identical states, which is the algebraic shadow of exclusion.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Sources: Kochen and Specker, J. Math. Mech. 17, 59 (1967); Wootters and Zurek, Nature 299, 802 (1982); Pauli, Phys. Rev. 58, 716 (1940).

Where it stands. Consistent on every point, because the framework keeps quantum mechanics' measured rules and changes the reading of what they describe.

How the framework meets everything relativity says cannot be

For a 10-year-old

Relativity has its own "you can't" rules. Nothing can go faster than light. You can't tell if you are moving steadily or standing still without looking outside. And over a hundred years ago an experiment showed there is no old-fashioned "ether" that light swims through. If space is a full glass of water, doesn't that bring the ether back? No. Think of the water of space as being the same however you move through it, like a perfectly smooth fog that looks the same in every direction no matter how fast you fly. You can never feel a wind from it.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

The ether of the nineteenth century was a substance with a state of rest; moving through it should have produced a detectable "ether wind", and Michelson and Morley found none. The framework's medium is space itself, and its resting state looks the same to every observer in steady motion, just as the quantum vacuum of modern physics does. So it keeps the principle of relativity: no observer is special. Gravity as curvature, the equivalence of falling and floating, and the cosmic speed limit are all kept.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Precision tests confirm Lorentz symmetry to about one part in 10¹⁸ or better in some sectors; the framework does not violate it. Its account of light bending (1.75″) and the Shapiro radar delay agree with measurements.

For a physicist

The medium's ground state is Lorentz invariant (⟨Tμν⟩ ∝ gμν), so it defines no frame. The resistance reading of curvature acts equally on time and space, which is what gives the full relativistic factor; that equality is inherited from general relativity. Gravitational waves travel at c, as confirmed by GW170817 to one part in 10¹⁵.

For a mathematician

A stress tensor proportional to the metric is the only Lorentz-invariant one; such a vacuum is invisible to uniform motion. In the framework it also drops out of the gravitational field equation (Steps 1 and 5).

Sources: Michelson and Morley (1887); Kostelecký and Russell, Rev. Mod. Phys. 83, 11 (2011) for Lorentz-violation limits; Abbott and others, ApJL 848, L13 (2017).

Where it stands. Consistent: the medium has no detectable rest frame, and the framework inherits relativity's rules. The factor of two in light bending is inherited, not independently derived.

Part IV. Why it is simpler, what would prove it wrong, and where it stands

Why it is simpler than string theory

For a 10-year-old

String theory is like trying to explain a song by saying it is played on an instrument with ten strings, six of which are curled up so tiny that nobody can ever see them, and there are more ways to tune those hidden strings than there are atoms in the universe. It is beautiful music, but hard to check. This framework says: start with a full glass, a rule about relationships, and the fact that you look from one place. No hidden directions. No other universes. One shape, seen from one side.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Simplicity here means fewer assumptions about what exists, not easier mathematics. String theory posits tiny strings, six or seven extra dimensions, supersymmetry and a vast landscape of possible vacua. The framework posits one filled medium with two rules for its relationships and one rule for perspective, and it rejects hidden universes and extra dimensions outright: nothing exists that you could never, even in principle, send a message to. Both programmes use the same exceptional mathematics (octonions, exceptional groups); string theory's work on it helped the authors understand their own structure. The honest difference is that the framework makes definite predictions now, and the count of measured numbers it needs is stated openly.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

The framework's inputs: about 28 measured numbers, against about 34 for the Standard Model with gravity and cosmology counted the same way (the 28 assumes every pending measurement agrees; counting only savings already confirmed it is 32, plus six stated choices; see Inputs and predictions). The gap is six numbers: three are firm (the tau, the down quark and the lightest neutrino) and three wait on measurements. Beyond the count, its gain is in explaining structure (which forces, which charges, handedness) and in ten dated predictions, most of them testable by about 2030.

For a physicist

The framework works in four spacetime dimensions; its internal space is the 27-dimensional exceptional Jordan algebra, a space of relationships, not of positions. It does not require supersymmetry, compactification or moduli stabilisation, and it does not have a landscape: the octonions are unique by Hurwitz's theorem and the algebra by the Jordan–von Neumann–Wigner classification.

For a mathematician

Uniqueness chain: normed division algebras (4, Hurwitz) → largest is 𝕆 → exceptional simple formally real Jordan algebras (exactly one, J₃(𝕆)) → its automorphism group F₄ → its stabiliser of a complex structure and an idempotent is the Standard Model group. Each step has no free choice.

What would prove it wrong

For a 10-year-old

A good theory sticks its neck out. This framework makes ten promises that experiments will check over the next ten years or so. If any one of them comes out wrong, the framework has a real problem, and it says so in advance.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

The framework's standard: if it can be shown wrong within the system, it fails. Its ten predictions are dated and specific, listed below. None was adjusted after the measurement.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer
  1. The quark triangle angle γ: 64.1° to 67.6° (LHCb and Belle II, about 1° by about 2030).
  2. The first-row quark-mixing shortfall must shrink to nothing.
  3. The neutrino timing angle δ: 253° to 292° (DUNE, Hyper-Kamiokande).
  4. Neutrinoless double-beta mass 1.3 to 4.1 meV (beyond current experiments).
  5. No primordial gravitational waves: r < 10⁻¹¹ (LiteBIRD, CMB-S4); from the majoron dark-matter lead, not the core.
  6. Tau mass 1776.969 MeV (Belle II).
  7. Neutrino mass sum 0.0591–0.0594 eV with normal ordering (DESI, Euclid, CMB-S4).
  8. Quarks in compact stars (modelled as self-bound strange stars, which pass the Bodmer–Witten ground-state check) pair with a gap of about 82 to 97 MeV at a strange-quark mass of 100 MeV (88 to 109 MeV for a running strange mass), which keeps the heaviest stars inside the observed window of 2.0 to 2.15 solar masses (NICER, gravitational waves).
  9. If dark matter is the cold majoron wave, there is no X-ray line (a lead, not part of the core).
  10. The strong coupling at the Z mass is 0.1178; lattice calculations at about 0.3% decide. An observed proton decay would also count against it.

Where the framework stands

For a 10-year-old

Out of nine big checks the authors set, seven pass and two pass partly. The framework explains which forces exist, the charges of the particles, why the weak force is one-handed, the masses of the electron family, the lightest quarks, how light neutrinos are, and how heavy a neutron star can get. The strengths of the forces now wait on one measurement, the heavy quark masses still wait for a rule, and it has two good leads, for dark matter and for why there is more matter than antimatter.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

The framework is finished in one specific sense: every question it raises has been worked to an end, either derived, honestly taken from measurement, or narrowed to one named missing rule. Three of those missing rules concern the same thing, the far side of the relationship matrix. It is not finished in the sense of having no inputs; no theory yet is.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Derived: the 8 | 3 | 1 forces and three colours; one generation's charges; one-handedness; ρ = 1; the neutron-star cap; the down and up quark relations; the tau mass; the neutrino sum and mixing pattern. Awaiting measurement: the force strengths (they meet if the strong coupling is 0.1178). Measured, with a lead: the ten quark numbers. Leads: matter excess, majoron dark matter. Measured: three families (with a hint), the twist, gravity's strength, dark energy.

In one paragraph

Space is a full medium with no gaps, and what it holds is kept. Those two pictures force the relationships of the medium to be octonions. Relations among three parts form a 3×3 octonion table. Seen from one spot, facing one way, that table shows exactly the forces of nature and the charges of one family of particles, and the side we face is why the weak force is one-handed. Mass is the energy held where push meets pull, the edge of a bubble. Gravity is what entanglement adds up to. Heavy partners on the far side make neutrinos light and may leave the dark matter behind. Every rule of quantum mechanics and relativity is kept. What remains is stated openly: some numbers are measured rather than derived, three rules about the far side are still missing, and ten predictions will test the whole framework within about a decade.
