About

The authors

Dynamic Relational Transitioning Geometry was created by Matthew Gelfman and Melissa Behrends-Gelfman. It grew from thought experiments and meditation, starting from one question: what if the great physicists were each right, but looking at the same thing from different sides?

Matthew began working on the theory in 1998. Melissa joined him in 2010, and since then it has been their joint work, with equal credit. Researcher identifiers (ORCID): Matthew Gelfman, 0009-0000-6402-2258; Melissa Behrends-Gelfman, 0009-0001-6281-1947.

How this work was made

Health limitations make most typing and working through math by hand very hard, so AI was used to help. It was used for three things only: to write down and keep their ideas, to do and check the math that is now too hard to do by hand, and to search what other scientists have already found. The ideas are theirs.

What the name means

Dynamic: the geometry keeps changing. Relational: particles exist individually, but what matters is how they relate; each particle sets its own view, and the proton's view is the one we live in. Transitioning: local curvature keeps moving between peaks, saddles and troughs while averaging to flat. Geometry: it is all one shape, seen from different perspectives.

Approach

The work proceeds in three steps: state the physical picture plainly; express it in mathematics; and compare every resulting number with measurement using code that anyone can run. Each idea is labelled by its standing: reproduced, already known, consistent, predicted, open, or rejected. Rejected hypotheses are kept on record, because knowing where not to look is part of the evidence.

The framework builds on the work of the physicists named on the Credits page. Where a result was first obtained by others, it is attributed to them on the Credits page and beside each equation on the Every equation, start to finish page.
