The problems that blocked a theory of everything

For a century physics has had two excellent theories that do not fit together, and a list of facts that neither explains. This page takes each problem in turn: what the older view says, how this framework answers it, and how far that answer has actually got.

Each problem opens with a picture for a 10-year-old. The other ways of explaining it are folded below: for a philosopher (pictures and ideas, no mathematics), for a chemist, biologist or engineer, for a physicist, for a mathematician, and for specialists, with sources. Open whichever suits you. The background for every problem, from Newton to the Standard Model, is on the page All of physics, from the beginning.

Gravity and quantum mechanics do not fit together

For a 10-year-old

Close your eyes and picture two rulebooks. One explains tiny things: atoms, light, bubbles of energy that hop and blur. The other explains big things: planets, stars, the bending of space that makes apples fall. Both rulebooks work perfectly in their own world. But when scientists try to use them together, for example inside a black hole, the answers come out as nonsense, like a calculator showing "error".

The framework says the two books were never about separate things. Space is like water filling a sealed bag, with no gaps. Every tiny bubble in it is connected to the others through that one water. Gravity is not an extra rope pulling things together. It is what those connections add up to when there are a great many of them. So you do not need to glue the two books together: the big book is what the small book looks like when you step back.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Physics has two foundations that disagree about what space is. In general relativity, space and time form a smooth, flexible fabric whose shape is gravity. In quantum theory, everything comes in discrete, uncertain packets, but space itself is a fixed stage. Treating space as one more quantum thing gives infinite answers that cannot be repaired. For ninety years this has been the central puzzle.

The framework takes a different starting point: the relationships come first, and space is what a vast web of relationships looks like from inside. If so, gravity is not a separate thing to be quantised. It is a large-scale effect of how strongly the parts of the world are entangled, in the way that temperature is a large-scale effect of moving molecules. Asking for "the quantum of gravity" would then be like asking for "the molecule of temperature".

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

A useful comparison is thermodynamics. Pressure and temperature are real and obey exact laws, but they are not fundamental forces: they are averages over many molecules. In 1995 Ted Jacobson showed that Einstein's equation of gravity follows in the same way from one assumption: that the entanglement across any small patch of space is proportional to its area. In this view gravity is an equation of state of space. The framework adopts this as its account of gravity: entangled particles are linked through one shared medium, and gravity is the large-scale result.

For a physicist

General relativity treated as a quantum field theory is not renormalisable: new infinities appear at every order of perturbation theory ('t Hooft and Veltman, 1974; Goroff and Sagnotti, 1986). The framework follows the emergent route. Jacobson (1995) derived the Einstein equation from the Clausius relation δQ = T dS applied to local Rindler horizons, with entanglement entropy proportional to area (Bekenstein, 1973; Bombelli, Koul, Lee and Sorkin, 1986; Srednicki, 1993). Related work: induced gravity (Sakharov, 1967), the Ryu–Takayanagi formula (2006), "spacetime from entanglement" (Van Raamsdonk, 2010) and ER = EPR (Maldacena and Susskind, 2013). In this route the metric is not a fundamental field, so its non-renormalisability is no more a problem than the non-renormalisability of the Navier–Stokes equations.

For a mathematician

The logical structure is: (1) assume an area law S = A/4ℓ² for the entanglement across every local horizon, with ℓ a fixed length; (2) require the first law of thermodynamics for the energy flux through every such horizon; (3) the Raychaudhuri equation then forces Rab − ½Rgab + Λgab = 8πG Tab, with G = ℓ² (in natural units) and Λ appearing as an integration constant. The equation is a consistency condition, not a postulate. The value of ℓ is not fixed by the argument, which is why gravity's strength stays an input.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Status in the framework: gravity from entanglement is carried as an inherited result. A lattice demonstration of the area law in a model medium is on the Mathematics page. Every attempt to fix Newton's constant from the framework's own numbers was tested and failed by four to nine orders of magnitude, so G is one of the measured inputs. Sources: Jacobson, Phys. Rev. Lett. 75, 1260 (1995); Van Raamsdonk, Gen. Rel. Grav. 42, 2323 (2010); Ryu and Takayanagi, Phys. Rev. Lett. 96, 181602 (2006).

Where it stands. The route (gravity as a consequence of entanglement) rests on published derivations (Jacobson, 1995) and is the framework's main answer. It removes the need to quantise space itself. It does not yet compute gravity's strength, which stays a measured number, and no quantum correction to gravity has been calculated from it.

What happens when we look (measurement, wave and particle)

For a 10-year-old

Imagine a coin spinning on a table. While it spins it is not heads or tails, it is a blur of both. When you slap your hand on it, it becomes one or the other. Tiny particles behave like that spinning coin: until something looks, they are spread out like a wave; when something looks, you find them in one place.

The framework says this is like looking at a sculpture from one side. The sculpture is one whole thing, but you can only ever see one side at a time. When you "look" at a particle, you pick a side to look from, so you see one answer. The whole sculpture was always there.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Quantum theory describes a system by a wave of possibilities. When measured, a single definite result appears, with probabilities the theory predicts exactly. What the theory does not say is what a measurement is, or why one result happens rather than another. This is the measurement problem, and the many interpretations (Copenhagen, many worlds, pilot waves, collapse theories) disagree about it while agreeing on every experiment.

The framework's position is perspectival. There is one geometry of relationships. Any observation is made from one spot, facing one way, and only one view of the geometry can be taken at a time; Heisenberg's uncertainty principle is the statement that complementary views cannot be taken at once. On this reading the wave is the whole, and the measured value is the whole seen from one perspective.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

In a lab, the double-slit experiment shows the puzzle: electrons sent one at a time build up an interference pattern like a wave, yet each arrives as a single dot. If you measure which slit each went through, the pattern disappears. The theory of decoherence (Zeh, 1970; Zurek, 1981) explains why big objects never show such blurs: contact with the surroundings quickly spreads the information. It does not, on its own, say why one particular outcome happens. The framework's answer is about meaning, not about new equations: the outcome is the view taken.

For a physicist

Formally the problem is the conflict between unitary evolution and the projection postulate (von Neumann, 1932). Decoherence produces an effectively diagonal reduced density matrix but not a single outcome. The framework's rule 4 states that every observation fixes one spot, one facing and one imaginary unit: in the octonion description this is the choice of one complex direction among seven, after which the usual complex Hilbert space of quantum mechanics is recovered. The framework uses this choice to obtain the force content (see "Why these forces"). For the outcome itself it adds no dynamics; the Born rule is inherited.

For a mathematician

The octonions contain seven imaginary units. Choosing one unit i reduces the octonions, as a vector space, to a copy of the complex numbers plus a three-dimensional complex space on which the symmetry group SU(3) acts (the subgroup of the octonions' automorphism group G₂ that fixes i). Quantum mechanics over ℂ is what remains visible after the choice. This is the precise sense of "one perspective": a symmetry is broken by the act of fixing a reference, not by a dynamical process.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Sources: von Neumann, Mathematical Foundations of Quantum Mechanics (1932); Zurek, Rev. Mod. Phys. 75, 715 (2003); Günaydin and Gürsey, J. Math. Phys. 14, 1651 (1973) for SU(3) ⊂ G₂. Status: interpretive; agrees with all quantum experiments because it keeps quantum mechanics' measured rules.

Where it stands. The framework keeps every measured rule of quantum mechanics, including the Born rule, as an inherited rule. Its answer to the measurement puzzle is an interpretation (every observation is one perspective on one shared geometry). It makes no prediction that differs from standard quantum mechanics here, so it cannot be tested on this point alone.

Spooky action at a distance (entanglement and Bell's theorem)

For a 10-year-old

Picture two gloves, one left and one right, put into two boxes and mailed to opposite sides of the world. Open one box, see a left glove, and you know instantly the other is right. That part is not spooky. But tiny particles are stranger: experiments show their answers are not decided in advance, like gloves that only become left or right when opened, and still always match.

The framework's picture: the two particles are like two ends of one stretched bubble in the water of space. They are not two things sending messages across a gap. They are one connected thing seen in two places. That is why they match, and also why you cannot use them to send a secret message faster than light.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

In 1935 Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen argued that quantum mechanics must be incomplete, because it allowed one measurement to fix a distant result instantly. In 1964 John Bell showed this was testable: any theory where results are fixed locally and in advance obeys a limit, and quantum mechanics breaks that limit. Experiments from the 1970s onward (Clauser, Aspect, Zeilinger, honoured with the 2022 Nobel Prize) show nature breaks the limit too.

So something about the world is not "separate pieces with local properties". The framework takes this at face value: relationship is more basic than location. Two entangled particles are one relation, and distance is a property of the space that relations build, not of the relation itself.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Bell experiments measure the correlation between results at two distant detectors for different detector settings. A local hidden-variable model gives at most 2 on the CHSH combination; quantum mechanics predicts up to 2√2 ≈ 2.83, and experiments find values near 2.8 with all major loopholes closed (Hensen and others, 2015; Giustina and others, 2015; Shalm and others, 2015). The correlations are real but cannot carry a message: each side's own results look random until the two lists are compared.

For a physicist

The framework is non-local in exactly the sense quantum mechanics is: it reproduces the quantum correlations (Tsirelson's bound 2√2) and respects the no-signalling theorem, because it keeps the quantum formalism. It is not a local hidden-variable theory, so Bell's theorem does not exclude it. Its physical claim is that entanglement is the primitive (Schrödinger, 1935: entanglement is "the characteristic trait" of quantum mechanics), and that geometry and gravity are built from it. This aligns with ER = EPR (Maldacena and Susskind, 2013), where entangled pairs are connected through the geometry itself.

For a mathematician

Bell's inequality is a statement about joint probability distributions: if all four outcomes A, A′, B, B′ have a joint distribution with values ±1, then |E(AB) + E(AB′) + E(A′B) − E(A′B′)| ≤ 2. Quantum states on a tensor product need not admit such a joint distribution, and the bound becomes 2√2 (Tsirelson, 1980). The framework does not add a joint distribution; it adds a reading of the tensor-product structure as one relation rather than two objects.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Sources: Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen, Phys. Rev. 47, 777 (1935); Bell, Physics 1, 195 (1964); Aspect, Dalibard and Roger, Phys. Rev. Lett. 49, 1804 (1982); Hensen and others, Nature 526, 682 (2015). Status: consistent; interpretive.

Where it stands. The framework keeps the quantum predictions that violate Bell's inequality, so it agrees with every Bell test, and it does not allow signals faster than light. Its picture (entangled particles are linked through one medium, so no distance needs to be crossed) is an explanation of why the correlations exist, not a new prediction.

Why these forces and not others

For a 10-year-old

There are four ways things push and pull in nature: gravity, electric and magnetic pushes, a strong grip that holds the centre of atoms together, and a weak nudge that lets some atoms change. The usual theory lists the last three like ingredients on a recipe card, without saying why the card says those and not others.

The framework says: start with the simplest rule for how things can relate, look at it from where you stand, and count what you see. You get exactly 8 kinds of strong-force carriers, 3 kinds of weak-force carriers, and 1 more. That is the real recipe card, and nobody had to write it by hand.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

The Standard Model of particle physics is built on symmetry. Each force corresponds to a group of transformations that leave the laws unchanged: SU(3) for the strong force, SU(2) for the weak force, U(1) for hypercharge. It works superbly, but the choice of these three groups is put in by hand from experiment. A deeper theory should say why.

The framework's answer has three steps. First, relationships must never combine into nothing; a theorem of 1898 (Hurwitz) shows that only four number systems can do this, the largest being the octonions. Second, relations among three parts need a 3×3 table of octonions. Third, any observer sees this table from one perspective. What survives that act of looking is exactly the Standard Model's set of forces. The forces are what the one structure looks like from inside.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Think of a crystal. Its outward shape follows from how its atoms can bond: salt makes cubes because of the geometry of sodium and chlorine, not because someone chose cubes. The framework treats the forces the same way. The "atoms" are the allowed relationships, the "bonding rule" is that relationships never cancel to nothing, and the "crystal shape" is the set of force carriers. Counting the symmetries left when one direction is fixed gives 8 + 3 + 1 carriers, the same numbers measured at particle colliders.

For a physicist

The 27-dimensional exceptional Jordan algebra J₃(𝕆) of 3×3 Hermitian octonion matrices (Jordan, von Neumann and Wigner, 1934) has automorphism group F₄. Fixing one imaginary unit (rule 4) and one diagonal direction leaves (SU(3)c × SU(2)L × U(1)Y)/ℤ₆, the exact global form of the Standard Model gauge group (Dubois-Violette, 2016; Todorov and Dubois-Violette, 2018; see also Furey, 2018; Boyle, 2020). The framework reads the three-way product of the algebra's link states as one full generation with all measured hypercharges and no mirror states. Forces are local symmetries, which is a theorem of relativity plus quantum mechanics (Weinberg, 1964), inherited rather than derived.

For a mathematician

Hurwitz's theorem (1898): the only normed division algebras over ℝ are ℝ, ℂ, ℍ, 𝕆, of dimensions 1, 2, 4, 8. Hermitian 3×3 matrices over 𝕆 form a Jordan algebra under X∘Y = ½(XY + YX), the only exceptional (non-special) simple Jordan algebra. Its automorphism group is F₄ (dimension 52). The subgroup preserving a complex structure on 𝕆 and a rank-one idempotent is the Standard Model group; its Lie algebra has dimension 8 + 3 + 1 = 12. The "three colours" are the three complex dimensions left in 𝕆 = ℂ ⊕ ℂ³ after i is fixed.

The strengths of the forces are not derived (see below).

Why charges come in exact steps

For a 10-year-old

The electron and the proton are completely different: the proton is almost 2,000 times heavier and is made of three smaller pieces. Yet their electric charges are exactly equal and opposite, so a hydrogen atom is perfectly neutral. That is like a pebble and a boulder that weigh different amounts but have exactly the same colour, to more decimal places than anyone can count. Why?

The framework's answer: all charges come from counting the same few directions in one shape. Like counting steps on one staircase, you can only land on whole steps, or on the thirds that quarks use. There is no way to land halfway.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Electric charge comes in exact multiples: the electron has −1, quarks have −⅓ and +⅔, and nothing has 0.4 or π. In the Standard Model these values are chosen to fit experiment. Dirac (1931) noticed that a single magnetic monopole anywhere in the universe would force all charges into steps, and grand unified theories force it by placing quarks and leptons in one family. The framework gets the steps from structure: charges are counts of directions in the one relationship matrix, so they must be fractions with small denominators.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Chemistry depends entirely on this. If the proton's charge differed from the electron's by even one part in a billion billion, ordinary matter would carry a net charge and the electric repulsion would overwhelm gravity on every scale: stars and planets could not form. Measurements show the two are equal to about one part in 10²¹ (Bressi and others, 2011).

For a physicist

In the three-way reading, one generation is the 16-state part of the 27 of E₆ under the Standard Model subgroup of F₄, with hypercharges Q = T₃ + Y read from the U(1) generator that commutes with the fixed complex structure. The resulting charges are those of the Standard Model, including the right-handed neutrino with Q = 0, and the anomaly cancellation conditions are satisfied automatically, as for any complete E₆ representation.

For a mathematician

The hypercharge generator is an element of the Cartan subalgebra of F₄, so its eigenvalues on any finite-dimensional representation are rational with a bounded denominator (here 6, reflecting the ℤ₆ quotient). Integrality of the electric charge of colour-neutral states follows from the ℤ₆ identification.

Sources: Dirac, Proc. R. Soc. A 133, 60 (1931); Georgi and Glashow, Phys. Rev. Lett. 32, 438 (1974); Bressi and others, Phys. Rev. A 83, 052101 (2011). Status: derived on the main line.

Where it stands. Derived on the main line: the three-way reading of the relationship matrix gives every measured charge of a generation, and makes the proton's charge exactly the opposite of the electron's, as measured to one part in 10²¹.

Why nature is left-handed

For a 10-year-old

Hold up your two hands. They look the same, but they are mirror images: no matter how you turn your left hand, it never becomes a right hand. Most of nature does not care about left and right. But in 1957 scientists discovered that the weak force does: it only touches particles that spin one way, as if it could only shake left hands.

The framework says this is not a strange choice nature made. It comes from the fact that every view is taken from one spot, facing one way. Facing is a direction, and a direction already has a "left" and a "right". Choose the view, and you have chosen the hand.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Until 1956 physicists assumed the laws of nature look the same in a mirror. Lee and Yang suggested testing it, and Wu's experiment in 1957 showed the weak force breaks mirror symmetry completely. The Standard Model builds this in by giving left- and right-handed particles different charges, without explaining why. The framework connects it to the perspective: observing means fixing a direction, and fixing a direction in the octonions picks an orientation. Handedness is the side from which the world is observed.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Mirror asymmetry appears across science, for example in the single handedness of the amino acids in living things. In particle physics the asymmetry is total: the weak force acts on left-handed particles and right-handed antiparticles only. Neutrinos have only ever been seen left-handed.

For a physicist

Fixing the imaginary unit i in 𝕆 defines a complex structure; the SU(2) that survives acts on one chirality only, because the 16 of the three-way reading decomposes with SU(2) doublets only on the side defined by the chosen i. Reversing the facing (i → −i) exchanges particles with antiparticles. The right-handed neutrino appears as an SU(2) singlet with no Standard Model charges, which is the state that gives neutrinos their mass through the seesaw.

For a mathematician

Complex conjugation on 𝕆 = ℂ ⊕ ℂ³ exchanges the 3 and the 3̄ of SU(3). A choice of i is a choice of orientation of the complex line ℂ ⊂ 𝕆; the two choices are related by an outer automorphism, so neither is preferred until one is made. The weak SU(2) acts on the representation built over one of them.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Sources: Lee and Yang, Phys. Rev. 104, 254 (1956); Wu and others, Phys. Rev. 105, 1413 (1957). Status: derived on the main line with one stated reading (the perspective).

Where it stands. Derived on the main line: one-handedness of the weak force follows from rule 4 (one spot, one facing, one imaginary unit). Mirror-image weak partners are absent from the matrix.

Why there are three copies of matter

For a 10-year-old

Everything around you is made of four tiny bubbles of matter: two kinds of quark, the electron, and a ghostly neutrino. But nature makes two more heavier copies of this whole set, which appear in collisions and quickly fall apart. When the first heavy copy was found, a famous physicist asked, "Who ordered that?"

The framework sees the three copies as the same shape placed three ways, like one pattern printed in three sizes. Its table of relationships can only be three by three, so there is room for exactly three. We still count it as something we measure, because the rule that forbids a fourth is not yet proved.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Why does nature repeat itself, and why exactly three times? The Standard Model has no answer: three is simply observed. The framework's structure only allows a 3×3 table of octonions (a 4×4 one cannot be built with the octonions' rules), so "three" appears naturally in its mathematics. Whether those three places are the three generations is plausible but not yet proved, so the framework labels three generations as measured, with a hint.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

The three generations differ only in mass: the muon is like a heavy electron, the tau heavier still. Experiments at the LEP collider measured how quickly the Z boson decays and found room for exactly 2.984 ± 0.008 light neutrinos, which fixes three generations of the light kind.

For a physicist

J₃(𝕆) has three diagonal entries and is the largest exceptional Jordan algebra; Jn(𝕆) is not a Jordan algebra for n ≥ 4. In the framework the three generations are three placements of the same shape (for the leptons, the self-link equals the cross-links, which reproduces Koide's relation). Exactly three is supported by the algebra and by LEP, but the step from three diagonal entries to three generations needs a rule not yet derived.

For a mathematician

For n ≥ 4 the Jordan identity fails for Hermitian octonion matrices because 𝕆 is not associative; for n = 3 the failure terms cancel by alternativity (Jordan, von Neumann and Wigner, 1934). This is the precise sense in which three is the maximum.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Sources: ALEPH, DELPHI, L3, OPAL and SLD, Phys. Rep. 427, 257 (2006); Jordan, von Neumann and Wigner (1934); Dubois-Violette (2016). Status: measured with hint, by the authors' decision.

Where it stands. Three generations is taken as measured (the Z boson's width counts exactly three light neutrinos). The framework offers a strong hint: the 3×3 table is the largest exceptional one possible, and the octonions have exactly three sizes in that table. It is not counted as derived.

Why the masses are so different

For a 10-year-old

The heaviest particle, the top quark, weighs as much as a gold atom. The lightest charged one, the electron, is about 340,000 times lighter. And neutrinos are lighter still, by another million. It is as if a family had a child the size of an ant and another the size of a whale.

The framework says mass is a balance point, like the edge of a soap bubble where the push out equals the pull in. The electron family's three masses come out of one balance, perfectly. For the quarks, two of them come out too, but the heavy ones still have to be weighed.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

In the Standard Model every mass is a separate number fitted to measurement. Nothing explains why they are spread over such a huge range. The framework treats mass as a balance between containment and leakage, the point where an energy is held in place. That idea explains why most of the proton's mass is binding energy and why nothing is destroyed in fission. For the electron, muon and tau it gives a single rule that fixes all three masses from one scale. For the heavy quarks it has a working picture but not yet a rule, so it says so openly.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

A chemist already knows that mass is mostly binding: about 99% of the mass of a proton comes from the energy of the strong force holding its quarks, not from the quarks themselves. The framework extends this: every mass is the balance energy of something contained. The lepton rule is a geometric one: the square roots of the three masses lie on a cone at exactly 45° (Koide, 1983), which the framework reads as maximum uncertainty between a particle's self-relation and its relations to the others.

For a physicist

Lepton sector: with the 45° condition (Koide's Q = ⅔) and a phase of 2/9 (Brannen), mτ = 1776.969 MeV follows from me and mμ (measured 1776.93 ± 0.09; Belle II will test it). The phase itself is a measured input (2/9 × (1 − 7.88 × 10⁻⁷)); every route to deriving it failed. Quarks: √(md/ms) gives the Cabibbo angle (Gatto, Sartori and Tonin, 1968) and the down mass; the up mass follows through η → 3π without lattice input. For the heavy quarks, a heavy far-side state (the D of the E₆ 27) can mix only with the down quark's single right-handed partner (a Nelson–Barr structure), which fits all ten quark numbers at 1.6σ with nine free numbers and predicts the unitarity-triangle angle γ = 64.1–67.6°.

For a mathematician

Write √mk = M(1 + √2 cos(θ + 2πk/3)). The 45° condition is that the vector (√me, √mμ, √mτ) makes a 45° angle with (1,1,1); this is equivalent to Q = (Σm)/(Σ√m)² = ⅔. For the down quarks, the press by the far side takes the form Hd = m(1 − κuu†)m with u a unit vector, a rank-one deformation that steepens the down ladder.

The top, bottom, charm and strange masses and the quark mixing remain measured inputs, with a recorded lead (one combined rule fits all ten quark numbers with nine free numbers).

Why the forces have the strengths they do

For a 10-year-old

The strong force is very strong, the electric force is medium, the weak force is weak, and gravity is unbelievably weak: a small magnet can lift a paper clip against the pull of the whole Earth. Scientists hoped that if you zoomed in far enough, all the forces would turn out to be equally strong, like three roads meeting at one crossroads. In the standard theory, they nearly meet but miss.

The framework finds hidden partners on the far side of its pattern that bend the roads so they meet exactly, but only if those partners have a particular weight. That weight says exactly how strong the strong force must be, and that number is being measured now. Until the measurement comes back, this is not counted as proved.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

A deep theory should explain not only which forces exist but how strong they are. The strengths change with the energy at which they are measured, and in the 1970s it was hoped they would all become equal at very high energy, signalling one original force (grand unification). With only the known particles, they come close but do not meet. The framework's structure contains partners that would make them meet, and it gives their weight: the meeting energy squared, divided by the grain of space. With that weight the strong force must have one particular strength. That is a prediction now being tested, and the framework does not count it as established until the measurement returns.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

The strength of a force is measured by a number like the fine-structure constant, about 1/137 for electricity at everyday energies. These numbers drift with energy because the vacuum around a charge screens or anti-screens it. Extrapolating the measured drifts upward, the three lines miss each other by about 9% in the electroweak mixing angle.

For a physicist

Two-loop Standard Model running from the Z pole gives sin²θW predictions that miss the measured 0.23122 by −8.9% ± 0.1% for a unified start (Georgi, Quinn and Weinberg, 1974). Adding the framework's three colourless far-side spin-½ weak pairs at 2.4 × 10⁸ GeV, with the coloured far-side states from the same 27 at the meeting energy, 4.6 × 10¹³ GeV (a ratio of about 2.0 × 10⁵), closes the triangle. The pairs' weight is the meeting energy squared divided by the grain of space. At three loops the meeting requires αs(MZ) = 0.1178, with the grain of space fixed in advance as the Planck energy divided by √2 (the unreduced Planck energy would have given 0.1196, which is not the framework's value); the most precise single determination is 0.11873 ± 0.00056 (Dalla Brida and others, 2025), which does not yet decide. Other fixed rules tried for the masses failed (√(meMPl) at 4.3σ, the hidden-partner ladder at 2.3σ). Gravity ties fail by 4 to 9 decades.

For a mathematician

At one loop, αi−1(μ) = αi−1(MZ) − (bi/2π) ln(μ/MZ). Three lines meet at one point only if (b₃ − b₂)/(b₂ − b₁) equals a ratio fixed by the measured values; added particles change the bi above their mass thresholds, so the condition becomes one equation on the logarithms of the thresholds, which here fixes the ratio of two masses but not either mass.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Sources: Georgi, Quinn and Weinberg, Phys. Rev. Lett. 33, 451 (1974); Gross and Wilczek (1973); Politzer (1973); Machacek and Vaughn (1983–85) for two-loop coefficients; PDG 2024. Status: awaiting measurement of the strong coupling.

Where it stands. Awaiting measurement. The three forces of the Standard Model alone do not meet at one strength (they miss by 8.9% at two loops). In the framework, three colourless weak pairs on the far side of the same matrix, at 2.4 × 10⁸ GeV, make them meet at 4.6 × 10¹³ GeV. That requires a strong coupling of 0.1178; the most precise measurement so far, 0.11873 ± 0.00056, lies 1.7 standard deviations above it and does not yet decide. This is not counted as established until that measurement returns. The meeting energy, the grain of space and gravity's strength are not derived, so the strengths stay measured inputs until then.

Why empty space does not weigh what it should

For a 10-year-old

Scientists calculated how much energy "empty" space should hold, from all the tiny jiggles that never stop. The answer was so huge that the universe should have blown apart instantly. The real amount is smaller by a number with about 120 zeros. It is the worst prediction in the history of science.

The framework says: a glass full to the brim does not feel heavier to the water inside it. If space is a full glass, the water of space does not push on itself. Only the small differences, the ripples, have weight. So the huge number was never supposed to count.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Quantum theory says the vacuum is not empty: it seethes with fleeting fields. Gravity responds to all energy, so the vacuum's energy should curve space. Estimates exceed the observed value by around 10120. Physicists have proposed cancellations, anthropic selection among many universes, and new symmetries, none agreed.

The framework's answer is conceptual and old in spirit: the medium is completely full, and a uniform fullness is the zero point. What gravitates is departure from it. Gravity, built from entanglement, has no way to "see" a uniform background energy. The leftover small dark energy is measured; its size sits near the balance of space's two ends, the grain and the horizon.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

An analogy from condensed matter is exact here. In superfluid helium the energy of the liquid at rest is zero by its own equilibrium, however large the energy of its atoms might seem if added up one by one; only disturbances carry energy. Volovik (2003) argued that the quantum vacuum behaves the same way, which he called the universe in a helium droplet.

For a physicist

The cosmological constant problem (Weinberg, 1989). In Jacobson's derivation, and in unimodular gravity (Einstein, 1919; van der Bij, van Dam and Ng, 1982), Λ appears as an integration constant and the vacuum energy density drops out of the field equation. The equilibrium argument of Klinkhamer and Volovik (2008) gives the same result. In those derivations Λ is a constant, measured as ΩΛ ≈ 0.69. The framework's main line ties its size to the grain and the horizon together (Cohen, Kaplan and Nelson, 1999): √(grain × horizon) = 3.5 meV against 2.24 meV measured, a size agreement that follows from the Friedmann equation and is not a derivation. On this reading it need not stay constant; no law for its change is derived (the formula of Li, 2004, was tested and goes the wrong way against the DESI hint). A strictly constant value is kept on record as the alternative.

For a mathematician

In unimodular gravity the metric determinant is fixed, so the field equations are only the trace-free part of Einstein's equation. The trace part is recovered from the Bianchi identity up to an arbitrary constant, which plays the role of Λ. A constant shift Tab → Tab + ρvacgab has no trace-free part, so it does not appear.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Sources: Weinberg, Rev. Mod. Phys. 61, 1 (1989); Volovik, The Universe in a Helium Droplet (2003); Klinkhamer and Volovik, Phys. Rev. D 77, 085015 (2008); DESI Collaboration (2025) for the hints of evolving dark energy. Status: explains the absence of the huge term; value measured; how it changes with time, if it does, is not derived.

Where it stands. The framework answers why the enormous energy of the quantum vacuum does not curve space: in a full medium, and when gravity comes from entanglement, that energy does not act as a source (it enters only as a fixed constant). The small observed dark energy is one measured number. On the main line its size is set by the grain and the horizon of space together, so it need not be constant; the framework has no derived law for how it changes, and a fixed amount is kept on record as the alternative.

What dark matter is

For a 10-year-old

Galaxies spin so fast that they should fly apart, like mud flung off a spinning wheel. Something invisible is holding them together, about five times more than all the stars and gas. Nobody has ever caught a piece of it.

The framework's candidate is a very quiet wave, left behind when the ghostly neutrino got its weight. So many of these waves overlap that they act like one smooth mist. You cannot see the mist, but you can feel its pull. Like camouflage, it hides the particles, not their weight.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

The evidence for dark matter is overwhelming but indirect: galaxy rotation (Rubin and Ford, 1970), galaxy clusters (Zwicky, 1933), the bending of light, and the pattern of the oldest light. What it is remains unknown. Some propose new particles; some propose that gravity changes at large distances.

The framework holds both thoughts honestly. Part of the effect, the outskirts of single galaxies, matches a picture in which space has a small baseline resistance; this is the perspective part. But colliding clusters show mass where there is no ordinary matter, which a change of perspective cannot fake. For that, the framework already contains a real thing: a wave left over from how the neutrino gets its mass.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Bosons, particles that can share a state, merge into a single wave when many sit within one wavelength. For a light enough particle this makes dark matter behave like a smooth fluid on small scales (Hu, Barkana and Gruzinov, 2000; Hui, Ostriker, Tremaine and Witten, 2017). The majoron, proposed in 1981 (Chikashige, Mohapatra and Peccei), is such a particle. If formed slowly as a cold wave, it behaves as standard cold dark matter on galactic scales.

For a physicist

The majoron is the pseudo-Goldstone boson of spontaneously broken lepton number. Lifetime to two neutrinos 1025 to 1048 s, far above the age of the universe. With a cold (misalignment-type) start (Preskill, Wise and Wilczek, 1983; Abbott and Sikivie, 1983; Dine and Fischler, 1983) it can be all of the dark matter; a thermal start makes it 32 times too light. No X-ray line is predicted. Inflation in the cold start must be low-scale, so the tensor-to-scalar ratio r < 10⁻¹¹. Milgrom's acceleration a₀ ≈ cH₀/2π (1.04 vs 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ m/s²) holds for galaxies only; the Bullet Cluster (Clowe and others, 2006) excludes it as the whole story.

For a mathematician

For a scalar field with mass m starting at amplitude fθ in a cold universe, the field is frozen until H ≈ m, then oscillates with energy density falling as a⁻³ like matter. The present abundance scales as Ω ∝ m1/2 f² θ², so fixing Ω (measured) relates m, f and θ: this is the relation quoted above in another form.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Sources: Chikashige, Mohapatra and Peccei, Phys. Lett. B 98, 265 (1981); Berezinsky and Valle, Phys. Lett. B 318, 360 (1993); Garcia-Cely and Heeck, JHEP 05 (2017) 102; Hui and others, Phys. Rev. D 95, 043541 (2017); Milgrom, ApJ 270, 365 (1983); Clowe and others, ApJ 648, L109 (2006). Status: lead.

Where it stands. Lead. The main line's candidate is the majoron, a spin-0 chargeless wave left when the hidden neutrino partner's own mass settles. As a cold wave it passes every current limit. Its mass is tied to the settling scale by mJ = 8.8 keV × (6.3 × 10¹⁰ GeV ÷ f)⁴ ÷ angle⁴ and is not fixed by the framework; the amount is a start condition (measured). It predicts no X-ray line and no detectable primordial gravitational waves. The framework's resistance reading reproduces the acceleration scale in galaxies but fails for galaxy clusters and the cosmic microwave background, so dark matter is read as partly perspective and partly a real wave.

Why there is matter and almost no antimatter

For a 10-year-old

Every bit of matter has an opposite twin, antimatter. When they meet, both vanish in a flash of light. At the beginning there should have been equal amounts, and everything should have flashed away, leaving only light. But here we are. For every billion pairs that vanished, one extra bit of matter was left over, and that leftover made every star and every person.

The framework says the heavy hidden partners of the neutrinos, when they fell apart early on, tipped the balance very slightly toward matter. It works in a universe that began cool rather than hot, and we have to measure one number about that beginning.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Sakharov (1967) listed what any explanation needs: a process that changes the matter count, a difference between matter and antimatter, and a moment out of balance. The Standard Model has all three in principle, but far too weakly. The framework uses a known mechanism (Fukugita and Yanagida, 1986) whose ingredients its own structure contains. It shows the observed amount is reachable, but only if the early universe started cold, which it treats as a measured fact about the start rather than something it derives.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

The excess is measured precisely: about 6.1 × 10⁻¹⁰ protons for every photon of the cosmic background light (Planck, 2018; and the abundances of light elements). The heavy partner neutrinos decay slightly more often to matter than to antimatter, and the Standard Model's own high-temperature processes (sphalerons) convert part of that into extra protons.

For a physicist

Leptogenesis with heavy right-handed neutrinos at 0.6–1.9 × 10⁵, 2.2–6.2 × 10⁸ and 1.3–1.65 × 10¹² GeV (full three-by-three seesaw with the measured mixing). Thermal leptogenesis from the framework's spectrum gives at most about 2 × 10⁻¹⁰ (2.6 to 3 times short). A non-thermal start in which the initial energy is handed to the partners directly (Lazarides and Shafi, 1991) reaches 6.1 × 10⁻¹⁰ with a handover fraction of 0.0017, and is the same cold start the majoron needs.

For a mathematician

The final asymmetry is ηB ≈ csph Σi εi κi (nNi/nγ), with ε the decay asymmetry, κ the efficiency after washout and csph = 28/79. The start condition fixes the initial nN/nγ; the matrix structure bounds ε.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Sources: Sakharov, JETP Lett. 5, 24 (1967); Fukugita and Yanagida, Phys. Lett. B 174, 45 (1986); Lazarides and Shafi, Phys. Lett. B 258, 305 (1991); Davidson and Ibarra (2002) for the bound on ε. Status: lead.

Where it stands. Lead. The excess can be made by the decays of the hidden neutrino partners (leptogenesis), in a universe that started cold, with one start number (a handover of 0.0017 against a ceiling of 0.05) taken as measured. A hot start falls 2.6 to 3 times short. This is allowed, not predicted.

Why the strong force does not pick a direction (the strong CP problem)

For a 10-year-old

The strong force has a hidden dial. If the dial were turned even slightly, the neutron would be lopsided, a little bit plus on one side and minus on the other. Experiments show the dial is set to zero, to better than one part in ten billion. Nobody knows why it sits so exactly at zero.

The framework's way of pressing on the down quarks happens to leave that dial at zero automatically, because the press only works one way, like a one-way valve.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

A good theory should not need settings tuned to absurd precision without reason. The strong force allows such a setting, and nature sets it to zero far more precisely than chance would. The popular proposed fix adds a new particle (the axion). The framework's structure offers an alternative known since 1984, in which a hidden heavy partner connects to ordinary quarks only in one direction, so the dial stays at zero while the other kind of asymmetry that experiments do see remains.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

The test is the neutron's electric dipole moment: how far its plus and minus charges sit apart. The best measurement finds no separation at a level that sets the strong angle below about 10⁻¹⁰ (Abel and others, 2020).

For a physicist

θ̄ = θ − arg det(MuMd). In Nelson–Barr models CP is a symmetry broken spontaneously, and the heavy-light mass matrix has a zero block, so arg det vanishes at tree level while the CKM phase is generated. In the framework the E₆ D state mixes only with the down quark's singlet partner and the reverse block is empty, giving θ̄ = 0 with J = 3.04 × 10⁻⁵.

For a mathematician

For a block matrix ((m, F), (0, M)) the determinant is det(m)·det(M), which is real if m and M are real, however complex F is. The low-energy mass matrix m m† + F F† can still be complex, which is how the physical quark twist arises.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Sources: Nelson, Phys. Lett. B 136, 387 (1984); Barr, Phys. Rev. Lett. 53, 329 (1984); Bento, Branco and Parada, Phys. Lett. B 267, 95 (1991); Abel and others, Phys. Rev. Lett. 124, 081803 (2020). Status: lead.

Where it stands. Lead. The same far-side press that is the framework's lead for the heavy quarks has the structure of a Nelson–Barr solution: with the return link empty, the strong-force angle is exactly zero while the measured quark twist survives (J = 3.04 × 10⁻⁵). It stands or falls with the quark lead.

Why neutrinos are so light

For a 10-year-old

Neutrinos are so light and shy that trillions of them pass through your body every second without touching anything. For a long time people thought they weighed nothing. They do weigh something, but less than a millionth of an electron.

Picture a seesaw with a heavy elephant on one end and a mouse on the other. The heavier the elephant, the higher the mouse goes up and the lighter its end feels. Every neutrino has a hidden heavy partner on the other end of a seesaw, and that is why the neutrino is so light.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Neutrino masses were the first clear evidence of physics beyond the Standard Model, which had them at zero. The seesaw idea (1977–1980) explains their smallness by a very heavy partner. In the framework this partner is not added; it is already in the one generation that the relationship matrix gives, and it is the only state permitted to have mass by itself.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Neutrinos change type as they travel (oscillation), discovered at Super-Kamiokande (1998) and SNO (2001), which requires mass. Oscillations measure differences of squared masses; cosmology measures the sum, now below about 0.07 eV (DESI, 2025).

For a physicist

Type-I seesaw: mν ≈ mD² / MR (Minkowski, 1977; Gell-Mann, Ramond and Slansky, 1979; Yanagida, 1979; Mohapatra and Senjanović, 1980). With the neutrino sharing the charged leptons' 3×3 link, the partners sit at 0.6–1.9 × 10⁵, 2.2–6.2 × 10⁸ and 1.3–1.65 × 10¹² GeV (full three-by-three seesaw with the measured mixing). Predictions: normal ordering, Σmν = 0.0591–0.0594 eV, TM1 mixing, δ = 253–292°, and an effective double-beta mass of 1.3–4.1 meV.

For a mathematician

The 6×6 mass matrix ((0, mD), (mDT, MR)) has, for MR ≫ mD, light eigenvalues given by −mDMR−1mDT to leading order (the Schur complement).

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Sources: Fukuda and others (Super-Kamiokande), Phys. Rev. Lett. 81, 1562 (1998); Ahmad and others (SNO), Phys. Rev. Lett. 87, 071301 (2001); Minkowski, Phys. Lett. B 67, 421 (1977); DESI Collaboration (2025); NuFIT. Status: holds; predictions open.

Where it stands. Holds. The hidden right-handed partner is the one state allowed a mass without the Higgs; its heaviness pushes the ordinary neutrino's mass down (the seesaw). The framework predicts normal ordering, a mass sum of 0.0591–0.0594 eV and the TM1 mixing pattern, all testable this decade.

Why time has a direction

For a 10-year-old

You have seen an egg break, but never seen a broken egg jump back together. Milk stirs into coffee but never un-stirs. The laws for tiny bubbles work the same forward and backward, so why does the world only go one way?

The answer most scientists give: the universe started very neat and tidy, and it has been getting messier ever since, because there are many more messy ways than neat ones. The framework agrees, and adds that while the whole gets messier, little pockets like stars, planets and living things build beautiful, complicated shapes by riding that flow, like a waterwheel turning in a stream.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

The fundamental laws are almost all symmetric in time, yet experience is not. Boltzmann explained the arrow statistically: disorder (entropy) grows because disordered states vastly outnumber ordered ones. That pushes the question back to why the past was so ordered, sometimes called the past hypothesis (Albert, 2000). The framework does not answer that; it treats the beginning as out of scope. It does make the local picture vivid: energy moves from simple to complex in pockets, paid for by greater disorder elsewhere (Schrödinger, 1944; Prigogine, 1977).

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Living cells, crystals growing from solution and hurricanes are all ordered structures that exist because energy flows through them and they export disorder. Black holes are where disorder grows most: one solar-mass black hole holds about 10⁷⁷ units of entropy, against about 10⁵⁸ for the Sun as a star (Bekenstein, 1973; Hawking, 1974; Egan and Lineweaver, 2010).

For a physicist

The thermodynamic arrow is tied to the low gravitational entropy of the early universe (Penrose, 1979). In the framework, black holes are regions where containment exceeds leakage; their entropy follows Bekenstein–Hawking, consistent with the area law the framework uses for gravity. No new law for the arrow is proposed.

For a mathematician

Boltzmann's entropy S = k ln W counts microstates W compatible with a macrostate. For almost every microstate in a low-entropy macrostate, the dynamics leads to higher-entropy macrostates in both time directions; the observed one-way arrow therefore needs a boundary condition, not a law.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Sources: Boltzmann (1877); Penrose, in General Relativity: An Einstein Centenary Survey (1979); Albert, Time and Chance (2000); Egan and Lineweaver, ApJ 710, 1825 (2010). Status: reading; boundary condition not derived.

Where it stands. A reading. The second law of thermodynamics is kept as measured. The framework's picture of energy spreading from simple to complex locally, while disorder grows overall, agrees with established physics. Why the universe began so ordered is an origin question, which the framework places outside its scope.

The two speeds of expansion (the Hubble tension)

For a 10-year-old

Two teams time how fast the universe is stretching. One team watches nearby exploding stars. The other reads the oldest light in the sky, from when the universe was a baby. Their stopwatches disagree, 73 against 67. Either one stopwatch is off, or the river of space changed speed, or the place where we stand is special.

The framework pictured space as thicker in some places and thinner in others. We do seem to sit in a thinner patch, but it is hard to grow a patch that big. So the puzzle is still open.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

A tension between two precise measurements may be an error, or the first sign of new physics. Both teams have checked their methods extensively. The framework does not claim to resolve it; it records which proposed explanations fit its own picture, and why its first idea (we live in a thin region) is not enough by itself.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

The local distance ladder (Cepheid stars and supernovae) gives 73.0 ± 1.0 km/s per megaparsec (Riess and others, 2022). The cosmic background light gives 67.4 ± 0.5 (Planck, 2018). Independent methods using gravitational-wave sirens will soon be precise enough to decide.

For a physicist

Candidate explanations: a local void (Keenan, Barger and Cowie, 2013; Haslbauer, Banik and Kroupa, 2020), excluded at 6–7σ under standard growth; timescape (Wiltshire, 2007; Seifert and others, 2025), contested; systematics (Freedman and others, 2024–25 find about 70); early dark energy (Poulin and others, 2019), weakened; a 1% larger electron mass at recombination (Hart and Chluba, 2020), the best single-parameter fit in comparisons (Schöneberg and others, 2022). The framework's freeze-in of masses gives a natural home for the last, but no number.

For a mathematician

The early-universe value depends on the sound horizon rs at recombination, which the cosmic background measures as an angle θs = rs/D. Changing the electron mass shifts recombination and rs; at fixed θs, H₀ scales roughly inversely with rs.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Sources: Riess and others, ApJL 934, L7 (2022); Planck Collaboration, A&A 641, A6 (2020); Hart and Chluba, MNRAS 493, 3255 (2020); Schöneberg and others, Phys. Rep. 984, 1 (2022). Status: unsolved.

Where it stands. Unsolved. The thinner region the framework pictured is real (the measured local underdensity matches the size needed), but ordinary growth cannot make it deep and wide enough, so it fails as the cause on the main line. The routes closest to the framework are uneven clock rates in thicker and thinner space (timescape) and a slightly unfinished freezing of the electron's mass at early times. Neither is derived.

Where the universe came from

For a 10-year-old

Close your eyes and picture a very still pond on a day with a light drizzle. The surface is almost perfectly flat. Here and there a drop lands and makes a ring of little waves that spread out. Our whole visible universe is like one of those spreading rings, seen from inside.

What made the first drop? That is the question nobody can answer, because we cannot look outside our own ring. The framework says so honestly and does not guess.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Cosmology traces the universe back 13.8 billion years to a hot, dense state. What, if anything, came before is beyond observation. Many theories propose answers (inflation's eternal multiverse, bounces, cycles), but none can yet be tested. The framework declines to add one; a theory of everything that can be seen need not be a theory of what cannot.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

What is known: the universe is expanding, it was once hot enough to make the light elements (hydrogen, helium, lithium in measured proportions), and its oldest light shows a nearly uniform temperature with tiny variations of one part in 100,000. Space is flat to within about 0.2%.

For a physicist

The pond picture reproduces spatial flatness, multiple causally separate expansions and a gravitational-wave background; it fails for a single centre and a shoaling speed-up. Black holes that exceed containment are read as the framework's analogue of local bangs; this is recorded as an interpretation, not a model. Inflation is not part of the main line, but a low-scale cold start is assumed by the dark matter lead, which predicts r < 10⁻¹¹.

For a mathematician

Spatial flatness corresponds to Ωk = 0.001 ± 0.002 (Planck with baryon acoustic oscillations). In a Friedmann universe this means the total density equals the critical density 3H²/8πG to that precision.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Sources: Planck Collaboration (2020); Fields, Olive, Yeh and Young (2020) for nucleosynthesis. Status: out of scope.

Where it stands. Outside the scope of the framework by the authors' decision: information about the whole of everything, including what came before the expansion, is close to impossible to obtain. The framework describes what can be seen. Its picture for that is a still pond in a light drizzle: a flat surface with tiny variations, where each droplet is like a bang and ours need not be the only one. It uses no hidden universes and no extra dimensions.

What happens to information in a black hole

For a 10-year-old

If you throw a book into a black hole, what happens to the story written inside? Stephen Hawking found that black holes slowly glow and shrink away. If they vanish, is the story lost forever? The laws of quantum physics say information can never be truly destroyed, so this is a real puzzle.

The framework has not worked on this puzzle yet. We say so plainly.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Hawking (1976) argued that black hole evaporation destroys information, contradicting a basic rule of quantum theory. Most physicists now think information escapes, and recent calculations support this, but how it escapes is debated. The framework's picture of black holes as balances of containment and leakage is compatible with information leaking out, but no calculation has been done.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

Hawking radiation is extremely faint for real black holes: a black hole the mass of the Sun has a temperature of 6 × 10⁻⁸ kelvin and would take about 10⁶⁷ years to evaporate.

For a physicist

The Page curve (Page, 1993) and its recent derivation with replica wormholes and islands (Penington, 2019; Almheiri, Engelhardt, Marolf and Maxfield, 2019) indicate unitary evaporation. The framework's entanglement-first picture is naturally aligned with these methods, but it has not reproduced them.

For a mathematician

For a pure state split into black hole and radiation, the entanglement entropy of the radiation must first rise and then fall to zero (the Page curve); Hawking's calculation gives a monotonic rise.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Sources: Hawking, Phys. Rev. D 14, 2460 (1976); Page, Phys. Rev. Lett. 71, 3743 (1993); Almheiri and others, JHEP 12 (2019) 063; Penington, JHEP 09 (2020) 002. Status: not addressed.

Where it stands. Not addressed by the framework. It is listed here so that no reader thinks it is claimed.

How many numbers have to be measured

For a 10-year-old

Every theory needs some numbers that you can only get by measuring, like a recipe that says "add salt to taste". A perfect theory of everything might need none. Today's best theories need about thirty.

The framework still needs about the same number of measured numbers. What it adds is the reason for the shape of the recipe: why there are these ingredients at all.

For a philosopher (pictures, no math)

Counting free parameters is a crude but honest measure of how much a theory explains. A theory that needs fewer inputs to predict the same facts explains more. The framework is honest that its count is not much smaller. Its explanatory gain lies elsewhere: in turning lists into consequences.

For a chemist, biologist or engineer

The Standard Model's inputs: 3 force strengths, 6 quark masses, 3 charged-lepton masses, 4 quark-mixing numbers, the Higgs value and mass, the strong-force angle (19 in all), plus 7 to 9 for neutrino masses and mixing. Standard cosmology adds six (the matter and dark matter densities, the dark energy, and three numbers describing the early ripples).

For a physicist

The framework's list, about 28: the three force strengths; the Higgs value; the lepton scale and twist; five quark masses (the down quark follows from the Cabibbo angle); four quark-mixing numbers and the strong-force angle; two neutrino splittings; the four neutrino mixing numbers that the mixing pattern does not fix; gravity's strength; the amount of dark energy; the amount of dark matter; the matter excess; and the two numbers for the seeds of galaxies. The proton's two containment numbers are effective numbers of the strong force, like the constants of the nuclear-mass formula, and are counted on neither side. The up quark's mass is counted as measured: the η → 3π route reaches it, but trades it for another measured number, so it is a check and not a saving. The numbers attached to the leads (the majoron's mass and the start conditions of the early universe) belong to those leads and are not part of the core. From these it gives the tau mass, the neutrino masses, the down and up quarks, ρ = 1 and the neutron-star maximum mass.

For a mathematician

A fairer comparison than raw counts is predictive surplus: the number of independent measured quantities reproduced or predicted minus the number of inputs. The framework's ten open predictions are listed on the Results page with their dates of test.

For specialists: sources and where it stands

Status: honest count stated. See the Results page for the full list of inputs and predictions.

Where it stands. The framework takes about 28 measured numbers, against about 34 for the Standard Model with gravity and cosmology counted the same way (the 28 assumes every pending measurement agrees; counting only savings already confirmed it is 32, plus six stated choices; see Inputs and predictions). Three of the six saved numbers are firm (the tau, the down quark and the lightest neutrino) and three wait on measurements. Beyond the count, its gain is that things the older theory simply assumes (which forces exist, the charges, one-handedness, the cap on neutron-star masses) follow from one structure, and that it makes ten definite predictions.

What is still missing

Three of the remaining gaps (the force strengths, the heavy quark masses, the matter excess) come down to rules about the same thing: the heavy partners on the far side of the relationship matrix, which never appear directly. A single rule for the far side's weights and links would move all three together. The framework names this openly rather than filling it with a guess. Its ten predictions, most of them testable by about 2030, are listed on the Results page; any one of them failing would count against it.
