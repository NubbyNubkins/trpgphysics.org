The framework

Each idea below is Matthew and Melissa's, from their original theory. Each one is explained in layers, in the spirit of Feynman: an everyday picture (anyone, any age), the picture (what Matthew and Melissa see), then as physics, as quantum physics, the math (for mathematicians) and for experts (where it meets established physics, and who found that part). The framework in one page

The universe is made of relationships. Particles are real, but what the universe does comes from how they relate. The framework keeps all of established physics and asks one question: what simple picture makes each established result one view of the same thing? It needs three ideas, one rule and one bet.

Purpose and method

This work starts from existing conclusions. Any idea that disagreed with determined measurements was discarded, and the record of each one is kept. Its purpose is to show that the Standard Model, gravity and quantum mechanics already fit together, building on the work of those who found them. Where a choice was made with today's data in view it is labelled as fitted, not predicted. Its predictions are only for measurements that have not yet been made.

Three ideas

  1. Space is one full medium of relationships, seen one perspective at a time, with entanglement first. Like liquid in a sealed bag, there are no gaps and nothing is gained or lost. Written as rules for how relationships combine (no holes, amount kept), only four number systems qualify (Hurwitz, 1898). Once a force richer than the weak force is known to exist, which is a measurement, only the largest of them, the octonions, will do, and fixing one direction in it leaves exactly three colours. Entangled partners are one structure seen from two places; through the area law and Jacobson's argument this gives the form of Einstein's gravity. The 3 × 3 octonion relationship matrix, seen from one spot facing one way, gives which forces exist (8, 3 and 1 carriers). Read three at a time, it gives every particle of a generation with its measured charge, and one-handed matter. Which forces exist follows by a theorem: the part of the spot's symmetry (Spin(9)) that respects one facing is exactly 8 + 3 + 1, whichever facing is chosen (Günaydin and Gürsey, 1973; Todorov and Dubois-Violette, 2018; Krasnov, 2019). The whole generation and one-handed matter are reproduced exactly with one further reading of the matrix, which is stated, not derived. The full medium is consistent with the measured flat universe, but does not by itself require it. Sections 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 8 and 9 below unpack this idea.
  2. Every structure is a balance of push and pull (containment). A star leaks, a black hole holds everything in, and a proton balances. Fitted to the proton and the Δ alone, the balance gives the containment pressure B = 60 (57–64) MeV/fm³. Carried into neutron stars, the same B meets the two observed limits only if the quarks in their cores pair with a gap of about 82 to 97 MeV. That gap is the prediction. Sections 4 and 5 below unpack this idea.
  3. Within the lepton families, a particle's tie to itself and its ties to the others are balanced (45°). One matrix with one scale gives the electron, muon and tau (Koide's relation), and applied to neutrinos with the two measured splittings it gives the neutrino masses. For leptons the balance follows from two of the framework's principles: three placements of one shape make the family matrix look the same after a turn of 120°, and maximum uncertainty between self and relationship sets the self share of its weight to exactly one half, which is the 45° angle. The principle of maximum uncertainty is itself a starting point, not derived from anything deeper, and the balance does not hold for quarks. Section 1 below shows it at work.

One rule: nothing extra. No proton decay, no fourth generation, no new forces, no mirror particles, and one Higgs pair (ρ = 1).

One bet: the far side of the matrix is real. Its three colourless weak pairs and their coloured partners weigh in through the grain of space. They make the three force strengths meet, which fixes the strong coupling at 0.1178, and they bring the Higgs mass within reach of a rule. If the bet fails, the three ideas and the rule still stand.

What it takes from measurement

It takes about 28 measured numbers, against about 34 for the Standard Model with gravity and cosmology counted the same way (the 28 assumes every pending measurement agrees; counting only savings already confirmed it is 32, plus six stated choices; see Inputs and predictions): the three force strengths; the Higgs value; the lepton scale and twist; five quark masses (the down quark follows from the Cabibbo angle); four quark-mixing numbers and the strong-force angle; two neutrino splittings; the four neutrino mixing numbers that the mixing pattern does not fix; gravity's strength; the amount of dark energy; the amount of dark matter; the matter excess; and the two numbers for the seeds of galaxies. Saved and confirmed by measurement: the tau mass and the down quark mass. The lightest neutrino mass is saved only if pending measurements agree. Saved only if pending measurements agree: the Higgs mass and two neutrino mixing numbers. One force strength is saved as well if the strong coupling lands at 0.1178. The proton's two containment numbers are effective numbers of the strong force, like the constants of the nuclear-mass formula, and are counted on neither side. The up quark's mass is counted as measured: the η → 3π route reaches it, but trades it for another measured number, so it is a check and not a saving. The numbers attached to the leads (the majoron's mass and the start conditions of the early universe) belong to those leads and are not part of the core.

What is still open

  • How the Higgs link tells the three families apart. This one question holds the heavy quark masses, quark mixing and the twist.
  • The strengths at the grain of space, including gravity's.
  • Why the Higgs settles, and why at 246 GeV.
  • What dark matter and dark energy are. Both amounts are measured. The matrix allows one candidate for dark matter, a cold chargeless wave, but gives no number for it.
  • The matter excess, which is measured: allowed, not predicted.
  • Where the seeds of galaxies came from. Their size and tilt are measured and not derived.
  • Why there are no more than three generations. The octonion matrix stops at 3 × 3, which is a hint and not a proof.
  • Where quantum probabilities come from.

1. Relationships make the universe

Everyday picture

A family is made of people, and each person is real on their own. But the word "family" is about how those people are connected: parent, child, brother, cousin. Take the connections away and you have a crowd, not a family. The universe is like that. Every particle is real, but what the universe does comes from how its particles are connected to one another.

The picture (what Matthew and Melissa see)

In Matthew's words: particles individually are there, sure, but the relationship is what matters. Write down how every particle relates to every other particle and you get a table, a matrix. That matrix is the first one. There are more matrices beyond it (antiparticles, decays, and others still to be worked out), and the theory is built from them.

As physics

Physics never measures a thing by itself. It measures one thing against another: a length against a ruler, a mass against a standard, a time against a clock. Even "the electron's mass" means the electron compared with something else. So the natural way to describe nature is as a table of comparisons. List every particle along the top and down the side, and fill in how each relates to each. That table is a matrix, and the patterns in it are the laws.

As quantum physics

In quantum mechanics a particle's properties are not fixed little labels it carries around. They show up in how it interacts. An electron's spin has a definite value only relative to the direction you measure it along. The relational view takes this seriously: the interactions are the reality, and the "things" are where interactions meet.

As math

Comparing two masses fairly, whatever units you use, requires a measure that does not change when every mass is multiplied by the same number. There is only one such measure:

Sij = ½ ln(mi/mj), (mi + mj)/√(mimj) = 2 cosh Sij

For the 19 known particles this gives 171 pairs. Together with antiparticles and decays, they form the particle-to-particle matrix. For the electron, muon and tau, one 3 × 3 matrix with equal self-terms, links of 1/√2 and a twist of 2/9 produces all three masses from a single scale, to 0.003%.

For experts

The charged-lepton square-root masses are the eigenvalues of M0·circ(1, eiδ/√2, e−iδ/√2). Here r = 1/√2 is equivalent to Koide's relation Q = 2/3 (Koide 1983; geometric form, Foot 1994; parametrisation, Brannen 2006), and δ = 0.222239 ± 0.000040 against 2/9. The same structure, applied to neutrinos with the measured splittings, gives, with today's limit from cosmology, normal ordering with m1 = 0.36 meV. The philosophy of relations first is also developed in Rovelli's relational quantum mechanics (1996).

Status. Established for the charged leptons (three masses from one scale, to 0.003%), with a falsifiable neutrino prediction. For quarks, the down and up masses follow from relations in the matrices (the up quark via the η → 3π decay, without lattice input); the bottom, charm and strange masses remain inputs, so the quark sector is partially solved.

2. Every particle has its own view, and ours is the proton's

Everyday picture

An ant and an eagle look at the same field. The ant sees giant blades of grass, and the eagle sees a green patch. Neither is wrong. Each sees the field at its own size. Every particle "sees" the universe at its own size too. We are built from protons, and we live at the proton's size, so the proton's view is the one we experience.

The picture (what Matthew and Melissa see)

The proton sits at the same scale as the part of the spectrum that physically appears to us, so in a sense it governs that spectrum. That does not make the proton the same thing as a star or anything else. It means the proton is the scale our view is set at, which is why proton-based math comes so close to describing our universe, while the quark scale is barely noticeable to us.

As physics

Each particle has a natural length, set by its mass. The proton sits at the same scale as everything that physically appears to us: atoms, chemistry, light, matter. In that sense the proton governs the world we see. This is why calculations built on the proton come so close to describing our universe, while the much smaller scale of the quarks hardly shows in everyday life.

As quantum physics

A particle's natural length is its Compton wavelength, ħ/(mc), the size below which you cannot pin it down without creating new particles. The proton's is about 0.2 femtometres. That length sets where quantum effects of the proton show, and so where our view of matter begins.

As math

Measured in proton units, the universe holds about 5.5 × 1079 protons' worth of mass and is about 6.5 × 1041 proton lengths across. Gravity between two protons is weaker than their quantum scale by about 1.7 × 1038. Every particle gives its own set of such numbers.

For experts

These are the large-number coincidences of Eddington (1930s), Dirac (1937) and Weinberg (1972), and they are still unexplained. In the relational reading, the proton's numbers are the ones we notice because the proton sets our scale of observation. Whether the proton is singled out by the laws, or is one rung on a smooth ladder, remains to be tested.

Status. Consistent with a long-standing, unexplained observation (the large-number coincidences). Whether the proton's scale is singled out by the laws themselves has not yet been tested.

3. Space is a full bag

Everyday picture

A glass that is half water and half air is not half empty. It is completely full: part water, part air. Space is like that. Imagine a sealed bag filled with liquid, with nothing between the liquid and what floats in it, because the liquid is space. What fills it can change, but the bag is always full.

The picture (what Matthew and Melissa see)

Space is like liquid filling a sealed bag. There is no gap between the liquid and anything in it, because the liquid is space. What fills it changes (matter, light, dark energy), but the bag is always full, and energy is conserved, though it can only be checked locally.

As physics

The universe holds matter, light and dark energy. As it expands, their shares change: light thins fastest, matter next, and dark energy stays steady. But the shares always add up to the whole. Energy is conserved, but only locally, because there is no outside place from which to weigh the entire bag.

As quantum physics

Quantum field theory already says empty space is never truly empty. Every field is present everywhere, even in its lowest state, and that vacuum has energy. "Space is a full medium" is the plain statement of that fact.

As math

"Always full" means the density shares sum to one:

Ωmatter + Ωradiation + ΩΛ = 1

Put into the Friedmann equation with the measured shares, this gives a universe 13.79 billion years old (measured 13.787 ± 0.020).

For experts

This is the spatially flat Friedmann–Lemaître model, with Planck 2018 parameters. Treating the vacuum as a medium is a recognised line of research (Volovik, The Universe in a Helium Droplet, 2003; analogue gravity, Barceló, Liberati and Visser).

Status. Reproduces the measured flatness and the age of the universe. This part agrees with standard cosmology and does not yet go beyond it.

4. Push against pull decides everything

Everyday picture

Stand on the ground. The Earth pushes up on you, and you push down on the Earth. Because the two balance, you stay put. Blow up a balloon: the air pushes out and the rubber pulls in. If the push wins, the balloon pops. If the pull wins, it shrinks. Every object in the universe is a balance like this. The balance has a name here: containment.

The picture (what Matthew and Melissa see)

A star is under-contained: its push wins, so energy leaks out as light and heat. A black hole is over-contained: its pull wins. Inside a proton, gluons pull in and quarks push out. The rule (push against pull) is the same at every size; the forces doing the pushing and pulling are different at each size.

As physics

A star is under-contained. Its push wins, so energy leaks out as light and heat, and that is why it shines. A black hole is over-contained. Its pull wins, so nothing gets out. Inside a proton, the quarks push outward and the gluons pull inward, and the balance sets the proton's size. The rule is the same at every size. Only the forces doing the pushing and pulling change.

As quantum physics

A quark confined in a small space cannot sit still, because confining a wave raises its momentum. That jitter is the push. The strong force between quarks, carried by gluons, is the pull. Most of the proton's mass is the energy of this push and pull, not the mass of the quarks themselves: about 99% if only the up and down quark masses are counted as Higgs mass, and still about 71% if the heavy quarks' indirect effect is counted.

As math

For stars the measure of containment is the compactness:

C = GM/(Rc²)

The Sun is about 0.000002, a neutron star about 0.18, the last stable star 4/9, and a black hole 1/2. For the proton, the pull is a pressure B. Fitting only the proton and Δ masses gives B = 60 (57 to 64) MeV/fm³ and a quark core 0.725 fm across. Carried into neutron stars, the same B gives stars of about 2.0–2.15 solar masses that meet both the pulsar-mass and gravitational-wave limits, provided quarks in their cores pair with a gap of about 82–97 MeV.

For experts

Stars: Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff structure, Buchdahl's limit. Proton: the MIT bag (Chodos, Jaffe, Johnson, Thorn and Weisskopf, 1974), with the cloudy-bag pion cloud (Théberge, Thomas and Miller, 1980) and the Donoghue–Johnson centre-of-mass correction. Neutron stars: perturbative-QCD quark matter, a₄ = 1 − 2αs/π (Freedman and McLerran, 1977; Fraga, Pisarski and Schaffner-Bielich, 2001), with colour-superconducting pairing (Alford, Rajagopal and Wilczek; Rapp, Schäfer, Shuryak and Velkovsky, 1998) and Hinderer tidal deformability. The measured pressure distribution inside the proton (Burkert, Elouadrhiri and Girod, 2018) shows the push-pull balance directly.

Status. Reproduces the proton's containment pressure, the structure of stars and black holes, and, with quark pairing, the heaviest neutron stars. The required pairing gap of about 82–97 MeV is a prediction that can fail.

5. The Sun: a leaking centre, and keeping scales apart

Everyday picture

From an aeroplane, a crowd looks like one smooth patch. Walk into it and you see people. The Sun's "gas" is like that: gas when seen at our size, particles when seen at theirs.

The picture (what Matthew and Melissa see)

At the Sun's centre is a particle that is, or acts like, a singularity. It explains the Sun's forces, but its containment is not strong enough, so it leaks. The gases around it are gases only at our scale; at the particle scale they are particles too, bursting outward from that centre because the leakage is not contained. As Matthew puts it, the biggest misunderstanding in physics is not whether these things exist, but mixing up scales when we think about them.

As physics

The framework proposes that the Sun's centre holds a particle that is, or behaves like, a singularity. It would hold the Sun's forces together, but its containment would not be strong enough, so it leaks. What surrounds it is gas at our scale; at the scale of the central object it is particles too, moving outward because the leakage is never contained. The essential point is that the two descriptions belong to different scales. Much confusion in thinking about such objects comes not from whether they exist but from mixing those scales.

As quantum physics

Fusion in the Sun's core is quantum tunnelling: hydrogen nuclei get through their electric repulsion only because they are waves. The neutrinos from that fusion reach us directly from the core, so the gas around the centre is measurably burning.

For experts

This is an interpretation within the containment picture rather than additional physics. Fusion in the core is confirmed by solar neutrinos (Borexino, 2014 and 2020). A small dense central object is not excluded (stars with central black holes, Bellinger and others, 2023). The decisive test is helioseismology close to the core.

Status. A hypothesis. It is not excluded by current data, and helioseismology near the solar core can test it. No quantitative model has yet been written.

6. Resistance, and why there are waves

Everyday picture

Shine a torch into a fish tank. The beam bends where it enters the water, because light moves more slowly in water. Space works in a similar way. Near anything heavy, space resists a little more, so light slows and bends as it passes.

The picture (what Matthew and Melissa see)

Space has a baseline resistance. Mass-energy raises it, equally for time and for space. Waves are energy spreading out through regions of different resistance.

As physics

Space has a baseline resistance. Mass and energy raise it, and they raise it equally for time and for space. A wave is energy spreading out through regions of different resistance, the way a ripple bends when it moves from deep water into shallow.

As quantum physics

Every particle is also a wave, so every particle feels this resistance. A falling apple and a passing photon follow the same rule. That is why gravity acts on everything equally, which is tested to one part in 1015.

As math

Give space an index of resistance

n = 1 + 2GM/(rc²)

Starlight grazing the Sun then bends by 1.75″ (measured 1.75″), and radar echoes from the planets are delayed by exactly the observed amount. If the resistance acted only on time, the bending would be half as large (0.876″). If it had no direction, there would be none. It must act on time and space equally.

For experts

This is the optical-metric form of weak-field general relativity (Gordon, 1923; Eddington). It is tested by the Shapiro delay (1964) and by Cassini (γ − 1 = (2.1 ± 2.3) × 10−5). Feynman, Weinberg (1964) and Deser (1970) showed that a long-range, universally coupled field of this tensor character must be Einstein's gravity, so the factor of two is general relativity's own.

Status. Reproduces the deflection of light and the Shapiro delay exactly. The factor of two is inherited from general relativity rather than derived independently.

7. Entanglement comes first, and gravity follows

Everyday picture

Put a coin between two mirrors so that you see heads in one and tails in the other. When you see heads, you know at once what the other mirror shows. Nothing travelled between the mirrors. It was always one coin, seen from two places. Entangled particles are like that.

The picture (what Matthew and Melissa see)

Entangled particles touch through the geometry, so there is no distance to cross. Quantum physics is entanglement, as Schrödinger said. Gravity is a byproduct of all that entanglement. Uncertainty means we see one perspective of a single geometry at a time.

As physics

All the particles in the universe are linked through entanglement, like a vast web of acquaintances. Gravity is what that web adds up to: wherever there is more stuff, there are more links, and the geometry draws together. Entangled particles touch through the geometry itself, so there is no distance to cross, and nothing travels faster than light.

As quantum physics

Schrödinger called entanglement the characteristic feature of quantum mechanics. Heisenberg's uncertainty principle says that we can see only one side of a single geometry at a time. Measure position sharply, and momentum spreads out. These are two views of one object, never both at once.

As math

Entangled pairs measured at angles a and b agree with correlation

E(a, b) = −cos(a − b)

This is stronger than any "hidden instructions" model allows. In a filled medium, the entanglement between a region and its surroundings grows with the area of the boundary, not the volume inside. Combine this with the rule heat = temperature × entropy at every horizon, and Einstein's equations of gravity follow. Uncertainty is a property of waves: Δx · Δk ≥ ½.

For experts

Schrödinger (1935); Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen (1935); Bell (1964); Clauser and Freedman (1972); Aspect, Grangier and Roger (1982). Area law: Bombelli, Koul, Lee and Sorkin (1986); Srednicki (1993). Einstein's equations as an equation of state: Jacobson (1995). Entanglement and geometry: Ryu and Takayanagi (2006); Van Raamsdonk (2010); ER = EPR (Maldacena and Susskind, 2013).

Status. Rests on Jacobson's derivation, which is established. The lattice calculation shown here demonstrates the area law in a model medium; it does not by itself determine the value of Newton's constant.

8. Space is always changing, and flat overall

Everyday picture

Drop two pebbles into a still pond. Where the ripples cross, the water forms bumps, dips and saddle shapes, and the pattern never stops moving. Yet the pond as a whole stays level.

The picture (what Matthew and Melissa see)

Space is made of crossing waves. Locally it curves like peaks, saddles and troughs, and the pattern keeps changing with place and time, but on average it is flat. That constant change is the "Transitioning" in the name.

As physics

Space behaves like crossing waves. Up close it curves in every way, as peaks, saddles and troughs, and the pattern keeps changing from place to place and moment to moment. Averaged over large distances it is flat, exactly as astronomers measure. That constant change is the "Transitioning" in the theory's name.

As quantum physics

No quantum field is ever perfectly still. Even in empty space it fluctuates, and these fluctuations have measurable effects, such as the Casimir force between two close metal plates. A geometry that is always transitioning is the large-scale shadow of that restlessness.

As math

The simplest crossing-wave surface is

z = cos x + cos y

Every cell holds one peak, two saddles and one trough. The total curvature over a cell is exactly zero, so the surface is flat on average.

For experts

Zero total curvature follows from the Gauss–Bonnet theorem. The count of peaks, saddles and troughs (1 − 2 + 1 = 0) is the Euler characteristic of the torus, as Morse theory gives. The observed spatial flatness is |Ωk| ≲ 0.002 (Planck 2018).

Status. Mathematically exact for the model surface, and consistent with the measured flatness of space. A dynamical equation for the transitioning geometry is still to be written.

9. The Big Bang was a drop landing on a still pond

Everyday picture

Picture a very still pond in a light drizzle. The surface is almost perfectly flat. A drop lands, and a ring of little waves spreads out. Nothing was added that was not already water: there is the same amount as before, spread out as waves.

The picture (what Matthew and Melissa see)

The Big Bang was an over-contained state releasing. Because space is always full and there is nothing outside it, the same amount is spread throughout, moving out as waves. Each drop on the pond is a big bang, and ours need not be the only one. From inside one ring every point experiences the same thing, so the "centre" is a moment in time rather than a place. Conservation holds, but it can only be checked locally. What caused the release is outside the framework's scope.

As physics

The Big Bang was the release of an over-contained singularity, a containment that could no longer hold. Because the bag is always full and there is no empty space outside it, the release did not happen at a spot inside a bigger room. It happened everywhere at once. The "centre" is a moment in time, not a place, so every observer sees the same afterglow in every direction.

As quantum physics

In the first instants everything was so hot that particles formed and dissolved continuously. As the universe cooled, the heavier particles stopped forming, then the lighter ones. The leftover proportions, such as about 25% helium by mass, match measurements.

As math

A full bag that expands uniformly obeys the Friedmann equation, (ȧ/a)² = (8πG/3)ρ, with no centre and no edge. Its afterglow is uniform to 1 part in 100,000, as observed.

For experts

Homogeneous, isotropic expansion is the Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker solution. The release itself has not yet been written as a dynamical equation within this framework.

Status. Consistent with the observed expansion and the uniformity of the cosmic background. The release itself has not yet been written as a dynamical equation.

How the nine sections fit together

The ideas are not independent assumptions. The relational principle (1) and the scale principle (2) say what is compared and from where. The full medium (3) says that there is nothing outside the comparisons. Containment (4, 5) and resistance (6) describe how structures form and how energy moves within the medium. Entanglement (7) is the relation itself at the quantum level, and gravity is its large-scale consequence. The transitioning geometry (8) describes the medium locally, and the release of an over-contained state (9) describes its history.

Written together, these statements become a single action, given on the Equations explained page. Most of its terms are the established physics of Einstein, Yang and Mills, Dirac and Higgs. The framework adds a containment field, a scale field and a relational form for the masses, and the Results page records what follows from them and what does not yet.

A note on honest labels

A "fail" is not a bad thing. It shows where not to walk again, which is exactly how physics has always worked. As Matthew wrote: "Most of physics was wrong but through constant analysis and thought experiments and trials and errors it became physics."
