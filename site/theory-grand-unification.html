One Force in the Beginning: the Story of Grand Unification

The idea that the three forces of the particle world were once a single force, and split apart as the universe cooled.

Chapter 1. Three rivers from one spring

Stand at the mouth of three rivers and they look nothing alike. One is wide and slow, one narrow and fast, one muddy. Walk upstream long enough and you may find they all pour from a single spring in the mountains.

In the early 1970s physicists noticed something like this about the forces. The strong force, the weak force and electromagnetism have very different strengths today. But their strengths change with energy: the strong force weakens at high energy, the others grow. Draw the three lines on a graph, climb to enormous energies, and the lines nearly meet.

What if, at the very beginning, when the universe was unimaginably hot, there was only one force?

Chapter 2. Sorting the particles into one family

In 1974 Howard Georgi and Sheldon Glashow proposed the simplest version, called SU(5). In it, every particle of one generation fits into two neat boxes, like socks sorted into a drawer. The same year Jogesh Pati and Abdus Salam suggested that lepton number is a fourth “colour” of quark.

A year later Harald Fritzsch, Peter Minkowski and Georgi found something even neater, called SO(10). Every particle of a generation, sixteen in all, fits into a single box. The sixteenth was a surprise: a heavy partner of the neutrino that nobody had seen. That partner turns out to be useful. If it is very heavy, it pushes the ordinary neutrino to be very light, like a seesaw with a giant on one end and a child on the other. This seesaw (Minkowski 1977; Yanagida; Gell-Mann, Ramond and Slansky; Mohapatra and Senjanović) explains why neutrinos weigh almost nothing.

In 1976 Feza Gürsey, Pierre Ramond and Pierre Sikivie went one step further, to a pattern called E6, where each generation fills a box of 27 places: the sixteen known particles, ten heavier companions and one loner.

Chapter 3. The beautiful bonus

Grand unification explains a puzzle the Standard Model only states. Why is the proton’s electric charge exactly equal and opposite to the electron’s, to better than one part in a billion billion? In grand unification, quarks and leptons are members of the same family, so their charges are locked together. Electric charge comes in fixed steps because the family demands it.

Chapter 4. The prediction that failed

If quarks and leptons are cousins, a quark can sometimes turn into a lepton. That means the proton, the heart of every atom, should eventually fall apart. The simplest SU(5) theory said a proton should last about 10³⁰ years. That is far longer than the age of the universe, but a huge tank of water contains so many protons that a few should decay every year.

Physicists built such tanks, deep underground in Japan and the United States. They watched for decades. No proton has ever been seen to decay. Super-Kamiokande puts the proton’s lifetime above 2.4 × 10³⁴ years. The simplest grand unified theory was ruled out.

That is science working as it should. A beautiful idea made a bold prediction, and nature said no.

Chapter 5. The survivors

The bigger versions, SO(10) and E6, survive. They push proton decay further out or need extra ingredients. Without an extra idea called supersymmetry, the three force lines do not quite meet at one point, so the theories add intermediate steps. Supersymmetry has not been seen at the Large Hadron Collider. The next giant tank, Hyper-Kamiokande in Japan, will look again for proton decay in the 2030s.

The theory at a glance

Main idea The three particle forces were one at very high energy; each generation fits one pattern (16 in SO(10), 27 in E6)
Measured numbers fewer force strengths (one instead of three) but more Higgs numbers; overall similar to the Standard Model
Got right why charges come in fixed steps; the heavy neutrino partner and the seesaw
Failed minimal SU(5): protons do not decay as fast as predicted
Still to come proton decay at Hyper-Kamiokande; gravity is not included

How it differs from TRPG

TRPG lands on exactly the same family patterns, the 16 and the 27, but gets them from relationships rather than from one big force. In TRPG the forces meet in strength but never merge. That one difference makes a clean test:

  • Grand unification: protons decay eventually.
  • TRPG: protons never decay at any rate we could ever see. Gravity alone would give about 10⁴⁴ years.

One proton decay seen at Hyper-Kamiokande would favour grand unification and break TRPG. Unlike grand unification, TRPG also tries to say something about gravity.
