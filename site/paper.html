Abstract

We present Dynamic Relational Transitioning Geometry (TRPG), a framework in which the universe is described by the relationships between its parts rather than by the parts alone. It rests on five rules and one bet. (1) Everything is one matrix of relationships, seen one perspective at a time, with entanglement first: matter is held states on its diagonal, forces are the links between them, the Higgs is the link across one state, and space and time are the geometry of the relationships. (2) Every bound structure is a pull balanced by a resistance (containment), and mass is that balance. (3) Within the lepton families a particle’s tie to itself balances its ties to the others. (4) Nothing extra. (5) The universe started smooth; whether a short stretching followed is left to measurement (Section 5). The bet is that the far side of the relationship matrix is real. Section 2 unpacks these into the working principles; earlier versions stated rules 1–4 as three ideas and one rule.

Combined with established physics, these principles reproduce the following.

  • Einstein’s gravity, through the entanglement area law and Jacobson’s thermodynamic argument. This includes the full deflection of starlight (1.75″) and the Shapiro delay.
  • The expansion of a flat universe, with an age of 13.79 billion years; with the alternative start phase, also the flatness itself and the uniform temperature of the sky.
  • A containment pressure B = 60 (57 to 64) MeV/fm3, obtained from the proton and Δ masses alone.
  • Carried to compact stars (observed as neutron stars, modelled here as strange quark stars), that same pressure gives maximum masses of 2.0 to 2.13 solar masses and a tidal deformability Λ1.4 = 459 to 575, consistent with observation, provided quarks in the core pair with a gap of about 82 to 97 MeV. Without pairing the stars fail; the gap band is the one the observed stars require, so the star masses are not evidence, and the gap is the prediction for an independent determination.
  • The three charged-lepton masses as the eigenvalues of a single relational matrix with two numbers, a scale and a twist, under the 45° balance.
  • The strong force SU(3) with three colours, once the medium’s inner space is required to have no gaps and to conserve the size of relationships; and, reading the forces from the relationship matrix seen from one perspective, with stated readings, which other forces exist and, reading the matrix’s states three at a time, every particle of a generation with its measured charge and one-handed matter. The force strengths are taken from measurement.
  • Fractional quark charges, which anomaly cancellation fixes once the number of colours is given.

The framework makes predictions that can fail and that experiments can test. The first is normal neutrino mass ordering (given today’s limit from cosmology), with a lightest mass of 0.36 meV and a sum of 0.0591 to 0.0594 eV. The second is a quark pairing gap of about 82 to 97 MeV in the cores of compact stars (for a strange-quark mass of 100 MeV; what the stars fix is 4Δ2 − ms2). A third is a strong coupling of 0.1178, on which the meeting of the force strengths rests and which awaits measurement. A fourth, beyond the reach of present and planned experiments, is that neutrinos are their own antiparticles, with neutrinoless double beta decay at an effective mass of 1.3 to 4.1 meV. Tested strictly, two quark-mass relations in the particle matrices pass: the down quark through the Cabibbo angle and the up quark through the decay η → 3π, without lattice input. The force strengths together with gravity’s strength, the top, bottom, charm and strange masses with quark mixing, the lepton twist (0.222222, close to 2/9 but measured, and the same open number as the electron’s mass) remain open, as do the mass and amount of dark matter (the matrix allows one candidate, a chargeless wave, the majoron, as a lead) and the size and tilt of the seeds of galaxies, which are measured; the alternative start phase would predict the tilt, ns = 0.963, and primordial gravitational waves at r = 0.0040 ± 0.0003, and is favoured only if the tilt settles near the CMB-only value. The matter–antimatter excess is not predicted; it is allowed. Where a result was already established by others (the W and Z mass relation, atomic binding, lepton lifetimes, the Cabibbo angle, hadron and nuclear masses), the framework reaches it by a second route, and each such check is reported with its status: confirmed, fitted, or failed.

1. Introduction

Physics has several very successful descriptions of nature. General relativity describes gravity as geometry. Quantum mechanics describes matter as waves of probability. Quantum field theory describes the forces. Cosmology describes the history of the whole. Each is confirmed to remarkable precision, and yet they are usually presented as separate subjects.

This work starts from a different question. What if the great physicists were each correct, and each was looking at the same universe from a different side? Then the task is not to replace any of them. It is to find the single picture in which each of their results is one view.

The picture proposed here is relational. A particle is real on its own, but what it does, and what the universe does, comes from how particles relate to one another. Write down how every particle relates to every other and you obtain a matrix. The structure of physics is the structure of such matrices.

Purpose and method. This work starts from existing conclusions. Any idea that disagreed with determined measurements was discarded, and the record of each one is kept. Its purpose is to show that the Standard Model, gravity and quantum mechanics already fit together, building on the work of those who found them. Where a choice was made with today’s data in view it is labelled as fitted, not predicted. Its predictions are only for measurements that have not yet been made.

Section 2 states the principles. Section 3 writes them as two actions, a fundamental one and an effective one for hadrons. Sections 4 to 8 show what follows, each compared with measurement. Section 9 gives the predictions, Section 10 the open questions, and Section 11 concludes.

2. Principles

The statement of v1.4.0: five rules and one bet. The framework is one matrix of relationships read in three roles. Held states sit on the diagonal: the 16 states of a generation, placed three times. Links between different states are the pulls: 8 colour links, 3 weak links and 1 hypercharge phase, the twelve carriers. The link across one state, from its left-handed side to its right-handed side, is the Higgs: the resistance that gives weight, which is also the perspective rule of handedness (Section 8). The strengths of these links (the masses and mixings) are where every open number of the framework lives. This layout is the one used to compute the proton on a lattice (Wilson, 1974): matter on the points, forces on the links; relabelling each point’s colours independently leaves every term unchanged (analysis/forces_resistances_matrix_2026-11-19.py; figures/relationship_matrix_diagonal_links.png). It is a reading of the same equations and changes no number.

  1. One matrix of relationships, seen one perspective at a time, with entanglement first. The geometry of the relationships is the stage: four numbers in a 2 × 2 grid of complex relationships have the determinant t2 − x2 − y2 − z2, unchanged by any change of perspective, which is 3 + 1 space and time with light speed built in (Penrose and Rindler, 1984); quaternions would give 5 + 1 and octonions 9 + 1. Gravity follows from entanglement and heat flow (Jacobson, 1995), and space can be built from entanglement in model universes (Van Raamsdonk, 2010), though this is not yet shown for ours. The rules of quantum theory reduce to two: complex waves, now required by experiment (Renou et al., 2021), and entanglement first, from which quantum theory has been derived (Hardy, 2001; Chiribella, D’Ariano and Perinotti, 2011). Uncertainty is what any wave does; the probability rule follows from the space of states (Gleason, 1957); spin–statistics and CPT are theorems. Time can be read from relationships, as change seen by any part entangled with a clock (Page and Wootters, 1983). Charges are which links touch a state (Noether, 1918).
  2. Balance. Whatever holds together is a pull balanced by a resistance, and mass is the balance point. The weak force is the electric family of pull cut short by the Higgs resistance (Anderson, 1963); the strong force is a pull whose carriers resist each other, squeezing the field into a tube, and its resistance scale follows from its strength. The same balance sets the proton’s containment pressure and, on the largest scales, the radius at which a galaxy group’s pull equals the tension of dark energy (about 1.6 Mpc for the Local Group, just outside its observed edge of about 1 Mpc).
  3. Maximum uncertainty between self and relationship (the 45° rule, leptons only; Section 10).
  4. Nothing extra.
  5. Smooth start. The universe began smooth (Penrose, 1979), which sets the direction of time. Whether the stiffness of space then drove a short stretching (Starobinsky, 1980) is decided by measurement; the main line follows today’s combined data and does not take it (Section 5).

The bet is unchanged: the far side of the relationship matrix is real.

Of these, rules 1 and 2 restate earlier ideas 1 and 2, rules 3 and 4 are unchanged, and rule 5 is new; its stretching phase is the alternative, not the main line. What is assumed without explanation falls from the earlier rules plus the borrowed rules of quantum theory, the borrowed stage of general relativity and about nine start facts, to five rules and one bet. Measured numbers are not reduced by this restatement (Appendix A.2). Not testable as such: the matrix layout and the pull-and-resistance reading (they restate the same equations), space and time from relationships in our universe (not yet), and why the start was smooth. The earlier statement follows, unchanged in content.

The earlier statement (v1.3.3). Stated most simply, the framework is three ideas, one rule and one bet. The ideas. (1) Space is one full medium of relationships, seen one perspective at a time, with entanglement first. (2) Every structure is a balance of outward push and inward pull (containment). (3) Within the lepton families, a particle’s tie to itself and its ties to the others are balanced (45°); for leptons this follows from the family symmetry together with maximum uncertainty between self and relationship (Section 10), and it does not hold for quarks (Section 7). The rule. Nothing extra: nothing beyond the 27 states of each generation’s matrix (the 16 known, the 10 of the far side and one neutral state), the twelve carriers and one Higgs pair. So no proton decay, no fourth generation, no new forces, no mirror families and no second Higgs pair. The bet. The far side of the relationship matrix is real (with two stated assumptions, Section 10); it gives the strong coupling and the Higgs-mass rule of Section 9, and if it fails the ideas and the rule still stand. Which forces exist follows from the perspective principle by a theorem (Section 8). A whole generation’s charges and one-handed matter are reproduced with one further reading of the matrix, which is stated and not derived. The full medium is consistent with the measured flat universe but does not by itself require it. The principles below unpack the three ideas.

  1. One medium. Space is like liquid filling a sealed bag. There is no gap between the liquid and anything in it, because the liquid is space itself.
  2. Always full. The proportions of matter, radiation and dark energy change, but together they always fill the whole. Energy is conserved, though conservation can be checked only locally.
  3. Resistance with direction. Space has a baseline resistance. Mass and energy raise it, equally for time and for space. Waves are energy spreading through regions of different resistance.
  4. Containment. Every bound structure is a balance of outward push against inward pull. If the push wins, the structure leaks, as a star does. If the pull wins, it holds everything in, as a black hole does. Inside a proton, quarks push out and gluons pull in. The rule is the same at every size, while the forces differ.
  5. Relational scale. Each particle sets its own scale of view, and no scale is privileged by the laws themselves. The proton sits at the scale of the spectrum we physically observe, so it sets our view. The only scale-fair distance between two masses is S = ½ ln(m1/m2).
  6. Entanglement first. Entanglement is the defining feature of quantum physics, as Schrödinger said in 1935. Entangled partners are joined through the geometry, so nothing needs to cross the distance between them. Gravity follows from entanglement, and uncertainty means that we see one perspective of a single geometry at a time.
  7. Transitioning geometry. Space behaves like crossing waves. Locally it curves as peaks, saddles and troughs, and the pattern keeps changing, but its total curvature averages to zero.
  8. Smooth start (v1.4.0). The universe began smooth. A short stretching driven by the stiffness of space against bending is kept as the alternative start (Section 5).

3. The action

The framework is written as two actions: a fundamental one, valid from the grain of space down, and an effective one for the inside of hadrons and compact stars. Units are natural (ħ = c = 1) and the metric signature is (+, −, −, −). MPl = G−1/2 = 1.22 × 1019 GeV is the Planck energy, M̄P = (8πG)−1/2 = 2.44 × 1018 GeV the reduced Planck mass, and the grain of space is Egrain = MPl/√2 = 8.63 × 1018 GeV (Section 9). Every term has mass dimension four.

Gravity (R). The curvature term is not put in by hand. It emerges from entanglement, as shown in Section 4. The entanglement route (Jacobson, 1995) yields only the trace-free part of Einstein’s equations, with the cosmological constant appearing as a constant of integration; the metric is therefore varied at fixed volume element (unimodular gravity: Einstein, 1919; van der Bij, van Dam and Ng, 1982; Henneaux and Teitelboim, 1989; Weinberg, 1989). In this form the constant part of V(H) does not curve space (Section 5). Changes of V(H) with time, such as its drop at the electroweak crossover near 160 GeV, still gravitate in the ordinary way; at that time they are a small part of the energy, which radiation dominates.

Containment (P). P is an effective field of the strong force, in the manner of Friedberg and Lee (1977): it describes the gluon vacuum inside hadrons and compact stars and is not added to the fundamental action, so the strong force is not counted twice. It has mass dimension one, so gP is a pure number. The depth of its potential, λv4/4, is the containment pressure B. Its stiffness, √(2λ) v, is the lightest glueball, whose mass from lattice calculations is 1.71 to 1.73 GeV. With B from the proton (Section 6), λ ≈ 1160 to 1210 and v ≈ 35 MeV. No parameter is tuned. A quartic coupling this large is far beyond the range where loop corrections are small (about 8π/3 for unitarity, 16π2 at most), so Seff is used only as a classical, mean-field description of the bag, as Friedberg and Lee used it, and not as a weakly coupled quantum field theory.

Leptons (Sℓ). Sℓ = M0 · circulant(1, eiδ/√2, e−iδ/√2), whose eigenvalues are the square roots of the charged-lepton masses (so M0 carries units of √mass). The term pairs the left-handed doublet LL with the right-handed singlet eR, as gauge symmetry requires, and Sℓ2/vH is a pure number. The matrix has two numbers, the scale M0 and the twist δ, with links of 1/√2 (Section 7). It is a fixed relational matrix, not a field: the action records the pattern and does not supply a mechanism that produces it.

Far side and neutrinos. Each generation’s 27 holds, besides the 16 known states, the 10 of the far side and one neutral state. The far-side 10 are spin-½ matter: a colourless weak pair and a coloured pair of charge ∓1/3 (the D states), each with both handednesses, so they take ordinary masses ML and MD (Section 10). The Higgs doublet H is the separate spin-0 copy of the colourless part of the same numbers, as in the standard E6 construction; its coloured spin-0 parts are absent (Section 8). MN is the Majorana mass of the hidden neutrino partners, written with N as a right-handed Weyl field and Nc its conjugate.

What earlier drafts had. A scale field σ, which coupled to nothing, is dropped: the relational scale S = ½ ln(m1/m2) is a coordinate on mass ratios, not a field.

Inputs. Put together as one calculation (Section 10), the framework takes about 28 measured numbers, against about 34 for the Standard Model with gravity and cosmology counted the same way (the 28 assumes every pending measurement agrees; counting only savings already confirmed it is 32, plus six stated choices; see Inputs and predictions). They are: the three force strengths; the Higgs value; the charged-lepton scale and twist; five quark masses (the down quark follows from the Cabibbo angle); four quark-mixing numbers and the strong-interaction angle; two neutrino splittings; the four neutrino mixing numbers that the mixing pattern does not fix; gravity’s strength; the amount of dark energy; the amount of dark matter; the matter excess; and the two numbers for the seeds of galaxies (their size and tilt). Saved against the standard list and confirmed by measurement are the tau mass and the down quark mass. The lightest neutrino mass, the Higgs mass (Section 9) and two neutrino mixing numbers are saved only if pending measurements agree, so the figure of about 28 holds only if they all do, and counting only confirmed savings it is 32 (Appendix A); and one force strength is saved as well if the strong coupling lands at 0.1178. The proton’s two containment numbers are effective numbers of the strong force, like the constants of the nuclear-mass formula, and are counted on neither side. The up quark’s mass is counted as measured: the η → 3π route trades it for the measured ratio Q, so it is a check and not a saving. The numbers attached to the leads (the majoron’s mass and the start conditions of the early universe) belong to those leads and are not part of the core.

4. Gravity

Resistance and the bending of light. Give space a resistance n = 1 + 2GM/(rc2) around a mass M. Light passing the Sun is then deflected by 1.75″, as measured. The radar echo from planets is delayed by exactly the Shapiro amount. The factor of two matters. A resistance acting on time alone would give half the deflection (0.876″), and one with no direction would give none. The resistance must act on time and space equally. This is the optical form of weak-field general relativity (Eddington, Gordon). Feynman, Weinberg and Deser showed that a long-range field which couples to everything, and which has this tensor character, must be Einstein’s gravity.

Gravity from entanglement. In a filled medium, the entanglement between a region and its surroundings grows with the area of the boundary rather than with the volume inside. We confirmed this on a lattice of coupled oscillators: a boundary law fits the computed entanglement far better than a volume law. Jacobson (1995) showed that if this area law holds and the relation δQ = T dS holds at every local horizon, Einstein’s equations follow. Gravity is then a consequence of entanglement, not an extra ingredient. Related work by Ryu and Takayanagi, and by Van Raamsdonk, connects entanglement directly to the shape of space.

Gravity’s strength. Entanglement fixes the form of gravity (Bekenstein, 1973; Hawking, 1975; Jacobson, 1995) but not its strength, which is the fineness of the links of space: ln(Egrain/mp) = 43.7, with Egrain = MPl/√2 (Section 3). It is tied to the force strengths, since the proton is light compared with the grain because the strong force is weak there and strengthens only slowly (Gross and Wilczek, 1973; Politzer, 1973; Wilczek, 2001); the simple count 2π/(7αs) at the grain gives 47.5 with the Standard Model’s states alone and 43.6 with the far side included. The second is close, but the count uses the six-quark rate of running all the way down while the actual rate changes where the far-side states enter, so the closeness is not counted as evidence. The tie trades one measured number for another. Setting gravity equal to the other forces where they meet predicts a grain energy 62 times too low (Kaplunovsky, 1988), and counting the framework’s 191 states (124 for the Standard Model with three hidden partners, 66 for the far side and one majoron; Sakharov, 1967) does not give the size. The Higgs field cannot set gravity’s strength (Zee, 1979; the required curvature coupling exceeds the LHC limit of Atkins and Calmet, 2013, by 4 × 1016); conversely, a Higgs self-coupling vanishing at the grain (Shaposhnikov and Wetterich, 2010) needs mH = 128.84 ± 1.45 GeV at two loops with the Standard Model’s states alone against 125.13 (Buttazzo et al., 2013; Particle Data Group, 2026), a strict fail at 2.6σ (Section 9).

Black holes. For the remnant of the first observed merger, GW150914, the ringdown is 250 Hz with a damping time of 4.0 ms. This agrees with the Kerr solution and with the measurement. A black hole is fully contained, so no echoes are expected, and none have been seen.

5. Cosmology

The full bag. The density parameters are measured to sum to one, Ωm + Ωr + ΩΛ = 1 to within 0.2% (Ωk = 0.0007 ± 0.0019; Planck 2018 with BAO): on the largest scales the universe is spatially flat. A full medium is consistent with this but does not require it, since closed and open universes are also full everywhere; flatness is taken from measurement. With the alternative start phase (below) it would also be explained: the stretching leaves |Ωk| near 10−5 or less. Solving the Friedmann equation with Planck 2018 parameters gives an age of 13.79 billion years (measured 13.787 ± 0.020).

No centre. The Big Bang is the release of an over-contained singularity. It is not an explosion into empty space: the same amount is everywhere, spreading as waves. Because there is no empty space outside the bag, the “centre” is a moment in time, not a place. Every observer sees the same background glow.

The start (v1.4.0): main line and alternative. The main line follows today’s combined data. With the CMB alone the tilt of the first ripples is 0.9638 ± 0.0040; combined with DESI’s baryon acoustic oscillations it is 0.969–0.974 ± 0.003, and the framework’s dark-energy reading allows the change in dark energy that DESI hints at (below), so the main line takes that direction: it does not adopt this stretching phase and leaves the form of the start open, as in v1.3.3, and flatness, the size and the tilt of the first ripples stay measured inputs. The alternative, kept on record and decided by the tilt, is a stretching phase. Space resists being curved; the simplest form of that stiffness is a term in R2 in the gravitational action (Starobinsky, 1980), which adds no particle. In this alternative, in the first instant it stretches space for about 54–55 e-folds, then hands its energy to the Higgs field as heat. The measured size of the first ripples, As = 2.1 × 10−9, fixes its one number, the scalaron mass of 3.1–3.2 × 1013 GeV; then the tilt of the ripples, ns = 1 − 2/N = 0.9630–0.9636, and the ratio of gravitational waves to ripples, r = 12/N2 = 0.0040 ± 0.0003, follow (Mukhanov and Chibisov, 1981; Bezrukov and Gorbunov, 2012), together with flatness, a uniform temperature, no separate dark-matter ripples and nearly Gaussian seeds. The expansion rate during the start is 1.6 × 1013 GeV. The heat afterwards is about 5 × 109 GeV, or 0.5–2 × 109 GeV if the Higgs couples to curvature with ξ ≈ 0.10–0.15 (Vilenkin, 1985; Bezrukov and Gorbunov, 2012). With the measured top mass the Higgs self-coupling turns negative far below the start’s expansion rate, so the Higgs is safe during the start only if ξ ≳ 0.06 (Herranen et al., 2014; Espinosa et al., 2015); ξ is the seventh choice made with today’s data in view (Appendix A.2). Consequences for the framework’s own pieces: the coloured far-side states (4.6 × 1013 GeV) are heavier than the scalaron and are never made; the far-side weak pairs (2.4 × 108 GeV) are made and must decay (Section 10); the majoron’s starting angle is averaged, because the expansion rate exceeds its settling value (Section 8). Why the start was smooth is not testable. The tilt and r are tested in Section 9. This alternative makes two dated predictions (Section 9); it returns if a CMB-only tilt or a reconciled CMB–DESI tilt settles near 0.963, or if primordial gravitational waves near r = 0.004 are seen.

Expansion as tension. Dark energy has a pressure equal to minus its energy density, which is tension: a pull spread everywhere, like a stretched rubber band, not a pull from an edge (there is no edge or centre). In gravity tension drives faster expansion. With ΩΛ = 0.685 against Ωm = 0.315 the two are equal at redshift 0.30 and the speed-up begins at 0.63, as measured. Bound systems hold where pull exceeds tension: about 110 pc for the Sun and 1.6 Mpc for the Local Group, just outside the observed radius of about 1 Mpc inside which the group has stopped expanding (Karachentsev et al., 2009).

Flat on average, curved locally. The simplest crossing-wave surface, z = cos x + cos y, has one peak, two saddles and one trough per cell. By the Gauss–Bonnet theorem its total curvature over a cell is exactly zero. This shows how local curvature can keep changing while averaging to zero; it is a picture on a two-dimensional surface and does not by itself fix the curvature of three-dimensional space.

Dark energy and dark matter. With gravity from entanglement, Einstein’s equations come out in their trace-free form (Section 3), so the constant part of stored field energy does not gravitate as vacuum energy, and the cosmological constant is a constant of integration fixed once (Einstein, 1919; Jacobson, 1995). Whether this protection survives quantum corrections is debated, and it is not claimed here. Otherwise the Higgs balance energy, 5 × 1054 times the observed value, would dominate (Weinberg, 1989). On the main line the size of dark energy is set by the two ends of space together, the grain and the horizon: √(grain × horizon) = 3.5 meV against 2.24 meV measured, the relation of Cohen, Kaplan and Nelson (1999). This agreement in size follows from the Friedmann equation and is not counted as a derivation; the amount stays one measured number. On this reading dark energy need not be constant, and no law for its change is derived: the holographic formula of Li (2004) gives today’s value but changes in the direction opposite to the hint reported by DESI (2025), and is not used. A fixed amount that never changes is kept on record as the alternative. The closeness of the scale to the neutrino masses is noted and not used. No state of the matrix itself is dark matter: its only chargeless state, the hidden neutrino partner, decays in 10−15 s or less, and dark matter as an entanglement response (Verlinde, 2017) matches the galactic acceleration scale (McGaugh, Lelli and Schombert, 2016) but fails published tests of galaxies, the Solar System and clusters. Reading dark matter as the medium’s baseline resistance holds for galaxies: cH0/2π = 1.04 × 10−10 m/s2 against Milgrom’s scale of about 1.2 × 10−10 m/s2 (Milgrom, 1983), and voids push (Hoffman, Pomarède, Tully and Courtois, 2017); it fails for the Bullet Cluster (Clowe et al., 2006) and the CMB (Planck Collaboration, 2020). The real part is the majoron of Section 8, a cold, chargeless wave.

6. Containment: the proton and neutron stars

The proton. Model the proton and the Δ as bags in containment balance. The pressure B pulls in, and the motion of the quarks pushes out. The pion cloud around the bag is its leakage (the cloudy bag model). We fitted only the measured masses of the proton (938.92 MeV, isospin average) and the Δ (1232 MeV), using the centre-of-mass correction of Donoghue and Johnson and static pion couplings. This gives B = 60.1 MeV/fm3, or 57.1 to 63.7 for a ±5% pion–nucleon coupling. The proton’s quark core then has an rms radius of 0.725 fm. As a check, the same calculation with the 1975 settings returns B = 58.4 MeV/fm3, against the published value of about 57.5.

Neutron stars. In a neutron-star core, the same pressure B holds the quarks in, and the measured strong interaction pushes out, with a4 = 1 − 2αs/π and αs run to the relevant scale. We solved the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff equations with Hinderer’s tidal equation, with the surface correction for a self-bound star. The star is modelled as quark matter all the way to its surface (a strange quark star, Alcock, Farhi and Olinto, 1986), with a strange-quark mass of 100 MeV, αs run at two loops from 0.1180 to twice to four times the quark chemical potential, and no hadronic layer. This model is consistent only if three-flavour quark matter is the ground state of matter while two-flavour matter is not (Bodmer, 1971; Witten, 1984; Farhi and Jaffe, 1984). With the pairing gap below, it is: at zero pressure strange matter has 820 to 880 MeV per baryon (below iron’s 930) and up–down matter 941 to 1021 MeV (above 934, so ordinary nuclei are safe), the narrowest margin being 7 MeV at the lowest B and largest gap. Without pairing strange matter is not the ground state (940 to 962 MeV), which is a second reason the gap is needed. The proton fit uses no free zero-point term (Z0 = 0), the centre-of-mass correction, a fitted bag colour coupling and the measured pion–nucleon coupling f2/4π = 0.0778 with the bag form factor 3j1(kR)/(kR). A hybrid star with a hadronic outer core would change the numbers and is not computed here. The stars actually fix the combination 4Δ2 − ms2, which enters the pressure: 1.7 to 2.8 × 104 MeV2 at the central B. The strange mass runs from 93.5 MeV at 2 GeV to about 120 to 140 MeV at the densities of a star’s core, which moves the gap window to about 88 to 109 MeV; at ms = 130 MeV and the shifted gaps, strange matter stays the ground state (819 to 877 MeV per baryon) and up–down matter stays above 934 MeV within each B’s own window, the narrowest margin shrinking to about 5 MeV at the lowest B. Two caveats go with a strange star. Every compact star would then be a strange star, and large pulsar glitches and the vibrations seen in magnetar flares are hard to explain without a thick nuclear crust, unless the core holds a crystalline paired phase (Mannarelli, Rajagopal and Sharma, 2007). Observed neutron stars are called compact stars below where the strange-star model is meant.

  • With the strong interaction alone, the heaviest star is 1.54 to 1.80 solar masses. This is below the 2.08 ± 0.07 solar masses measured for PSR J0740+6620, so it fails.
  • Quarks in such cores are expected to pair (colour superconductivity). At the central B = 60.1 MeV/fm3, a pairing gap Δ of about 82 to 97 MeV gives a heaviest star of 2.0 to 2.13 solar masses, a radius of 10.8 to 11.3 km for a 1.4-solar-mass star, and a tidal deformability Λ1.4 = 459 to 575.
  • These values meet both the pulsar mass and the GW170817 limit (Λ1.4 below about 580). Across the full range of B allowed by the proton (57.1 to 63.7 MeV/fm3), the window shifts to Δ ≈ 75 to 108 MeV, higher B requiring a larger gap.

The containment pressure comes from the proton, and the push comes from the measured strong force. The one remaining quantity, the pairing gap, becomes a prediction (Section 9).

Stars and black holes. For stars the measure of containment is the compactness C = GM/(Rc2). The Sun has C ≈ 0.000002, a neutron star about 0.18, the last stable configuration 4/9 (Buchdahl’s limit), and a black hole 1/2. A star is under-contained and leaks energy. A black hole is over-contained. The same equations describe every step in between.

7. Relational masses

The relational distance. For any two masses, the quantity S = ½ ln(m1/m2) is the only measure that is unchanged when all masses are multiplied by the same factor. Their combined ratio is (m1 + m2)/√(m1m2) = 2 cosh S. Applying this to every pair of the known particles gives the particle-to-particle matrix. The antiparticle and decay matrices are built in the same way.

The charged leptons. Write the square roots of the three charged-lepton masses as the eigenvalues of one circulant matrix, M0 · circulant(1, reiδ, re−iδ). With r = 1/√2 and δ = 2/9, the matrix gives the electron, muon and tau masses from a scale and the twist, to 0.003%. Fitted with r left free, the twist is 0.222239 ± 0.000040, against 2/9 = 0.222222 (0.4σ); with r fixed at 1/√2, the electron-to-muon ratio pins it at 0.22222205, which is 2/9 to about one part in a million (Section 10). The relation r = 1/√2 is equivalent to Koide’s formula (Koide, 1983), in the geometric form of Foot and the parametrisation of Brannen. In the relational reading, the shared part and the relational part of the mass vector are equal, which is exactly r = 1/√2. Each part is 941.6 MeV, within 0.35% of the proton mass. We note this coincidence without claiming an explanation.

Quarks. Running all six quark masses to a common scale gives balance angles of 52.15° for the up-type quarks and 48.06° for the down-type, against 45° for the leptons. These angles do not depend on the scale chosen, so they are the targets any theory of quark masses must meet. A simple pattern of link strengths (3/6, 4/6, 5/6 for leptons, down-type and up-type) misses the data by about seven standard deviations and is rejected. The relation √(md/ms) ≈ sin θC (Gatto, Sartori and Tonin, 1968) holds to 0.04%.

Quark-mass relations, tested strictly. Each relation contained in the particle matrices was tested with measured masses on both sides and judged against the measurement’s own error bar. A full Higgs coupling of the top quark (yt = 1; Hill, 1981) gives mt = 174.1 GeV against 172.60 ± 0.27 GeV (+0.9%, but 5.6σ). The 45° balance applied to the triples (c, b, t) and (s, c, b) (Rivero, 2011) gives mc = 1353 MeV against 1272.9 ± 4.5 MeV (+14.8σ) and ms = 84.5 MeV against 92.9 ± 0.7 MeV (−10σ); no standard definition of the charm mass rescues it. An earlier chain that started from the bottom mass alone fed its own high charm value into the strange step and so appeared to work to about 1%. The Cabibbo relation, in its sine form sin θC = √(md/ms), gives ms/md = 19.9 against 19.8 ± 0.3 from the measured masses (ms = 92.9 ± 0.7 MeV and md = 4.70 ± 0.07 MeV; Particle Data Group, 2026), +0.3σ, which passes; earlier versions compared it with a value of 20.4 ± 0.3 that has no published source. The tangent form gives 18.9 (−2.7σ); the sine form was chosen with both in view, so the form is a fitted choice (Appendix A.6). The up quark is reached through the decay η → 3π, in which electromagnetic effects nearly cancel (Sutherland, 1966; Bell and Sutherland, 1968). Leutwyler’s relation (mu/md)2 + (ms/md)2/Q2 = 1 (Leutwyler, 1996), with the Cabibbo value of ms/md and Q = 22.1 ± 0.7 from the measured decays (Colangelo, Lanz, Leutwyler and Passemar, 2018), gives mu/md = 0.437 ± 0.060 (measured 0.462 ± 0.020) and mu = 2.04 ± 0.28 MeV (measured 2.16 ± 0.07 MeV), with no lattice input. With the uncertainty of Q included, both lie 0.4σ from the measurement. Because Q is known to only 3%, the prediction is wide, so this is a loose test. The result also depends on the choice of Q: the lattice value Q = 23.4 ± 0.5 (Particle Data Group, 2026) gives mu/md = 0.53 ± 0.03 and mu = 2.47 ± 0.14 MeV, 2.0σ above the measurement, on the line between a pass and a partial (Appendix A.6). Each relation is published separately; linking them through the matrices is the contribution here. The 45° relations among the heavier quarks are numerical patterns good to 1–10%, not laws, so the bottom, charm and strange masses remain measured inputs.

Cone or spiral. Written as exact statements and tested at μ = 162.5 GeV, the lepton ladder is a cone, √mk ∝ 1 + √2 cos(δ + 2πk/3) with opening 45.000° (Koide, 1983; Foot, 1994; Brannen, 2006). The quark openings are 48.150° ± 0.015° (down-type) and 52.147° ± 0.018° (up-type); they differ from 45° by 213σ and 405σ and from each other by 174σ, and a single 45° cone for all six quarks (Kartavtsev, 2011) fails by 13σ. Two cones about a common axis would give zero mixing, so the CKM tilt measures the misalignment of the two cone axes. The spiral (geometric steps) fails in all five forms tested, including Froggatt–Nielsen powers of the Cabibbo parameter (Froggatt and Nielsen, 1979), whose fitted exponents (2.0, 2.7, 4.2, 3.7) are near but not at integers; the quarks are nevertheless much closer to equal logarithmic steps than the leptons. The down-type opening is scale-independent under QCD and remains a fixed value to explain. No quark mass moves.

Mutual pushes of quarks and leptons. Renormalization-group running (Gell-Mann and Low, 1954; Machacek and Vaughn, 1984) couples the two sectors in three ways. Through the Higgs, the top shifts every lepton Yukawa coupling by 1.67% per e-fold, the leptons shift the quarks by 6 × 10−7; this is flavour-universal and changes a ladder’s scale, not its shape. Within a weak doublet the top drives the bottom by −0.83% per e-fold with opposite sign, the route by which the two quark ladders bend each other. QED self-running separates the lightest and heaviest lepton by 2.84%, and direct photon exchange between sectors is 10−5 to 10−4. None closes a near miss: matching the lepton scale to mp/3 requires a common scale of about 1.3 GeV, where the cone opening is 45.05° rather than 45°; quark balance ratios move away from 5/4 and 5/3; and the 6.8% charm and 8.9% strength misses are far larger. The Koide relation holds for pole masses to 10−5 although QED running should spoil it by 0.35% (Sumino, 2009), so any quark rule must specify its scale before it is tested.

The three layers of mass. Every composite mass is a Higgs layer (the elementary masses), a containment layer (constituent masses and hadrons; Sakharov and Zeldovich, 1966; De Rújula, Georgi and Glashow, 1975; 8 light baryons from 3 fitted numbers to 0.56% rms) and a nuclear layer (the semi-empirical binding formula; Weizsäcker, 1935; Bethe and Bacher, 1936; 5 fitted numbers). Together they give the masses of 2,899 nuclides to 0.008% rms (AME2020; Wang et al., 2021). For 56Fe the layers are 595 MeV (1.1%), 52,001 MeV (99%) and −494 MeV (−492 MeV measured). The proton is about 9% quark mass, 32% quark kinetic energy, 36% gluon energy and 23% trace anomaly (Ji, 1995; Yang et al., 2018), so about 91% is containment energy. The structure holds; its numbers are fitted, and the bottom layer, the Higgs links, is open. Balance points of particle pairs fall on other particles’ masses at chance level.

Sizes and the proton’s home. The matrix of sizes Sij holds one number per particle. Five patterns in it fail chance tests, including whole powers of one parameter (Froggatt and Nielsen, 1979). Seen from the proton, each particle’s balance mass is mp/(1 + e−2S), a logistic curve (Verhulst, 1838): lighter particles at their own mass, heavier ones capped at the proton, the proton with itself at one half. This is the scale-view principle in exact form and moves no number.

Scaling backwards. Run at two loops from the top mass to the Planck energy (Machacek and Vaughn, 1984; Arason et al., 1992), the down-type opening moves from 48.15° to 47.72° and the up-type from 52.15° to 52.31°, while the leptons stay at 45.05°; neither quark ladder is a 45° cone at any energy, and mb/mτ reaches only 0.62. The top Yukawa coupling, run down from large values (Pendleton and Ross, 1981; Hill, 1981), lands at 1.28–1.32 against 0.937: right in kind, 36–41% high. A rule for the elementary masses must act on ratios, which do not run.

8. Quantum structure

Entanglement. Measured entangled pairs show the correlation E(a, b) = −cos(a − b), stronger than any local hidden-variable model allows (Bell). In the relational picture the two particles are one structure seen from two places. This is the content of the ER = EPR proposal of Maldacena and Susskind, and no signal travels faster than light.

Uncertainty. Any wave localised to a width Δx has a spread in wavenumber Δk with Δx·Δk ≥ ½. Heisenberg’s relation is this property of waves, applied to matter.

Charges. For one generation with N colours, the four anomaly-cancellation conditions fix the hypercharges uniquely. The up-quark charge is ½ + 1/(2N) and the down-quark charge is −½ + 1/(2N). For N = 3 these are +2/3 and −1/3. Anomalies do not fix N; it is measured through the neutral-pion decay rate and the ratio of hadron to muon production in electron–positron collisions, and it follows from the inner algebra below.

Forces, colours and generations. The measured carrier counts (eight carriers with two neutral, and four with two neutral) allow only SU(3) and SU(2) × U(1) in the classification of Lie algebras (Killing; Cartan), and the global form S(U(3) × U(2)) passes the ℤ6 check for every field. A medium with no gaps has an inner algebra without zero divisors, and relationships that keep their size when combined require a conserved norm; by Hurwitz’s theorem (1898) only ℝ, ℂ, ℍ and 𝕆 qualify. A force richer than the weak force requires 𝕆, whose symmetry group G2 leaves SU(3) when any one direction is fixed, as every observation fixes one; the remaining six directions form three complex ones, which are the three colours. ℍ acting from the left and from the right carries SU(2)L × SU(2)R, but only under a different meaning of symmetry from the one that gives colour: on the number systems alone no single meaning yields the strong, weak and hypercharge carriers together, and constructions that join ℂ, ℍ and 𝕆 into one algebra (Dixon, 1994; Furey, 2016) reintroduce zero divisors, which the no-gaps rule forbids. The strong force is therefore derived. Read two at a time, the weak force, hypercharge and left-handedness would be taken from measurement; on the main line, with the relationship matrix read three at a time from one perspective (Section 8), they follow with one stated reading, and only the strengths are measured. The minimal particle content also needs at least one further Higgs field to match the measured masses (Georgi and Jarlskog, 1979). Hermitian octonionic matrices satisfy the Jordan identity only up to 3 × 3 (Jordan, von Neumann and Wigner, 1934), while an irremovable CP-violating phase needs at least three generations (Kobayashi and Maskawa, 1973), so on this picture the number of generations is exactly three. These steps rest on an interpretive identification of the physical picture with algebraic rules. That forces are local symmetries (Weyl; Yang and Mills) follows from relativity and quantum mechanics, which the framework inherits. Completed with the 4 × 4 group of Pati and Salam, this route is kept on record as the alternative picture; the main line is the relationship-matrix picture below.

Mass as a balance point. Mass is the energy at which containment and leakage balance. For the proton this gives a mass of four times the containment energy, about 99% of the proton’s mass when only the up and down quark masses are counted as Higgs mass; counting the strange quark the Higgs share is about 9%, and with the heavy quarks’ indirect effect about 29% (Shifman, Vainshtein and Zakharov, 1978); the rest is set by one Higgs balance point (246.22 GeV), and one containment pressure (B = 60.1 MeV/fm3) serves both the proton and the compact star. Fission is a shift of the balance (166 MeV against 173.3 MeV measured). The shape of each force follows from what space does to its field: spreading (1/r2), confinement in a tube (constant pull), or screening (short reach); the nuclear force is shared leakage carried by pions. As strict numbers the balance falls short: the pull between quarks is 0.155 against 0.18–0.19 GeV2, and the deconfinement temperature 105 against 156.5 ± 1.5 MeV. It does not give elementary-particle masses, the strengths of the forces, or which forces exist.

The main picture of the forces: the relationship matrix. Where gravity emerges from entanglement, an exact symmetry must come with a force (Harlow and Ooguri, 2019; proven for spaces with a boundary), so forces are expected rather than added. Treating matter itself as motion in an inner space fails: a charged particle would weigh about 5 × 1017 GeV, and a smooth inner space gives no left-handed matter (Witten, 1983). The 3 × 3 Hermitian octonionic matrix, read as the relationship matrix seen by an observer who stands at one point and faces one way, has exactly 12 carriers, 4 neutral, in blocks 8 | 3 | 1, with one meaning of symmetry and no zero divisors (Todorov and Dubois-Violette, 2018; Dubois-Violette and Todorov, 2019; Krasnov, 2019; Baez, 2020–21; reproduced here). The weak pairs carry hypercharges in the ratio 3 : 1, and anomaly cancellation then fixes their partners at +2/3, −1/3 and −1. The matrix lacks exactly two of the partners a whole generation needs, those of the up quark and the electron (four states, one octonion seen from one perspective), and these partners and the strengths are one problem. Counting only what the matrix holds, one common strength predicts sin2θW = 0.75 where the forces meet, against about 0.43 from the measured values; counting the whole generation (Chamseddine and Connes, 1996) gives 0.375, and, run to the Z mass at two loops, 0.2107 against 0.2312, 8.9% off, the near miss of Georgi, Quinn and Weinberg (1974) and still a fail (Georgi and Glashow, 1974; Baez and Huerta, 2010). The strengths therefore remain inputs. Four homes for the missing partners built from two-way combinations (relationships between two links, the matrix’s other turns, the doubled link space and a 4 × 4 matrix, which is not a consistent algebra) all fail, and the three-way reading below supplies them; the generations cannot be slots inside the same matrix, so they are taken as three placements of the one shape, leaving “at least three” as a theorem and “at most three” resting on the nothing-extra rule and measurement; and a right-handed force cannot coexist with it, which removes the left–right scale and leaves about 0.4 of the observed matter excess. On this picture handedness is the side the observer faces: with the no-gaps rule applied to spin (a second added reading), mirror-image partners would be a gap, so matter must be one-handed, and anomaly consistency leaves the measured arrangement (Lee and Yang, 1956; Wu, 1957). The lepton 45° balance holds to 15 parts in a million and follows, for leptons only, from the family symmetry and maximum uncertainty (Section 10), and the twist and the electron’s mass are one open number. Any new force carrier would exclude it. Which forces exist is not a choice within this picture. Take the perspective principle as one place of the matrix kept and one imaginary direction kept. The symmetry group of the place is Spin(9), acting on the 16 real numbers that link it to the other two, and the part of Spin(9) that respects the direction is exactly 12-dimensional, 11 + 1, where 11 can only be 8 + 3; the result is the same for every direction (computed here for all seven basis directions and a random one). The eight complex link states carry the remaining charge in the ratio 3 : 1 with multiplicities 6 and 2, a quark pair in three colours and a lepton pair (Günaydin and Gürsey, 1973; Todorov and Dubois-Violette, 2018; Krasnov, 2019). The three forces therefore follow from the perspective principle by a theorem. What the theorem does not give are the strengths and the missing partners. This picture is carried as the main line, with the 4 × 4 picture kept on record as the alternative.

A relationship among three. The first principle is read at two levels: two at a time for the numbers in the matrix, which forces the octonions, and three at a time for the states built from them, so that nothing combined with its own mirror image and itself gives nothing. The three-way product {x, y, z}, with the middle state entering as its mirror image (Jordan, 1933; Loos, 1977), has no zero divisors of this kind on the matrix’s link states; the remaining two-way ones, “the same state twice gives nothing”, are for matter the exclusion principle (Pauli, 1925). With the perspective read as the spot kept exactly and the facing kept as a direction (overall phase left out), the turns of the three-way product that respect it number exactly 12, four neutral, in blocks 8 | 3 | 1, and the 16 link states are a whole generation with the measured charges: quark pair +1/6, lepton pair −1/2, down partner +1/3, up partner −2/3, electron partner +1 and neutral partner 0. All anomaly sums vanish, no mirror states exist, and the two states picked by the perspective are the neutral and electron partners. This is the whole-generation pattern of Georgi (1975) and Fritzsch and Minkowski (1975), without their extra carriers. Other readings give 11, 13 or 14 turns or inconsistent charges, so the reading is an added step; applied to ℂ and ℍ it gives 3 and 5 kept turns, and only 𝕆 gives 8 | 3 | 1. Only 9 of the 12 turns coincide with the two-way count, so the weak force is re-identified: half the states are weak pairs and half singles, as measured, replacing the two-way reading above, under which every state was a pair. The strengths are unchanged: the geometry gives the whole-generation count, 0.375 with strong equal to weak, 8.9% off at the Z mass at two loops. Charges as literal bound sets of three (Harari, 1979; Shupe, 1979) fail; the count of colour switches holds as a pattern (Georgi and Glashow, 1974; Furey, 2016). The framework adopts this reading.

Perspective in place of handedness. The three-way structure has exactly one i: only 1 and the facing direction commute with all 46 of its turns, so every state takes its screw sense from it and the 16 states cannot be wound independently. The perspective rule reads: one spot, one facing, one i throughout. Reversing the facing (i → −i) exchanges matter with antimatter and left with right together, and the charge list of the 16 states differs from its mirror image, so the world is not mirror-symmetric (Weyl, 1929; Lee and Yang, 1956; Wu, 1957). A separate i for spin would double every state into mirror pairs, excluded by the nothing-extra rule; the earlier reading that spin uses the same i becomes part of the perspective rule. Mass is the link between a paired and a single state through the far side of the matrix, whose 10 parts carry the numbers of a colourless weak pair of charge ±1/2 (the Higgs field’s numbers) and six coloured parts (Freudenthal, 1954). These numbers appear twice, as in the standard E6 construction (Gürsey, Ramond and Sikivie, 1976): as spin-½ matter in each generation’s 27 (the far-side weak pairs and D states of Sections 9 and 10, each with both handednesses, so not mirror families), and once as the spin-0 Higgs doublet that links paired to single states. Only the colourless part of the spin-0 copy is a particle; its coloured spin-0 parts must be read as matrix entries rather than particles (Dimopoulos and Georgi, 1981), and why is not derived (the doublet–triplet problem). Each state has exactly one mass partner, but the four links come out equal, so their sizes are not given. The same link gives no quark mixing, the right starting point, but not the small measured tilt.

The forces that build the atom. The largest set of the 12 turns under which the 16 states are their own mirror image is unique: the 8 strong turns plus one mixture of the single turn with a weak turn, which is electric charge, the two mirror-symmetric forces that bind (Vafa and Witten, 1984). Settling the colourless far-side doublet keeps exactly these 9 turns, the other three becoming W± and Z; this is one statement rather than two, since a mass link maps the world onto its mirror image, and that the far side settles, in the direction keeping most turns (Michel and Radicati, 1971), is not derived. The kept charges give proton +1, neutron 0 and hydrogen 0 exactly (measured to 10−21; Bressi et al., 2011). The state count gives the direction of running: colour confined near the proton scale, the weak force hidden 26 orders of magnitude before it would confine, electric charge free. The Higgs link is a Yukawa force whose charge is mass (Yukawa, 1935), the link that gives mass rather than the third binding force, which is B − L (below), with range 1/534 of the proton radius; it binds nothing and is not gravity, since universality of free fall holds to a few parts in 1015 (MICROSCOPE, 2022: η = (−1.5 ± 2.3 ± 1.5) × 10−15) while the Higgs charge per unit mass is 1 for the electron and about 0.29 for the proton. A Higgs coupling to all energy would raise the LHC Higgs rate about 130-fold (ATLAS and CMS, 2022). With two settled directions, a complex far side allows a free top-to-bottom ratio (Lee, 1973), but bottom = τ still fails. The whole matrix read three-way has exactly three independent sizes and no larger such matrix exists (Jordan, von Neumann and Wigner, 1934; Albert, 1934), a hint for three generations but not a derivation, since one matrix cannot hold both forces and generations.

The energy the Higgs does not give, and matter number. Under the 12 turns exactly one Higgs-free mass term is allowed: the hidden neutrino partner with itself, the state that the perspective rule keeps exactly and that therefore carries no charge. With no perspective, or with the spot held only as a direction, there is none. This is the Majorana mass of the seesaw (Majorana, 1937; Minkowski, 1977; Yanagida, 1979; Gell-Mann, Ramond and Slansky, 1979); its size is an input. Keeping the spot exactly breaks one further generator, a 13th commuting with the 12, whose charges are +1/3 on quarks and −1 on leptons: B − L (Davidson, 1979; Marshak and Mohapatra, 1980). Strong, electric and B − L form the largest facing-blind subset of the 13. The far side settling breaks the weak generators and gives the Dirac masses; the spot breaks B − L and gives the Majorana mass; together they leave the 9 of SU(3) × U(1)em. Two readings are possible: (ii) B − L is gauged above about 1011 GeV, with fifth-force limits (Wagner et al., 2012) requiring a heavy carrier; or (i) B − L is never a force. The main line adopts (i); (ii) is recorded as the alternative. Because gravity allows no exact global symmetry (Banks and Seiberg, 2011; Harlow and Ooguri, 2019), reading (i) keeps the spot at every scale except the grain of space: B − L is broken there, very slightly, and that small breaking is what gives the majoron its mass (Akhmedov, Berezhiani, Mohapatra and Senjanović, 1993; Rothstein, Babu and Seckel, 1993). In reading (i) the phase of the field that settles the Majorana mass survives as a massless or nearly massless Goldstone boson, the majoron (Chikashige, Mohapatra and Peccei, 1981), which is part of a piece the framework already has rather than an extra state. The Higgs-free mass does not separate the generations (no such term touches a charged state; a common value gives m2/m3 = 0.0035 against 0.172), and a generation-blind Higgs fails the LHC muon-to-tau comparison by about 68σ (ATLAS and CMS, 2022). The Higgs value, G and the cosmological constant are all properties of space rather than turns, but the Higgs value melts above 159.5 GeV (D’Onofrio and Rummukainen, 2016) and trial relations among them miss by factors of 90 to 450. The matrix supplies a ℤ2, (−1)3(B−L), under which all 16 states are odd and all 10 far-side parts even (Krauss and Wilczek, 1989). Among the 16 it protects only the lightest neutrino; the relic neutrinos together (59 meV in all) give 0.5% of the dark matter (analysis/third_energy_tests_2026-11-14.py), and a protected dark state would need a kind of particle the matrix does not have (Kadastik, Kannike and Raidal, 2010). For the far-side parts the sign cannot be exact: if it were, the lightest neutral far-side weak state would be stable, and a stable state of that kind is excluded by direct-detection searches by about two orders of magnitude; it would also forbid the mixing with ordinary matter through which the far-side parts decay. The sign is therefore approximate there, and no prediction rests on it. The one dark matter candidate the matrix allows is the majoron, as a lead. With the settling scale near the heavy partners (about 1011 to 1012 GeV), its lifetime is at least 1025 s; below about 37 eV it behaves as wave dark matter (Hu, Barkana and Gruzinov, 2000; Hui et al., 2017), allowed above about 2 × 10−20 eV (Rogers and Peiris, 2021). Its mass and abundance are not derived (Berezinsky and Valle, 1993; Garcia-Cely and Heeck, 2017). Produced by misalignment (Preskill, Wise and Wilczek, 1983; Abbott and Sikivie, 1983; Dine and Fischler, 1983) after a start colder than the settling value f (the lightest partners may share the heat, but majorons made by their pairs are suppressed by about (MN/f)2), it is all of the dark matter when m_J = 0.027 eV × (1.5 × 1012 GeV ÷ f)4 ÷ angle4 (equivalently 8.8 keV × (6.3 × 1010 GeV ÷ f)4 ÷ angle4), where f is the settling value and the angle is where the field started, so its mass is not fixed, and it is cold. Because the field settled before the start, the start’s own fluctuations are imprinted on it as fluctuations in dark matter uncorrelated with the rest; the CMB limit on these (Planck Collaboration, 2020, Constraints on inflation; fraction below 0.038) caps the start’s expansion rate, which leaves primordial gravitational waves at r ≲ 0.08 (f × angle ÷ M̄P)2, with M̄P the reduced Planck mass, below about 10−11 for any settling value used here; this needs B − L to stay broken through the start and afterwards (f above both the start’s expansion rate ÷ 2π and the reheating temperature) (Fox, Pierce and Thomas, 2004; Hertzberg, Tegmark and Wilczek, 2008). This is the origin of the gravitational-wave test in Appendix A.4, and it belongs to the majoron lead; a hot start leaves about 73% of it hot, which fails. A non-thermal start that hands its energy directly to the heavy partners (Lazarides and Shafi, 1991) avoids this and keeps leptogenesis, at the cost of one number. With the alternative start phase (Section 5), the expansion rate during the start, 1.6 × 1013 GeV, divided by 2π, exceeds every settling value used here, so the majoron’s starting angle is averaged rather than chosen: there are no separate dark-matter ripples and the angle is no longer a start condition. The bound r ≲ 10−11 above then applies only to the cold start, which is the main line; the alternative predicts r = 0.0040. Keeping the majoron cold needs the heat after the start below about 1.6 × 109 GeV, which the Higgs–curvature coupling ξ ≈ 0.12–0.15 gives; whether hot majorons from the partners stay under a few per cent at that heat has not been recalculated.

Neutrinos and the matter–antimatter excess. If the neutrino shares the relation matrix of the charged leptons, its Dirac link is the charged-lepton mass matrix, and the seesaw mechanism (Minkowski; Yanagida; Gell-Mann, Ramond and Slansky; Glashow; Mohapatra and Senjanović) fixes the heavy partners’ mass matrix as MN = −D mν−1 D, with D = diag(me, mμ, mτ) and mν built from the light masses and the measured mixing. Computed as the full 3 × 3 matrix with TM1 mixing, the partners weigh 0.6 to 1.9 × 105, 2.2 to 6.2 × 108 and 1.3 to 1.65 × 1012 GeV across θ23 and the unmeasured phases; earlier drafts quoted 7.3 × 105, 1.3 × 109 and 6.3 × 1010 GeV from the ratio mℓ2/mν, which holds only without mixing. The heaviest then needs a right-handed coupling of about 3 for left–right breaking at 1011.7 GeV, the scale at which the three measured force strengths unify in the minimal Pati–Salam model (proton lifetime about 1036 years); that is near the limit where the calculation stays reliable (about √(4π) ≈ 3.5), so the tie to Pati–Salam is strained rather than comfortable. Flavoured leptogenesis from these partners (Fukugita and Yanagida, 1986), solved with the full rate equations (Buchmüller, Di Bari and Plümacher; Blanchet and Di Bari; Nardi, Nir, Roulet and Racker), does not predict the observed baryon asymmetry of 6.1 × 10−10: with the shared-matrix rule read literally (this calculation always used the full matrix, so it is unchanged) and only the unmeasured phases free, the largest value found is about 1.5 × 10−13, some 4,000 times short. One narrow region survives every test tried and allows the observed value: δ ≈ 274°, consistent with current neutrino data, a hot start within a window of about five in temperature (capped by magnetic monopoles; Zeldovich and Khlopov, 1978; Preskill, 1979), and a lightest partner almost unlinked to the electron. The same shared-matrix rule fails for quark pairs (mt/mb = 41), so it is stated for leptons only.

Lifetimes. Lepton universality, written relationally, gives the tau lifetime from the muon’s as ττ = τμ · BR · e−10S. This equals 289.7 fs, against 290.3 fs measured.

Carrier and energy matrices. Every interaction joins two matter particles through one carrier. Written as matrices, the photon, Z, W, gluon and Higgs couplings reproduce the measured charges, and the main line predicts ρ = mW2/(mZ2 cos2θW) = 1 exactly, because the far side holds exactly one weak doublet of hypercharge ½ (Veltman, 1977); measured, 1.0003 ± 0.0005 after known corrections. Veltman’s condition for cancelling quadratic divergences (Veltman, 1981) needs a 313 GeV Higgs, texture zeros (Fritzsch, 1977) miss the mixing, and equal entries miss: the open masses are the Higgs-matrix entries and the tilt is the W matrix. The pair link value (m1 + m2)/√(m1m2) is exactly the inverse reduced-mass factor, so the binding energies of hydrogen (13.598 eV), positronium (6.803 eV), muonium (13.540 eV) and muonic hydrogen (2.528 keV) are reproduced, as in Bohr’s treatment (Bohr, 1913) with the reduced mass; this is a reformulation, not an independent test. The same matrices give the top width (1.35 GeV against 1.42 (+0.19 −0.15) GeV) and light hadron masses to a few per cent with five fitted numbers (De Rújula, Georgi and Glashow, 1975). Gravity written as a perspective fails in matrix form; for every elementary pair the Higgs-to-gravity force ratio is 1.96 × 1032. The Higgs cannot be a top-quark condensate (Bardeen, Hill and Lindner, 1990).

9. Predictions

Awaiting measurement. Each result in this section follows within the framework but rests on a measurement that has not yet returned. None is counted as established until it does; the deciding measurement is named with each.

The strong coupling (awaiting measurement). The force strengths meet at 4.6 × 1013 GeV if the three colourless far-side weak pairs of the relationship matrix sit at 2.4 × 108 GeV (Section 10). At three loops this requires αs(MZ) = 0.1178, with the grain of space fixed in advance as the Planck energy divided by √2. The grain was fixed on 17 November 2026, before the deciding measurement. It, and the form of the rule for the pairs’ weight, were chosen with today’s data in view (fitted, not predicted), so only later measurements test the prediction; the unreduced Planck energy would have given 0.1196, which is not the framework’s value. The world average is 0.1180 ± 0.0009; the most precise single determination is 0.11873 ± 0.00056 (Dalla Brida and others, 2026), which lies 1.7 standard deviations above the predicted 0.1178 and within one standard deviation of the world average and does not yet decide. Lattice determinations at about 0.3% will. An observed proton decay would also falsify this part.

The Higgs mass (awaiting measurement). The framework’s balance of containment, applied at the grain of space, says that the Higgs holds itself together down to the grain and no further, so its self-coupling vanishes there. This is the rule of Shaposhnikov and Wetterich (2010), which assumes that gravity stays well behaved at the grain (Weinberg, 1979; Reuter, 1998). Run at two loops (Machacek and Vaughn, 1984; Luo, Wang and Xiao, 2003) from the starting values of Buttazzo et al. (2013), the Standard Model’s states alone give mH = 128.84 ± 1.45 GeV, which is 2.6 standard deviations above the measured 125.13 ± 0.11 GeV (Particle Data Group, 2026). With the far-side states of the relationship matrix included, three colourless weak pairs at 2.4 × 108 GeV and their three coloured partners at 4.6 × 1013 GeV, both fixed beforehand by the force strengths and not adjusted here, the result is 127.3 ± 1.5 GeV, within 1.4 standard deviations. Estimating the three-loop step from the Standard Model’s own two-to-three-loop shift (Buttazzo et al., 2013), a step decided before it was run and accepted whichever way it went, moves this by −0.1 GeV, to 127.2 ± 1.5 GeV, and changes no verdict (analysis/higgs_three_loop_check_2026-11-18.py). This is an estimate: the far-side states enter only above 2.4 × 108 GeV, while the shift comes mostly from the top-quark and strong-force matching near the weak scale, but a full three-loop run that includes the far-side states has not been done. The test and its pass line were fixed before it was run. The Higgs mass therefore leaves the list of measured inputs, while the Higgs value stays measured. This is not counted as established until measurement returns. The rule needs a top pole mass of 171.6 GeV against 172.60 ± 0.27 GeV measured (Particle Data Group, 2026); the quoted error allows 0.5 GeV for the ambiguity in what the measured top mass means, and without that allowance the gap is 2.1 standard deviations, just outside the pass line of 2 set before the test, so the pass rests on that allowance; on the top mass alone (171.6 needed against 172.60 ± 0.27 measured) it is 3.7. A sharper top mass or strong coupling that pushes the gap beyond 3 standard deviations would falsify it. Any extra weak matter at high energy would help in the same way, so the agreement does not single out this framework.

No proton decay. In the 27 states of each generation (Gürsey, Ramond and Sikivie, 1976; Slansky, 1981) every charge is exact and all five anomaly sums vanish. With the framework’s content, the twelve known carriers, one Higgs pair, the majoron and no coloured spin-0 states, no renormalisable interaction changes baryon number, since that takes three quarks at once (Weinberg, 1979b; Wilczek and Zee, 1979). The only remaining source is the grain of space itself, which gives a lifetime near 1044 years against the measured floor of 2.4 × 1034 years (Takenaka et al., 2020). The far side’s spin-½ D states do not change this: with only a colourless spin-0 field, every renormalisable term they enter (their mass, their mass link to the ordinary down quarks and their Higgs link) keeps baryon number when D carries 1/3, as d does. The statement rests on the nothing-extra rule: coloured spin-0 partners of the Higgs would bring the decay back.

Quark mixing stays unitary. The only states that mix with known quarks are the D states, at 4.6 × 1013 GeV. Their mixing with right-handed down quarks causes no flavour-changing Z couplings at tree level, since both carry the same charges, and their mixing with left-handed quarks is suppressed by (vH/MD)2 ≈ 10−23. The first row of the quark-mixing matrix therefore sums to one: |Vud|2 + |Vus|2 + |Vub|2 = 1 to far better than any measurement. The present shortfall, 0.0016 ± 0.0007 (2.3 standard deviations; Particle Data Group, 2026; the Cabibbo-angle anomaly), must shrink to nothing as nuclear and radiative corrections improve; a confirmed shortfall would falsify the far-side reading.

Neutrino masses. The same relational rule, applied to neutrinos with the two measured mass splittings, gives, with today’s limit from cosmology, normal ordering. With JUNO’s 2026 splittings (Δm221 = 7.388 × 10−5, Δm231 = 2.509 × 10−3 eV2) the masses are m1 = 0.36 meV, m2 = 8.6 meV and m3 = 50.1 meV, summing to 0.0591 eV; with the global fit NuFIT 6.0 (including atmospheric data) m2 = 8.7 meV, m3 = 50.3 meV and the sum is 0.0594 eV. Earlier versions quoted 8.7 meV and 0.0592 eV, from the global fit without atmospheric data; the change in v1.3.3 is only the input. The rule also has an inverted solution, with a sum of 0.103 eV; it is disfavoured by the current cosmological bound, not by the rule itself. The sum lies just above the minimum normal ordering allows (0.0588 eV), so the sharpest number is the lightest mass. JUNO’s first ordering analysis favours normal ordering at 2.1σ (JUNO Collaboration, 2026), not yet decisive; it will reach 3σ within a few years, and cosmological surveys are approaching the required sensitivity to the sum. Inverted ordering, or a sum clearly different from 0.059 eV, would falsify this part of the framework.

Neutrino mixing (conditional). Exact tribimaximal (Harrison, Perkins and Scott, 2002) and trimaximal mixing are excluded by the data, while the TM1 pattern (Xing and Zhou, 2014; Albright and Rodejohann, 2009) gives sin2θ12 = 0.318. That agreed with the global fits within 1σ, but JUNO’s first precision measurement gives 0.3092 ± 0.0087 from its first 59 days (JUNO Collaboration, 2025) and 0.3036 ± 0.0064 with more data (JUNO Collaboration, 2026), which puts TM1 2.3 standard deviations high. This is not yet a failure, which needs 3σ, but it is the sharpest pressure on the framework today: if JUNO’s next data keep this central value, TM1 fails, and with it the savings of θ12 and the neutrino CP phase (Appendix A.2) and the phase prediction below. Awaiting measurement: not 100% until JUNO’s next release. If the neutrino flavours are perspectives on one relational structure of this kind, the CP-violating phase lies near 270°, close to maximal CP violation; current global fits weakly prefer 180° to 215°, and DUNE and Hyper-Kamiokande will decide.

Quark pairing in compact stars. With the containment pressure set by the proton, compact stars require a quark pairing gap of about 82 to 97 MeV for a strange-quark mass of 100 MeV (75 to 108 MeV across the proton’s range of B); what they fix is 4Δ2 − ms2 ≈ 1.7 to 2.8 × 104 MeV2, which gives 88 to 109 MeV for a running strange mass of 120 to 140 MeV. Theoretical estimates span 50 to 150 MeV, and the gap has not been measured. A firm determination outside this band, from lattice or effective-theory calculations, or from compact-star cooling and merger signals, would falsify this part. The range of 2.0 to 2.15 solar masses is the observed window that the gap must respect, not a separate prediction. A star clearly above about 2.15 solar masses would break this part; PSR J0952−0607, at 2.35 ± 0.17 solar masses (Romani and others, 2022), and the 2.6 solar-mass object in GW190814 (Abbott and others, 2020) are the cases to watch.

Neutrinoless double beta decay. The Higgs-free mass of Section 8 exists only if neutrinos are Majorana particles (Majorana, 1937). Neutrinoless double beta decay (Furry, 1939) then occurs with an effective mass of 1.3 to 4.1 meV with TM1 mixing and both Majorana phases free (1.3 to 4.0 with JUNO’s splittings, 1.4 to 4.1 with the global fit; 1.8 to 2.3 meV if the relational matrix fixes them at zero, a reading not yet established), below the present limit of 28 to 122 meV (KamLAND-Zen, 2024) and below the 10 to 20 meV reach of planned experiments. Proof that neutrinos are Dirac particles, or an effective mass clearly outside this range, would falsify it.

Primordial gravitational waves and the tilt of the first ripples (alternative start; awaiting measurement). On the main line no primordial gravitational waves are expected at a measurable level (r ≲ 10−11 within the majoron lead), and the tilt is a measured input. The alternative start phase (Section 5) predicts r = 0.0040 ± 0.0003 for N = 54–55, below today’s limit r < 0.036 (BICEP/Keck, 2021). Simons Observatory and LiteBIRD reach about 0.003 and 0.001 in the early 2030s; not seeing primordial gravitational waves at that level would falsify the start phase. The tilt is ns = 0.9630–0.9636. Against published constraints (McDonough and Ferreira, 2025): Planck (PR4) 0.9638 ± 0.0040 (−0.1σ); Planck (PR4) with DESI baryon acoustic oscillations 0.9690 ± 0.0035 (−1.6σ); Planck, ACT and SPT combined 0.9693 ± 0.0029 (−2.1σ); ACT DR6 with Planck and DESI 0.9743 ± 0.0034 (−3.2σ; ACT Collaboration, 2025); Planck, ACT and SPT with DESI 0.9737 ± 0.0025 (−4.2σ). It therefore agrees with the CMB alone and fails once DESI is combined; that shift is driven by a disagreement of about 2σ between DESI and the CMB (McDonough and Ferreira, 2025), which these combinations assume away. This is why the stretching phase is the alternative and not the main line: a CMB-only tilt above about 0.972 at 3σ, or DESI and the CMB brought into agreement at the higher value, would falsify the start phase in this form (Bezerra-Sobrinho and Medeiros, 2025, discuss the same tension). Not 100% until those measurements return.

Unmeasured values. The carrier and energy matrices give values not yet measured: the Higgs self-couplings (three-point 191 GeV, four-point 0.776), the HHWW coupling (0.213), Higgs couplings to the charm quark, the electron and the light quarks equal to m/246 GeV, and the binding energies of true muonium (1.407 keV), tauonium (23.66 keV) and the proton–tau atom (16.35 keV). Most are standard physics in the framework’s form, so they test its bookkeeping rather than new physics. The dark matter candidate is a cold, chargeless spin-0 wave decaying only to neutrinos (its mass is not fixed); dark matter that feels the weak force would falsify it.

10. Open questions

Quark masses. Of the relations in Section 7, only the down and up links pass strict tests; the bottom, charm and strange masses are measured inputs. The up-quark step uses no lattice QCD, but its value of Q comes from a dispersion analysis of measured decays, whose ±3% uncertainty dominates. Every relation tried for the bottom mass fails, including b = τ at the left–right scale (26% off) and the 45° colour rule, which misses charm by 15–19σ. The scale-free balance angles of Section 7 remain the targets (the down-type cone opening is 48.15° ± 0.015° with current lattice masses); the cone fits the leptons only, and the spiral fits no ladder exactly. Quark mixing belongs with these masses: the simplest mass link gives none, and the small measured tilt is not produced. The strongest lead is a Schur (Feshbach) fold of one heavy far-side colour-triplet state of charge −1/3 (the D quark of the 27 of E6; Gürsey, Ramond and Sikivie, 1976) along the TM1 direction (−1, −1, 2) (Harrison, Perkins and Scott, 2002; Schur, 1917; Feshbach, 1958): with one fitted strength all three mixing angles come within 19%, and 2% of random directions do as well. The direction deepens the ladder’s existing break; the 19% excess is far outside the errors, so it is a lead, not a result.

Force groups (awaiting measurement). Section 8 obtains the strong force and three colours from the full-medium rules (building on Hurwitz; Günaydin and Gürsey; Dixon; Furey; Connes and Chamseddine). In the main picture, the relationship matrix read three at a time from one perspective holds every particle of a generation with its measured charge, the measured carrier counts, the weak hypercharges and one-handed matter, at the cost of stated readings; the 4 × 4 picture, in which the weak force, hypercharge and handedness had to be taken from measurement, is kept on record as the alternative. Only the force strengths remain under this heading. With the standard two-loop method the Standard Model alone misses the measured weak mixing by −8.9% ± 0.1%. The gap closes if the three colourless far-side weak pairs of the matrix (spin ½, no colour, one per generation) sit at 2.2 × 108 GeV, with the coloured far-side states at the meeting, 4.6 × 1013 GeV; the doublet–triplet split is the known one (Dimopoulos and Wilczek, 1981). These states add nothing to the matrix. Their weight is the meeting energy squared divided by the grain of space, 2.4 × 108 GeV, close to the 2.2 × 108 GeV the strengths require. This rule was written down after the required weight was known, so the agreement is not evidence; it is one of the fitted choices (Appendix A.2, A.6). Stated exactly, the bet is one assumption: the weak pairs have no direct link to the background value that weighs their coloured partners, only the gravitational one. A single shared E6 coupling would weigh both alike, put the pairs at the meeting energy and fail to close the strengths; the two couplings are not tied together here because the forces never merge into one group (Masiero, Nanopoulos, Tamvakis and Yanagida, 1982). This is assumed, not derived. They cannot be stable, since any produced during reheating would exceed the dark matter many times over (Chung, Kolb and Riotto, 1999; Giudice, Kolb and Riotto, 2001); they decay through mixing with the ordinary lepton doublets, the colourless counterpart of the mixing that tilts the down quarks, which adds one small coupling (about 4 × 10−16) to the count. If the universe never reheated above 2.4 × 108 GeV, as the majoron lead requires, none were made and that coupling is not needed. With the alternative start phase the heat reaches about 0.5–5 × 109 GeV, so they would be made, and the coupling would be required: it must exceed about 9 × 10−12 so that they decay (within about 10−9 s) before they would dominate near 23 GeV and thin out the matter excess (analysis/start_phase_starobinsky_2026-11-19.py). Not derived: the meeting energy itself, the grain factor, and gravity’s strength. The test is the strong coupling (Section 9). The meeting is a crossing, not a merging. Computed at two loops, the three strengths agree at 4.6 × 1013 GeV to 0.02 in 1/α and then separate again; at the grain of space they are 27, 41 and 49. No placement of the far-side states makes them equal at the grain (the smallest spread found is 17 in 1/α). In a unified group the equality would follow from the merging (Georgi and Glashow, 1974). The framework has no merged group, so it needs a boundary condition instead: the three strengths are equal where each generation’s 27 becomes complete, that is, where the coloured far-side states enter. This is the second assumption inside the bet, alongside the weight rule, and it is not derived; the prediction αs = 0.1178 rests on both. This criterion stays partly met until that measurement returns.

The twist. The measured twist, 0.222222 to about one part in a million, matches 2/9 but is not derived. It cannot be exactly 2/9 if the 45° balance is also exact, since the two together miss the electron-to-muon mass ratio by 10 parts per million; reading 2/9 as a fraction of a ring is not an explanation. Rules that reproduce it for the leptons (δ = Q/3 and related forms) fail for both quark families.

The 45° balance, derived for leptons. Two of the framework’s principles give it. Three placements of one shape (Section 8) mean the family matrix of amplitudes, the square roots of the masses, is unchanged by a turn of 120°, which makes it circulant, with a the tie to itself and b the ties to the others (Brannen, 2006). Maximum uncertainty between self and relationship means the self share of the matrix’s weight, p = 3a2/(3a2 + 6|b|2), is ½; every symmetric measure of uncertainty peaks there (Shannon, 1948). And p = ½ is exactly |b|2 = a2/2, which is Koide’s Q = 2/3 and the 45° angle (Koide, 1983; Foot, 1994). The charged leptons measure p = 0.50000, and the tau mass follows at 1776.969 MeV, +0.4σ against 1776.93 ± 0.09 MeV (PDG 2024). The rule holds for the pole masses to about 10−5, although running from a high scale should spoil it by 0.35% (Sumino, 2009); the framework does not explain this. For the neutrinos p = 0.5004 with the lightest amplitude negative, which is a consistency check only, because their masses were built from the rule. For the quarks p = 0.456 (down-type) and 0.393 (up-type), and no choice of signs reaches ½, so the principle as stated does not hold for them; colour is the named difference and why it changes the balance is open. Maximum uncertainty is itself a starting principle of the framework and is not derived from anything deeper. The matter–antimatter excess. Not predicted; allowed in one narrow region (Section 8). With majoron dark matter the hot start that region needs fails, so the excess is carried by non-thermal leptogenesis (Lazarides and Shafi, 1991), with one number; the field that settles the Majorana mass cannot itself be that start. The cold start squeezes the numbers. The literal partners, the lightest near 105 GeV, cannot make the excess from the lightest one’s decays, whose asymmetry is capped near 10−11 (Davidson and Ibarra, 2002). In the region that works the perspective is tilted, the partners are near 3.6 × 104, 1.7 × 1010 and 1011 GeV, the source is the middle one, and the lightest one’s later erasing is included. The start must hand about 0.2% of its energy to that partner and reheat the universe to between 106 and 1.6 × 109 GeV, so the lightest partner does meet the heat; the majoron stays cold because the settling field never re-enters it (hot majorons made by partner pairs stay under a few per cent below about 109 GeV). Above 2.4 × 108 GeV the far-side weak pairs are also made and their decay coupling is needed. The same CMB limit that keeps the majoron cold caps the start’s expansion rate at about 3 × 10−5 f × angle, that is 3 × 106 to 5 × 107 GeV for f between 1011 and 1.5 × 1012 GeV, while handing energy to the middle partner needs a start field heavier than twice its mass, 3.4 × 1010 GeV; the start field must therefore be 700 to 104 times heavier than the expansion rate, which simple single-field starts do not give and a steep or two-field start does. The window is narrow; closing it would remove the lead, not the core. With the alternative start phase the start’s own field (the scalaron, 3.2 × 1013 GeV) can hand energy to the heaviest partner (about 3% of its decays at ξ = 0.12); at the largest asymmetry allowed (Davidson and Ibarra, 2002) this gives 1.0 × 10−10 against 0.87 × 10−10 needed, before erasure by the middle partners, which are in the heat. This is borderline, not a result; the excess stays a measured number.

Two leads with numbers. The quark triangle angle: if the quark link carries a quarter turn, the unitarity triangle is right-angled at α (Antusch, King, Malinsky and Spinrath, 2010), and with the measured mixing the best fits give α = 89 to 93° and γ = 64.1 to 67.6° (66.1 best; 67.4° exactly right-angled), against γ = 66.4 (+2.7 −2.8)° measured; the chance of such a match by luck is 24 to 80%, so it is a lead. The direct measurement of α, 84.1 (+3.7 −3.0)° (Particle Data Group, 2026), sits 1.6σ below 90°. The small neutrino splitting: the hint m2/m3 = 3 − 2√2 = tan2 22.5°, with m1 = 0.36 meV and the measured large splitting, gives Δm221 = 7.37 ± 0.08 × 10−5 eV2 with JUNO’s large splitting (2.509 × 10−3 eV2), against JUNO’s measured 7.388 ± 0.078 (JUNO Collaboration, 2026), within 0.2σ. Versions up to v1.2.2 gave 7.40 ± 0.06: the relation is the same and has no free number, and the value moved in v1.3.2 (18 November 2026) only because the large-splitting input changed from the global fit’s 2.519 × 10−3 to JUNO’s 2.509 × 10−3 eV2 (29 September 2026). Both values lie within 0.2σ of JUNO’s, so the change gained nothing. The lightest mass used here was itself obtained with the measured small splitting, but setting it to zero moves the result by only 0.01, so the reuse does not decide the agreement. The exact right-angled triangle (lead, 25 November 2026). A charge rule for the far-side weight (tested, does not hold). The weight rule of the far-side weak pairs, the meeting energy squared divided by the grain, would follow from a discrete six-step charge (ℤ6) carried by the spin-0 field Φ that gives the coloured far-side states their mass. If the two halves of each coloured state differ by one step and those of each weak pair by two, the coloured states take their mass from Φ directly and the weak pairs only from Φ2 divided by the grain, which gives 2.45 × 108 GeV; the leptons then carry three steps, which forbids every operator that would turn a proton into a lepton. But a discrete charge that gravity respects must be free of anomalies (Krauss and Wilczek, 1989; Ibáñez and Ross, 1991), and this one is not. Counted field by field over three generations, the colour and weak sums are 3 and 15, not multiples of 6; with the half-strength form of the condition sometimes used, those two pass but the gravitational sum, 21, fails, and the Standard Model’s own fields pass without the far side, so the far side is not required either way. No discrete charge of any order works (ℤ2 to ℤ18 were searched with every flavour-blind assignment): the quarks’ own Higgs links make their colour sum vanish, so the colour sum is three times the coloured states’ step, ±3, which vanishes only for ℤ3, and there Φ2 carries the charge of Φ*, so the weak pairs would take their mass at the same step as the coloured ones. The weight rule therefore stays a fitted choice (Appendix A.2, A.6). Script: analysis/farside_charge_rule_2026-11-25.py.

Gravity’s strength and dark matter. Gravity’s strength and the three force strengths are one open problem, the strengths at the grain of space (Section 4). Dark matter is read as partly perspective (Section 5) and partly the majoron (Section 8); its mass and amount are open. A gravitational breaking of the kept spot does not fix the mass: counted correctly it gives about 1% of the dark matter at the settling value used here, and some power of the breaking lands for almost any settling value, so it is not evidence. The majoron as dark matter makes no directly observable number; it is a candidate that fits every limit, not a tested result.

The Higgs side, the generations and the proton’s mass. Why the far side settles, and at 246 GeV, is not derived; nor is how the Higgs link distinguishes the three placements, which is the single question behind the heavy quark masses, the mixing tilt and the twist. Whether the entanglement that gives gravity and the colour balance that gives the proton’s mass are one relationship is open. The generations are unequal in a simple way: the heaviest member carries 99.62% of the up-type masses, 97.94% of the down-type and 94.36% of the charged-lepton masses (quark masses run to 162.5 GeV); this describes the measured masses and is not derived.

The seeds of galaxies and quantum probabilities. On the main line the size and tilt of the first ripples are measured and not derived, and what laid them down is open. The alternative start phase lays them down: the size stays a measured number and the tilt is predicted (Section 9), at 0.963, which today’s combined data disfavour. The probabilities of quantum mechanics (Born, 1926) are inherited; v1.4.0 adopts the published results that derive them from the space of states (Gleason, 1957) and from information rules with entanglement first (Chiribella, D’Ariano and Perinotti, 2011), which the framework does not derive by itself.

11. Conclusion

A single relational picture, in which space is a full medium, structures are set by containment, and entanglement comes first, reproduces gravity, cosmic expansion, the proton’s containment pressure, the masses of the heaviest compact stars and the charged-lepton masses, and, through two quark-mass relations, the down and up quarks. Required to have no gaps, its inner space yields the strong force with three colours, and its relationship matrix, seen from one perspective, gives which other forces exist, their charges and one-handed matter, each with a stated reading. It does this with about 28 measured numbers, against about 34 for the Standard Model with gravity and cosmology counted the same way (the 28 assumes every pending measurement agrees; counting only savings already confirmed it is 32, plus six stated choices; see Inputs and predictions). It makes predictions that experiments will test, among them the neutrino mass ordering and scale, the quark pairing gap and the strong coupling, and one, neutrinoless double beta decay at 1.3 to 4.1 meV, that lies beyond present reach. Read three at a time, its states are every particle of a generation with the measured charges. Its binding forces are exactly those blind to the observer’s facing, giving a colour-neutral proton and a neutral atom. Where others established a result first, from the W and Z mass relation to atomic binding and nuclear masses, it reaches the same numbers by a second route, and says plainly where that route is fitted or fails. Dark matter’s amount is measured; it is read as partly perspective, and the matrix allows one candidate for the rest, a cold, chargeless wave, the majoron, as a lead. The force strengths with gravity’s, the settling of the Higgs side, the heavy quark masses and quark mixing, the lepton twist, the upper limit of three generations, the seeds of galaxies and what dark matter is remain open, as they are for all of physics, and the matter excess is allowed but not predicted. The framework does not ask any established result to be given up. It asks that each be seen as one view of the same universe.

In v1.4.0 the same framework is restated as five rules and one bet: one matrix of relationships read in three roles (held states, links between them, the link across each), a single balance of pull and resistance from the proton to the Local Group, maximum uncertainty for the leptons, nothing extra, and a smooth start. The stage of space and time and the rules of quantum theory are no longer borrowed whole. A stretching start driven by the stiffness of space is kept as the alternative, with two predictions, r = 0.0040 and ns = 0.963; the main line follows today’s combined data, which disfavour that tilt. The measured numbers that remain are almost all link strengths, and they are where the work goes next.
