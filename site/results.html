1. Results reproduced

Gravity

A resistance n = 1 + 2GM/(rc²) acting equally on time and space gives a deflection of starlight at the solar limb of 1.75″ and the measured Shapiro delay. A scalar resistance gives no deflection and a time-only resistance gives half (0.876″); both are excluded by observation. In a filled medium the entanglement entropy follows a boundary law (residual 0.003, against 0.243 for a volume law), which, through Jacobson's argument, yields Einstein's equations. The lattice demonstrates the mechanism; it does not determine the numerical value of Newton's constant.

Compact objects

With B taken from the proton and the outward pressure from the measured strong coupling, unpaired quark matter gives a maximum neutron-star mass of only 1.54–1.80 M☉, below the observed 2.08 ± 0.07 M☉ of PSR J0740+6620. Including colour-superconducting pairing with a gap of about 82–97 MeV (at the central B = 60.1 MeV/fm³; 75–108 MeV across B = 57.1–63.7) gives Mmax = 2.0–2.15 M☉, R1.4 = 10.8–11.3 km and Λ1.4 = 440–580, meeting both the pulsar-mass limit and the GW170817 bound. The black-hole ringdown of GW150914 is reproduced (250 Hz, 4.0 ms), and no echoes are expected.

The proton

Treated as a bag in containment balance with a pion cloud, and fitted only to the proton and Δ masses, the proton gives B = 60.1 MeV/fm³ (57.1–63.7) and a quark-core rms radius of 0.725 fm. Repeating the calculation with the 1975 settings returns B = 58.4 MeV/fm³, against the published value of about 57.5. The measured pressure distribution inside the proton (Burkert, Elouadrhiri and Girod, 2018) displays the push–pull balance directly.

Mass as a balance point

In this framework mass is the energy at which containment and leakage balance, the containment principle that runs through the whole theory. For the proton, the balance of the containment pressure against the inward push of its quarks gives a mass equal to four times the containment energy, and this accounts for about 99% of the proton's mass when only the up and down quark masses are counted as Higgs mass; counting the strange quark the Higgs share is about 9%, and with the heavy quarks' indirect effect about 29% (Shifman, Vainshtein and Zakharov, 1978); the remainder is set by one Higgs balance point (246.22 GeV). The same law, with the same containment pressure B = 60.1 MeV/fm³, governs the proton and the neutron star. Fission is a shift of the balance: 166 MeV by the balance against 173.3 MeV measured for uranium, with the nucleus dividing as observed. The shape and reach of each force follow from what space does to its field: a field that spreads gives 1/r² (electric force, gravity); one expelled into a tube gives a constant pull (the strong force); one screened by the Higgs has a short reach (the weak force); and the nuclear force is shared leakage carried by pions, with a reach of about 1.43 fm.

As strict numbers the balance falls short. The pull between quarks from the proton's own B and αs is 0.155 GeV², against 0.18–0.19 (16% low); the deconfinement temperature is 105 MeV, against 156.5 ± 1.5 (33% low); and the nuclear coupling is about a third low. It fails outright for elementary particles, which have no containment skin of their own (the threshold would be 448 GeV), for the electron as an electric balance (off by 105), for the top-quark coupling, and for the basic strengths of the forces and which forces exist. The quark masses, the twist, left-handedness and the matter excess are not changed by it.

Leptons

The three charged-lepton masses are the squared eigenvalues of one circulant matrix with a single scale, links of 1/√2 and a twist of 2/9, reproducing the electron and muon masses from the tau mass to 0.003%. The fitted twist is 0.222239 ± 0.000040, within 0.4σ of 2/9. The 45° balance of the lepton matrix is the point of maximum uncertainty (exactly one bit) between a lepton's own view and its fully related view; the down-type and up-type quarks fall just below it (0.994 and 0.967 bits). This is proposed as a principle; why nature maximises this uncertainty is not derived. The shared and relational parts of the mass vector are equal (941.55 MeV each), within 0.35% of the proton mass; this is recorded as an observation, not a claimed explanation.

Quarks

Each relation in the particle matrices was tested with measured masses on both sides and judged against the measurement's own error bar. Two links pass. The Cabibbo relation (Gatto, Sartori and Tonin, 1968) gives ms/md = 19.9 against 19.8 ± 0.3. The up quark follows from the decay η → 3π, in which electromagnetic effects nearly cancel (Sutherland, 1966; Bell and Sutherland, 1968). Leutwyler's relation (mu/md)² + (ms/md)²/Q² = 1, with Q = 22.1 ± 0.7 extracted from the measured decays (Colangelo, Lanz, Leutwyler and Passemar, 2018), gives mu = 2.04 MeV (range 1.73–2.29; measured 2.16 ± 0.07) with no input from lattice QCD. The heavier links fail when tested strictly. A full Higgs coupling of the top quark (Hill, 1981) gives 174.1 GeV against 172.60 ± 0.27 (0.9% high, but 5.6 standard deviations). The 45° balance on the triples (c, b, t) and (s, c, b) (Rivero, 2011) puts charm 15 standard deviations high and strange 10 low, and no definition of the charm mass rescues it. These are numerical patterns good to 1–10%, not laws, so the bottom, charm and strange masses remain measured inputs. Each relation is published separately (Hill; Rivero; Gatto, Sartori and Tonin; Leutwyler); linking them through the matrices is this framework's contribution.

Quark masses: a cone or a spiral

Two pictures of the quark ladders were written as exact statements, nine in all, and tested at one energy (the top quark's own, 162.5 GeV) against each measurement's own error. In the cone, the square roots of a ladder's three masses sit at 120° around one axis with a fixed opening (Koide, 1982; Foot, 1994; Brannen, 2006). In the spiral, each turn also climbs by a set factor. The leptons are the cone: opening 45.000°, exact to a part in a million. Any three masses can be written as a cone, so for quarks the cone says nothing until its opening is fixed, and the two quark openings, 48.150° ± 0.015° (down-type) and 52.147° ± 0.018° (up-type), match neither 45° nor each other (by 213, 405 and 174 standard deviations); all six quarks on one 45° cone (Kartavtsev, 2011) also fails. If both quark ladders were cones about the same axis, the weak force would not mix them at all; the measured mixing is the right size for a small offset, so the tilt between the ladders is exactly the amount by which the two cones do not share an axis.

The spiral fails as a law in every form tested: equal steps, the up ladder as the down ladder squared, steps set by powers of the mixing (Froggatt and Nielsen, 1979), the two ladders as the two ends of one spiral, and the lepton cone wound into a spiral. What survives is a shape: comparing the second step with the first, the quarks (1.33 and 0.89, against 1 for equal steps) are much nearer a spiral than the leptons (0.53), and the fitted powers of the mixing for the four quark steps (2.0, 2.7, 4.2, 3.7) are near whole numbers without being on them. The one statement that lands is the Cabibbo link already listed above. So the leptons are the cone and the quarks lean toward the spiral; they are different shapes, which is why the lepton rule never carried over. The down ladder's 48.15° opening does not change with energy and is a fixed value still to be explained, as 45° is. No quark mass moved: the bottom, charm, strange and top masses stay measured.

Do quarks and leptons push on each other's masses?

They do, and the sizes are known: a particle's measured mass depends slightly on the energy at which it is looked at, because every other particle flickers around it (Gell-Mann and Low, 1954; Machacek and Vaughn, 1984). There are three routes. Through the Higgs link, the quarks (almost entirely the top) push every lepton link by 1.67% per step in energy, while the leptons push back on the quarks by only 0.00006%; this push is the same for all three rungs of a ladder, so it changes a ladder's size, not its shape. Inside one weak pair the push is rung by rung and of opposite sign: the top pushes the bottom by −0.83% per step, a true push and pull, and this is how the two quark ladders bend each other above the top's energy, consistent with two ladders hung from one shared state. Through the electric force each particle pushes itself, by up to 2.84% between the lightest and heaviest lepton, and directly through the photon quarks and leptons push each other by only about one part in 104 to 105. Nothing of the in-pair kind joins a quark to a lepton.

Tested against each near miss, none of these pushes closes it. The electric push is the right size to move the lepton scale's 0.35% gap from one third of the proton, and the two agree at a common energy of about 1.3 GeV, but there the opening is no longer 45° and nothing in the framework picks that energy. Putting the quarks on the leptons' footing moves their balance ratios away from 5/4 and 5/3, by less than a fifth of the gap. The charm miss in the colour rule (6.8%) and the strength miss (8.9%) are far beyond any of these pushes. What is kept: the lepton cone is exact for masses as weighed and for no common energy, although the electric push should spoil it by 0.35% (Sumino, 2009), a known unexplained fact sharper than the rule itself; and since quarks are never weighed alone, a quark rule must name the footing on which it holds before it is tested, which is part of why every lepton-style rule tried on quarks lands close and not on.

Forces, colours and generations

Read directly off the particle matrices, the carrier counts (eight carriers, two of them neutral; and four carriers, two neutral) admit only SU(3) and SU(2) × U(1) in the classification of Lie algebras, and the global form S(U(3) × U(2)) passes the ℤ6 check for every field. The counts themselves are measured. Requiring that the medium has no gaps (no zero divisors) and that relationships keep their size when combined (a conserved norm) restricts its inner algebra to ℝ, ℂ, ℍ or 𝕆 (Hurwitz, 1898). A force richer than the weak force requires 𝕆; fixing any one direction, as every observation does, leaves SU(3) acting on three complex directions, which gives three colours. ℍ acting from the left and from the right gives SU(2)L × SU(2)R, but only with a different meaning of “symmetry” from the one that gives colour; on the number systems alone no single meaning gives the strong, weak and hypercharge carriers together, and the routes that join ℂ, ℍ and 𝕆 into one algebra (Dixon; Furey) bring back zero divisors, which the no-gaps rule forbids. The strong force is therefore derived; the weak force, hypercharge and left-handedness are taken from measurement in this two-way reading (on the main line, read three at a time from one perspective, they follow; see below). Hermitian octonionic matrices satisfy the Jordan identity only up to 3 × 3 (Jordan, von Neumann and Wigner, 1934), and an irremovable CP-violating phase requires at least three generations (Kobayashi and Maskawa, 1973), so on this picture the number of generations is exactly three. These steps rest on the framework's pictures of a full medium and of observation as one perspective; translating the physical picture into algebraic rules, and identifying forces with symmetries of the inner space (Weyl; Yang and Mills), are interpretive steps rather than derivations. Completed with the 4 × 4 group of Pati and Salam, this route is kept on record as the alternative picture; the main line is the relationship-matrix picture below.

The main picture of the forces: the relationship matrix

The framework's main picture reads the forces from its central object, the relationship matrix. Where gravity emerges from entanglement, an exact symmetry must come with a force (Harlow and Ooguri, 2019; proven so far only for spaces with a boundary), so in this framework forces are expected rather than added. Taking matter itself as motion in an inner space (Kaluza; Klein) fails: a charged particle would weigh about 5 × 1017 GeV, and a smooth inner space cannot give left-handed matter (Witten, 1983). The forces instead come from the framework's central object, the relationship matrix. The 3 × 3 Hermitian octonionic matrix, seen by an observer who stands at one point and faces one way, has exactly 12 carriers, 4 of them neutral, in blocks of 8, 3 and 1, the measured pattern, with one meaning of symmetry and no zero divisors. The weak pairs then carry hypercharges in the ratio 3 : 1, and anomaly cancellation fixes their partners at +2/3, −1/3 and −1. This count is published work (Todorov and Dubois-Violette, 2018; Dubois-Violette and Todorov, 2019; Krasnov, 2019; Baez, 2020–21), reproduced here; the framework's part is the reading of it as a relationship matrix seen from one perspective, which is one added reading of the perspective rule.

Read two at a time, this does not finish the problem. The matrix's link states then hold the weak pairs, the down quark's partner and the neutral partner, but lacks exactly two of the partners a whole generation needs, those of the up quark and the electron: four states, one octonion's worth seen from one perspective. These two missing partners and the strengths are one problem. Counting only what the matrix holds, one common strength predicts sin2θW = 0.75 where the strong and weak forces meet, against about 0.43 from the measured values, 75% off. Counting the whole generation that each force acts on (Chamseddine and Connes, 1996) gives 0.375, 12% low, and 0.207 at the Z mass against the measured 0.2312 at one loop, and 8.9% off with the standard two-loop method: the fifty-year-old near miss of Georgi, Quinn and Weinberg (1974). With these states alone the strengths do not meet, so here the form is derived and the strength is measured, as for gravity. Four homes for the missing partners were tested with two-way combinations (relationships between two links, the matrix's other turns, the doubled link space, and a larger 4 × 4 matrix, which is not a consistent algebra) and none works without gaps. The three-way reading below supplies both partners; it leaves the strength miss unchanged. Left-handedness follows with one added reading (below). The generations cannot be slots inside the same matrix; they are three placements of the one shape, so on this picture “at least three” is a theorem and “at most three” rests on the nothing-extra rule and on measurement. A right-handed force cannot sit alongside it, which removes the left–right scale of 1011.7 GeV and leaves the matter excess from a hot start about 2.6 to 3 times short of the observed value. Any new force carrier (a heavier W or Z, or a quark–lepton carrier) would rule this picture out. The framework carries this relationship-matrix picture as its main line, because much else depends on it; the 4 × 4 picture above is kept on record as the alternative, should the matrix picture ever be shown wrong. Neither is a derivation of the forces from the first two principles alone, and the force-groups criterion stays partly met.

A relationship among three: every particle of a generation

The framework's first rule says relationships never combine into nothing. It is read at two levels. For the numbers in the matrix it applies two at a time, and this forces the octonions (Hurwitz, 1898). For the states built from those numbers it applies three at a time: nothing, combined with its own mirror image and itself, gives nothing. The three-way product {x, y, z}, in which the middle state enters as its mirror image, is known mathematics (Jordan, 1933; Loos, 1977), and on the matrix's link states it has no gaps. The only gaps left are two-way ones of the kind “the same state twice gives nothing”, and for matter that is the exclusion principle (Pauli, 1925), which physics requires. Red light with red light is simply one brighter red.

The observer's perspective is read precisely: the spot is kept exactly, the facing is kept as a direction, and the overall phase, which no measurement sees, is left out. The turns of the three-way product that respect this number exactly 12, four of them neutral, in blocks of 8, 3 and 1. Under them the 16 link states are a whole generation with the measured charges: the quark pair (+1/6, six states), the lepton pair (−1/2, two), the down partner (+1/3, three), the up partner (−2/3, three), the electron partner (+1, one) and the hidden neutrino partner (0, one). All four anomaly sums vanish. Nothing is extra and nothing is missing, and the 16 states are not their own mirror image, so matter is one-handed directly. The two states the perspective picks out are themselves particles: the hidden neutrino partner and the electron partner. The pattern is the whole-generation pattern of Georgi (1975) and of Fritzsch and Minkowski (1975), here without their extra force carriers; the framework's part is the reading.

Tested strictly, the result holds and its costs are stated. Other readings of the perspective give 11, 13 or 14 turns or inconsistent charges, so the reading is an added step, not a forced one. Applied in the same way to the complex numbers and the quaternions, it gives 3 and 5 kept turns; only the octonions give the measured 8, 3 and 1. The weak force is re-identified: only 9 of these 12 turns are shared with the two-way count above, which made every link state a weak pair; here half are pairs and half are singles, as measured, and the count 8 | 3 | 1 stands. The force strengths are not fixed: this geometry gives the whole-generation count automatically, 0.375 with strong equal to weak, which is the near miss at the Z mass (8.9% at two loops), so this reading alone does not fix the strengths; the far-side weak pairs that would close the gap are described under “The theory as one calculation”. Charges read as literal bound sets of three (Harari, 1979; Shupe, 1979) fail, because no inner structure is seen; the count of three colour switches holds as a pattern (Georgi and Glashow, 1974; Furey, 2016). The framework now reads the matrix this way. With it, which forces exist, which states they act on, every charge and one-handedness follow with stated readings, and only the strengths remain under the force-groups heading, which stays partly met.

Why matter is one-handed, and the three generations

On the relationship-matrix picture, handedness belongs to the side the observer faces, not to the particle alone: facing the other way swaps left with right and matter with antimatter together, which is the observed CP symmetry of the weak force. The matrix has a single imaginary direction of its own, the direction the observer faces. If spin is written with that same direction (the no-gaps rule applied to spin, one added reading), mirror-image partners would be exactly the kind of gap the first rule forbids, so matter must be one-handed (read three at a time, the argument is simpler still: no mirror states exist at all); of the two one-handed arrangements, one is inconsistent, and the other is the measured world (Lee and Yang, 1956; Wu, 1957). Reaching the other side of the spiral requires mass, which is why only massive particles show both handednesses; the pion decay ratio agrees within its 0.24% error. Left-handedness therefore moves from “put in” to “follows with one added reading”, not to a derivation. Carried to its end, “left” and “right” are not needed as a separate idea at all. The three-way structure has exactly one i, the observer's facing direction: the only quantities that commute with all 46 of its turns are 1 and that i, so every state takes its screw sense from it, and the 16 states cannot be wound independently (with the 12 carriers alone they could). The perspective rule therefore reads: stand at one spot, face one way, with one i throughout. Facing the other way turns i into −i and changes three names together: matter and antimatter, left and right, i and −i. The charges of the 16 states and of their mirror image are different lists, so the world is not its own mirror image, as measured (Weyl, 1929; Lee and Yang, 1956; Wu, 1957). Giving spin a separate i would double every state into mirror pairs, which the nothing-extra rule forbids. What stays inherited is that matter is made of spinning waves and that quantum mechanics uses one i (Stueckelberg, 1960). Read two at a time, a whole generation does not fit inside one matrix, because the partners of the up quark and the electron are missing; read three at a time, it does (see above).

The three generations sit beside the weak force as three placements of the one relationship matrix, not as three slots inside it. Because it is one shape, all three feel the same forces, as measured to 0.1–0.2%. At least three generations follow from the measured matter–antimatter difference (Kobayashi and Maskawa, 1973); at most three is not derived on this picture and rests on the nothing-extra rule and on measurement. No single structure gives both the forces and exactly three generations. The lepton 45° balance, self weight equal to relationship weight, holds to 15 parts in a million but is not derived by any balance principle tested, and the twist and the electron's mass turn out to be one open number rather than two. A suggested pattern of balance ratios near 5/4 and 5/3 for the quark families fails at 2 to 5 standard deviations and is not adopted.

Gravity's strength, the Higgs field and the dark side

Mass without left and right. The observer stands at one spot of the 3 × 3 matrix; the 16 states run from it, and the rest of the matrix, the far side, holds 10 parts. Under the 12 carriers these are a colourless weak pair of charge ±1/2, which carries exactly the Higgs field's numbers, and six coloured parts of charge ±1/3 (Freudenthal, 1954; the pattern of Georgi, 1975, and Fritzsch and Minkowski, 1975, here not added but part of the same matrix). Through the colourless pair each paired state links to exactly one single state: quark pair to up partner and to down partner, lepton pair to electron partner and to hidden neutrino partner, all other links being zero. Mass is this link through the far side of the matrix. It says which links exist, not their sizes: taken alone the four links are equal, whereas the top quark is 60 to 80 times the bottom. The six coloured parts must be read as entries of the matrix, not particles, or the proton would decay (Dimopoulos and Georgi, 1981); that is a stated reading.

Gravity's strength. With gravity a consequence of entanglement (Bekenstein, 1973; Hawking, 1975; Jacobson, 1995), its strength is the fineness of the links of space, one number that entanglement alone does not fix. It is tied to the force strengths: the proton is light compared with the grain of space because the strong force is weak there and strengthens only slowly on the way down (Gross and Wilczek, 1973; Politzer, 1973; Wilczek, 2001). This tie holds, but it trades one measured number for another. Two attempts to obtain the number fail: gravity as strong as the other forces where they meet predicts a grain energy 62 times too low (the gap noted by Kaplunovsky, 1988), and counting the framework's 124 states (Sakharov, 1967) turns a grain size into a strength without giving the size. Gravity's strength and the three force strengths are therefore one open problem, the strengths at the grain of space.

Gravity and the Higgs field. The Higgs field cannot set gravity's strength (Zee, 1979): that needs a coupling to curvature 4 × 1016 times above the limit from the LHC (Atkins and Calmet, 2013). In the other direction, requiring the Higgs self-coupling to vanish at the grain of space (Shaposhnikov and Wetterich, 2010) gives a Higgs mass. This is the framework's own balance of containment, applied at the grain: the Higgs holds itself together down to the grain and no further. Run at two loops (Machacek and Vaughn, 1983–85; Luo, Wang and Xiao, 2003) from the published starting values (Buttazzo and others, 2013), the Standard Model's states alone give 128.8 ± 1.5 GeV against the measured 125.13 ± 0.11 GeV (Particle Data Group, 2026), which is 2.6 standard deviations high. With the matrix's far-side states included (three colourless weak pairs at 2.4 × 108 GeV and their three coloured partners at 4.6 × 1013 GeV, both fixed beforehand by the force strengths and not adjusted here) the result is 127.2 ± 1.5 GeV with an estimated three-loop step, within 1.4 standard deviations. The test and its pass line were fixed before it was run. The pass rests on a 0.5 GeV allowance for how the top-quark mass is defined; without it the gap is 2.1 standard deviations. Of the rules borrowed from other theories, this is the only one that set a number and passed, so choosing it is a selection among several. The Higgs mass therefore follows within the framework and leaves the list of measured inputs; the Higgs value, 246 GeV, stays measured. This awaits measurement and is not counted as established until it returns: the rule needs a top-quark mass of 171.6 GeV against 172.60 ± 0.27 measured (3.7 standard deviations on the top mass alone), the error allows 0.5 GeV for the known ambiguity in what the measured top mass means (without that allowance the gap is 1.9 standard deviations, and on the top mass alone it is 3.2), and a sharper top mass or strong coupling could push the gap past 3 standard deviations, which would fail it. The rule also takes over the assumption that gravity stays well behaved at the grain (Weinberg, 1979; Reuter, 1998). Any extra weak matter at high energy would help in the same way, so the agreement does not single out this framework.

Dark energy and dark matter. When gravity comes from entanglement, stored field energy does not act as dark energy; the dark energy is a constant fixed once (Einstein, 1919; Jacobson, 1995). Without this the Higgs balance energy, 5 × 1054 times the observed dark energy, would make the universe unrecognisable (Weinberg, 1989). On the main line the size of dark energy is set by the two ends of space together, the grain and the surface (the horizon): √(grain × surface) = 3.5 meV against 2.24 meV measured, the relation of Cohen, Kaplan and Nelson (1999). This agreement in size follows from the Friedmann equation and is not counted as a derivation, so the amount stays one measured number. On this reading dark energy need not be constant, and no law for its change is derived: the holographic formula of Li (2004) gives today's value but changes in the direction opposite to the hint reported by DESI (2025), and is not used. A fixed amount that never changes is kept on record as the alternative. No state of the matrix itself is a dark matter particle: the only state with no force charge, the hidden neutrino partner, decays in 10−15 s or less, and making it stable would leave its neutrino massless. The main line's candidate is instead the wave left over when that partner's own mass settles, the majoron (see “Dark matter: part perspective, part a real wave” below). Reading dark matter as a response of entanglement (Verlinde, 2017) gets the size of the galactic acceleration scale right (1.09 against 1.20 ± 0.24 × 10−10 m/s²; McGaugh, Lelli and Schombert, 2016) but fails published tests on galaxies, the Solar System and clusters, and it is not carried forward. Quark mixing did not come out of any of these tests: the simplest mass link gives no mixing, the right starting point, and the small measured tilt is not produced.

The forces that build the atom

In every bound system the push is the same thing, a wave refusing to be squeezed (Heisenberg, 1927); only the pull changes. Among the 12 carriers, a set of turns is blind to the observer's facing if the 16 states, under those turns, are their own mirror image. The largest such set is unique: the 8 strong turns together with one mixture of the single turn and a weak turn, which is electric charge. These are the two forces that bind, and they are mirror-symmetric, as measured (Lee and Yang, 1956; Wu, 1957; Vafa and Witten, 1984). The word “largest” is needed: smaller blind sets exist, such as the weak force alone. When the colourless Higgs numbers on the far side of the matrix settle to a fixed value, exactly the same 9 turns are kept; the three lost turns become the short-range W+, W− and Z. This is one statement, not two confirmations, because a mass link maps the world onto its mirror image. That the far side settles at all, and in the direction that keeps the most turns (Michel and Radicati, 1971), is not derived.

From the kept charges the proton is up + up + down = +1 exactly, the neutron 0 and hydrogen 0 to the limit of the arithmetic (measured: zero to 1 part in 1021; Bressi and others, 2011), and three quarks of different colours are colour-neutral because there are exactly three colours. Each neutral bundle leaves a leftover at the next scale: the nuclear force outside the proton and chemistry outside the atom. Counting the framework's own states fixes the direction of each force (Gross and Wilczek, 1973; Politzer, 1973): the strong force grows with distance and locks colour in near the proton's own scale; the weak force would lock in only near 3 × 10−24 GeV, but the Higgs side settles 26 powers of ten earlier and hides it; electric charge fades with distance and runs free. Where each happens uses the measured strengths. The Higgs link is a real pull whose charge is mass (Yukawa, 1935); it is the link that gives mass. Matter number, which might have been a third force of the push-and-pull kind, is read in the main line as never becoming a force (next section). It has a reach of 1/534 of the proton's radius; it binds nothing that exists, and it is not gravity, since every kind of matter falls alike to 1.5 parts in 1015 (MICROSCOPE, 2022) while the Higgs charge per unit mass is 1 for the electron and about 0.29 for the proton. The Higgs field does not count all energy: if it did, the LHC would see about 130 times more Higgs particles than it does (ATLAS and CMS, 2022). Gravity counts all the energy, most of that energy is relationship energy, and the Higgs link adds between 1% and 29% of a proton, depending on what is counted. Whether the entanglement behind gravity and the balance behind the proton's mass are the same relationship is not shown and is now an open question.

The two settled directions left alone by the kept turns link the up quark and the neutrino to their partners, and the down quark and the electron to theirs. With one real Higgs pair they are equal (top = bottom = tau, which fails); with the complex states of the three-way reading there are two Higgs pairs and the top-to-bottom ratio is free (T. D. Lee, 1973), but bottom = tau stays and fails. The whole matrix read three at a time has exactly three independent sizes, and no larger matrix of this kind exists (Jordan, von Neumann and Wigner, 1934; Albert, 1934), which matches the lepton rule's three sizes at three angles. That “at most three sizes” would become “at most three generations” only if a generation is a size of one matrix, and one matrix cannot hold both the forces and the generations (three generations would need 48 link states; the matrix has 24). It is a strong hint, not a derivation.

The energy the Higgs does not give, and matter number

Every mass in an atom needs the Higgs to join a particle to its partner. The matrix allows exactly one mass that needs no Higgs: the hidden neutrino partner paired with itself. It is allowed because the perspective rule keeps the observer's spot exactly; that state is the one kept exactly, so it carries no charge of any kind. With the spot held only as a direction, or with no perspective, no mass exists without the Higgs. This is the standard heavy-partner mass that makes neutrinos light (Majorana, 1937; Minkowski, 1977; Yanagida, 1979; Gell-Mann, Ramond and Slansky, 1979), here placed in the main line with no new reading. The pairing is allowed, not required, and its size is an input.

Keeping the spot exactly gives up one turn, a thirteenth, which commutes with the other twelve. Its values are +1/3 on quarks and −1 on leptons: matter number, B − L (Davidson, 1979; Marshak and Mohapatra, 1980). Strong, electric and matter number together form the largest facing-blind part of the 13. Two settlings each give up one thing and give one kind of mass: the far side settling (the Higgs value) gives up the weak turns and gives every mass that joins a pair to its partner; the spot kept exactly gives up matter number and gives the one Higgs-free mass. With both, the 13 turns become the 9 of the strong and electric forces, which is why matter number is conserved in everything seen yet is not a force anyone feels. Two readings were possible, and nothing measured separates them. In one, matter number becomes a real force above about 1011 GeV, the B − L force of the earlier 4 × 4 picture and of Georgi (1975) and Fritzsch and Minkowski (1975); torsion balances limit any long-range version to a strength of about 10−24 (Wagner, Schlamminger, Gundlach and Adelberger, 2012), so its carrier would be heavy and bind nothing. In the other, the spot is kept exactly and matter number is never a force, only a turn the perspective excludes. The main line takes the second reading: matter number is never a force. The first is kept on record as the alternative. The choice has a consequence: in the main line the piece that settles the Higgs-free mass leaves behind a chargeless wave, the majoron, which is the main line's dark matter candidate (below).

Tested strictly, this energy does not give the three generations: no Higgs-free pairing touches a charged particle, one common value would make the neutrino mass ratio 49 times too small, and its three values follow no pattern, so the count of 24 link states against the 48 that three generations need is unchanged. A Higgs that treats every generation alike, with something else supplying the difference, fails at the LHC by about 68 standard deviations in the muon-to-tau comparison (ATLAS and CMS, 2022); what survives is that the Higgs sets the scale and the geometry sets the pattern. The Higgs value, gravity's strength and dark energy can fairly be grouped as properties of space, none of them a turn of the matrix, but they are not the same kind of thing: the Higgs value melts above 159.5 GeV (D'Onofrio and Rummukainen, 2016), has spin 0 and does not count all energy, and trial relations between them miss by factors of 90 to 450; one lands within 7%, a coincidence among six tries that is recorded and not used. The matrix does supply the protection a dark particle would need, a sign, (−1) to the power three times matter number, that survives both settlings (for the far-side parts it can only be approximate: were it exact, the lightest neutral far-side state would be stable, and searches for dark matter exclude such a state), but among its states no particle to protect: every state with that sign is ordinary matter, and the relic neutrinos give only 0.5% of the dark matter. A candidate needs something beyond the 16 states (Krauss and Wilczek, 1989; Kadastik, Kannike and Raidal, 2010). With matter number never a force, that something is already present without adding a state: the leftover wave of the piece that settles the Higgs-free mass (see “Dark matter: part perspective, part a real wave”).

Neutrinos and the matter–antimatter excess

If the neutrino shares the relation matrix of the charged leptons, the seesaw mechanism (Minkowski; Yanagida; Gell-Mann, Ramond and Slansky; Glashow; Mohapatra and Senjanović) gives heavy partner masses of 7.3 × 105, 1.3 × 109 and 6.3 × 1010 GeV. On the alternative 4 × 4 picture the heaviest matched a right-handed force at 1011.7 GeV, with a proton lifetime of about 1036 years; the main line has no right-handed force and no proton decay. With the framework's content (the twelve known carriers, one Higgs pair, the majoron and no coloured spin-0 states) this is a proof and not only an expectation: in the 27 states of each generation (Gürsey, Ramond and Sikivie, 1976; Slansky, 1981) every charge is exact, all five anomaly sums vanish, and no ordinary interaction can change the number of quarks, because that takes three quarks at once (Weinberg, 1979; Wilczek and Zee, 1979). What is left is an effect of the grain of space itself, a lifetime near 1044 years against the measured floor of 2.4 × 1034 years (Super-Kamiokande, 2020). The proof rests on the nothing-extra rule: coloured partners of the Higgs would bring the decay back. Flavoured leptogenesis (Fukugita and Yanagida, 1986), solved with the full rate equations, does not predict the observed baryon asymmetry of 6.1 × 10−10: with the lepton relation read literally and only the unmeasured phases free, the largest value found is about 1.5 × 10−13, some 4,000 times short. One narrow corner survives every test tried, so the observed excess is allowed: a specific perspective on the neutrino matrix with δ ≈ 274° (consistent with current neutrino data), a hot start within a window of about five in temperature (capped by magnetic monopoles), and a lightest partner almost unlinked to the electron. With the main line's dark matter that hot start fails, because most of the dark matter would then be hot, so the excess is carried instead by a cold start that hands its energy straight to the hidden partners (Lazarides and Shafi, 1991), with one measured start number (handover 0.0017). This is an allowed region and a lead, not a prediction.

Carrier matrices: what the three-way meetings predict

Nature never joins two matter particles directly: every meeting is two matter particles and one carrier, a photon, Z, W, gluon or Higgs. Writing each carrier as a matrix of which two particles it joins, and how strongly, reproduces the measured couplings and charges, and gives the framework's own reason for a known fact of the electroweak theory (Glashow, 1961; Weinberg, 1967; Salam, 1968): the W and Z masses are tied by one Higgs value, ρ = 1, because the far side of the relationship matrix holds exactly one weak pair of charge ½ (Veltman, 1977). Measured, ρ = 1.0003 ± 0.0005 after known corrections. The matrices also hold eight values that have never been measured, listed under Predictions. Four tries to get the open masses from these matrices fail: the balance that would cancel the Higgs's sensitivity to high energies (Veltman, 1981) needs a Higgs of 313 GeV; zeros in the mass matrices (Fritzsch, 1977) miss the quark mixing; equal entries miss; and the strengths are still off. What the matrices do show is where the open numbers live: the unexplained masses are exactly the entries of the Higgs matrix, and the quark-mixing tilt is exactly the W matrix, so the missing rule must act on both together.

Energy matrices

The particle matrix gives every pair a link value, (m1 + m2)/√(m1m2). Built as energy matrices for the two kinds of force, push-and-pull and perspective, it turns out to be exactly the factor that sets how strongly two particles bind: their balance mass is √(m1m2) divided by the link value, which is the reduced mass of Bohr's atom (Bohr, 1913) written as a relationship. It therefore reproduces hydrogen (13.598 eV), positronium (6.803 eV), muonium (13.540 eV) and muonic hydrogen (2.528 keV) exactly, as the standard treatment does, and it gives atoms never yet observed: true muonium, 1.407 keV; tauonium, 23.66 keV; a proton bound to a tau, 16.35 keV. For the strong force, light baryons and mesons come out to a few per cent with five fitted numbers, the method of De Rújula, Georgi and Glashow (1975). The weak decays give the tau lifetime (289.7 fs, against 290.3 fs) and the top quark's width (1.35 GeV, against 1.42 (+0.19 −0.15) GeV). Weak as perspective and strong and electric as push-and-pull hold; gravity as a perspective fails in matrix form, because it takes the shape of a product, like the Higgs link. For every pair of elementary particles the Higgs pull exceeds the gravitational pull by the same factor, 1.96 × 1032. A further test, that matter number as a force could close the strength gap, fails, and with matter number never a force it no longer applies.

Three scales, and mass as a slight offset from balance

The framework's three scales, quarks and gluons, protons and electrons, and the top, each have one push-and-pull and one perspective; this is a classification and moves no number. The Higgs link adds a little to a bound top pair (22 to 43 MeV, about 2%), and the Higgs cannot be built from top pairs (Bardeen, Hill and Lindner, 1990): fail. The Cabibbo relation √(md/ms) = 0.2242 against 0.2250 holds (Gatto, Sartori and Tonin, 1968), but the same rule fails for the turn between the second and third families, and a rule in which the quarks share one common state fails there too (0.39 of the measured value). Reading each mass as a slight offset from balance holds as a picture, but no shared missing variable sets the offsets, the tiers of the scale view are not the generations, and gravity does not add mass: binding lowers it.

Dark matter: part perspective, part a real wave

A planet as grouped balances. A planet is a grouping of balances, like bubbles squeezed together: its mass is the sum of its parts' balance energies, lowered slightly by binding (up to about 0.9% for nuclei; Weizsäcker, 1935). This holds.

Dark matter as perspective. In the framework space has a baseline resistance, and regions of lower resistance, the voids, push while matter clumps along the cosmic web. For galaxies this reading holds: their outskirts follow one acceleration scale (Milgrom, 1983), about 1.2 × 10−10 m/s², close to the framework's cH0/2π = 1.04 × 10−10 m/s², and the Local Group is pushed away from a large void (the Dipole Repeller; Hoffman, Pomarède, Tully and Courtois, 2017). It fails for colliding clusters, where the mass is seen to separate from the gas (the Bullet Cluster; Clowe and others, 2006), and for the cosmic microwave background (Planck, 2018). So dark matter is read as partly perspective and partly real material that does not shine.

The real part: overlapping waves. Identical particles that can share a state (bosons; Bose, 1924; Einstein, 1925) merge into one wave once more than one sits inside a wavelength; near the Sun this happens for any such particle lighter than about 37 eV. The overlap hides the particles, not their mass, so the pull is unchanged. This is a studied class, wave dark matter (Hu, Barkana and Gruzinov, 2000; Hui, Ostriker, Tremaine and Witten, 2017), open between about 2 × 10−20 eV (Rogers and Peiris, 2021) and 30 eV. With matter number never a force, the main line already has such a particle without adding a state: the piece that settles the hidden neutrino partner's own mass leaves a spin-0, chargeless wave, the majoron (Chikashige, Mohapatra and Peccei, 1981). Its lifetime is 1025 to 1048 s, far longer than the age of the universe (4.4 × 1017 s). It is the main line's dark matter candidate. Its mass and how much of it the early universe made are not derived (Berezinsky and Valle, 1993; Garcia-Cely and Heeck, 2017). If it formed as a slowly settling wave in a universe that started colder than the settling value f (Preskill, Wise and Wilczek, 1983; Abbott and Sikivie, 1983; Dine and Fischler, 1983), it can be all of the dark matter. Its mass follows mJ = 8.8 keV × (6.3 × 1010 GeV ÷ f)4 ÷ angle4, so it is not fixed and can lie anywhere from below 1 eV to 350 keV, and it is cold, so galaxies form normally. A hot start fails: about 73% of the dark matter would then be fast-moving. A start that hands its energy straight to the hidden partners (Lazarides and Shafi, 1991) avoids this and still lets their decays make the matter excess, at the cost of one number. A further idea, that gravity's tiny leak into the kept spot sets the majoron's mass, does not fix it and is not evidence.

The three layers of mass

Every mass built from elementary particles is three layers: the Higgs field, which gives the elementary masses; the balance of the strong containment, which turns quarks into their home masses and into hadrons; and the nuclear interaction energy, the binding released when a nucleus forms. Together they give atoms to 0.008% of their mass across 2,899 nuclei (Wang and others, 2021), against a worst miss of 0.95% without the third layer. For iron-56 the Higgs layer is 595 MeV (1.1%), the balance layer 52,001 MeV (99%) and the binding −492 MeV measured, −494 MeV from the balance formula. The proton itself is about 9% quark mass, 32% quark motion, 37% gluon field and 23% scale energy (Ji, 1995; Yang and others, 2018), so about 91% of it is balance energy, as the containment principle says. This is the standard structure (Weizsäcker, 1935; Bethe and Bacher, 1936; Sakharov and Zeldovich, 1966; De Rújula, Georgi and Glashow, 1975) written in the framework's terms, and it holds. It does not derive the bottom layer: the Higgs links (the elementary masses), the three home-mass numbers and the five binding numbers are fitted. The leftover balance points in the matrices fall on other particles' masses no more often than chance.

Sizes and the proton's home

Writing every pair's size, S = ½ ln(m1/m2), as a matrix shows every gap as a distance; it holds one number per particle and adds no information. Five patterns in it were tested against random mass sets, and none survives: the same ratios in every family, the same stretched shape, a fixed shrink factor, whole powers of one number (Froggatt and Nielsen, 1979), and a shared cone. Lined up by its balance with the proton, every particle sits on one S-shaped curve, φ = 1/(1 + e−2S) (Verhulst, 1838): everything much lighter is seen at its own mass, everything much heavier is capped at the proton, and the proton with itself sits at exactly one half. This is the scale-view principle in exact form; it is a way of seeing the masses, not a source of them. The eigenvalues of the matrices are set mainly by their two ends, the electron and the top.

Scaling backwards to the Planck energy

Masses and strengths change with the energy at which they are seen, by exact equations (Gross and Wilczek, 1973; Politzer, 1973; Machacek and Vaughn, 1984; Arason and others, 1992). Run from the top's energy up to the Planck energy, neither quark ladder ever becomes a 45° cone: the down ladder moves from 48.15° to 47.72° and the up ladder from 52.15° to 52.31° over 17 powers of ten, while the leptons stay at 45.05°. The strong force pulls every quark mass by almost the same factor, so ratios barely run. The top's link, pulled down from any large value at high energy (Pendleton and Ross, 1981; Hill, 1981), lands at 1.28 to 1.32, a top of 222 to 229 GeV in this scheme, against the measured 0.937 (163 GeV): right in kind, 36–41% too high. So the rule for the elementary masses must act on their ratios, which do not run, and not on their sizes, which do.

The condition that the contents always fill the medium (Ωm + Ωr + ΩΛ = 1) gives a flat universe and, with Planck 2018 parameters, an age of 13.79 Gyr (measured 13.787 ± 0.020). 2. Predictions

Awaiting measurement. Each result in this section follows within the framework but rests on a measurement that has not yet returned. None is counted as established until it does. The measurement that decides each one is named with it.

Neutrino masses and ordering. The lepton relation, applied to the two measured splittings, admits only normal ordering, with m1 = 0.36 meV, m2 = 8.6–8.7 meV and m3 = 50.1–50.3 meV (Σ = 0.0591 to 0.0594 eV, depending on the measured splittings). Inverted ordering would require Σ ≥ 0.10 eV, which exceeds the DESI bound. Because the predicted sum lies just above the minimum allowed for normal ordering (0.0588 eV), the most distinctive number is the lightest mass. Falsified by: a determination of inverted ordering by JUNO, or a measured sum clearly different from 0.059 eV.

Neutrino mixing (conditional). Of the simple mixing patterns, exact tribimaximal and trimaximal mixing are excluded by the data; the TM1 pattern (Xing and Zhou; Albright and Rodejohann) gives sin²θ12 = 0.318. JUNO's 2026 measurement, 0.3036 ± 0.0064, puts this 2.3σ high: not yet a failure, which needs 3σ, but the sharpest pressure on the framework today. If it reaches 3σ, TM1 fails, and with it the θ12 and CP-phase savings. If the three neutrino flavours are perspectives on one relational structure of this kind, the CP-violating phase is predicted near 270° (253–292°, tied to θ23; close to maximal CP violation); current global fits weakly prefer 180–215°. Tested by: DUNE and Hyper-Kamiokande.

Quark pairing gap. With B fixed by the proton, neutron stars meet the observed limits only for a pairing gap of about 82–97 MeV (75–108 MeV across the proton's range of B). Theoretical estimates span 50–150 MeV. Falsified by: a firm determination of the gap outside this band, from lattice or effective-theory calculations or from neutron-star cooling and merger observations.

Neutrinoless double beta decay. The one mass the Higgs does not give exists only if neutrinos are their own antiparticles (Majorana, 1937). Then double beta decay with no neutrinos (Furry, 1939) occurs, with an effective mass between 1.3 and 3.9 meV. Today's limit is 28 to 122 meV (KamLAND-Zen, 2024), and planned experiments reach about 10 to 20 meV, so this is a real prediction that cannot be checked for a long time. Falsified by: proof that neutrinos are not their own antiparticles, or an effective mass clearly outside this range.

Dark matter as a cold, chargeless wave. The main line's candidate is the majoron: spin 0, no charge, decaying only to neutrinos, and cold if the universe started colder than the settling value f. Its mass is one input, not derived: it follows mJ = 8.8 keV × (6.3 × 1010 GeV ÷ f)4 ÷ angle4, so it can lie anywhere from below 1 eV to 350 keV, and the amount is a measured start condition. No X-ray line is predicted. Falsified by: dark matter found to be a particle that feels the weak force.

The strong coupling. The force strengths meet at 4.6 × 1013 GeV if the three colourless far-side weak pairs of the relationship matrix sit at 2.4 × 108 GeV. This requires αs(MZ) = 0.1178, with the grain of space fixed in advance as the Planck energy divided by √2. The most precise single determination, 0.11873 ± 0.00056 (Dalla Brida and others, 2025), lies 1.7 standard deviations above it and does not yet decide. Falsified by: a strong coupling measured to about 0.3% that matches neither value, or an observed proton decay.

Values never yet measured. The carrier and energy matrices hold values no experiment has reached: the Higgs's coupling to itself (three-way 191 GeV, four-way 0.776) and to two W bosons at once (0.213); the Higgs's couplings to the charm quark, the electron and the light quarks, each its mass divided by 246 GeV; the binding of true muonium, 1.407 keV, of tauonium, 23.66 keV, and of a proton with a tau, 16.35 keV. Most of these are standard physics computed in the framework's form rather than new physics, so they test the framework's bookkeeping. Falsified by: any of them measured clearly different.

Five further predictions. (1) The quark unitarity-triangle angle γ = 64.1° to 67.6° (best 66.1°; LHCb and Belle II reach about 1° by about 2030). (2) The first-row quark-mixing shortfall must shrink to nothing. (3) No primordial gravitational waves: r < 10−11 (LiteBIRD, CMB-S4); this follows from the majoron dark-matter lead, not the core. (4) The tau mass from rule 7: 1776.969 MeV (Belle II). (5) If dark matter is the cold majoron wave, there is no X-ray line (a lead, not part of the core). With the neutrino sum (0.0591–0.0594 eV, depending on the measured splittings), the neutrino timing angle, the double-beta mass and the neutron-star maximum, and with the strong coupling above, these make ten dated predictions, collected on The problems that blocked a theory of everything and All of physics.

What would break the framework. Any new force carrier, any mirror particle or a fourth generation; a neutrino mass sum clearly below 0.059 eV, or inverted ordering; a precisely measured neutron star above about 2.15 solar masses; dark matter that feels the weak force.

The standing in detail

Derived from the rules: the eight-part number system (Hurwitz) and the strong force with exactly three colours; with one stated reading each, which other forces exist (8 | 3 | 1), every particle of a generation (16 states, the up and electron partners included) with its measured charge, one-handed matter in the measured arrangement with no mirror states and one i throughout, which mass links exist (each paired state to exactly one single state), and the same forces for all generations; at least three generations; the form of gravity; mass as balance energy and the shape and reach of each force. Measured rules, very accurate but not derived: the lepton 45° balance (15 parts in a million), the twist (one part in a million) and the shared lepton matrix. Passing strict tests: the down quark, the up quark (with the decay value of Q) and the anomaly sums. Consistent with today's bound but not yet tested: the neutrino mass total of 0.0591 to 0.0594 eV. Met by construction, not tests: compact stars inside the observed window of 2.0–2.15 solar masses, whose masses set the pairing-gap band. Reproduced with fitted numbers: fission at 166 MeV against 173. Taken from measurement, about 28 measured numbers, against about 34 for the Standard Model with gravity and cosmology counted the same way (the 28 assumes every pending measurement agrees; counting only savings already confirmed it is 32, plus six stated choices; see Inputs and predictions): the three force strengths, the Higgs balance point, the lepton scale and twist, five quark masses, quark mixing and the strong-force angle, two neutrino splittings and four neutrino mixing numbers, the strength of gravity, the amounts of dark energy and dark matter, the matter excess and the two numbers for the seeds of galaxies. The Higgs mass follows from the rule at the grain of space and awaits measurement. Strictly counted, the framework removes the tau mass and the down quark mass from the Standard Model's list; the lightest neutrino mass, the Higgs mass and two neutrino mixing numbers follow only if pending measurements agree. Still open, each needing a new idea: the strengths at the grain of space, that is, the three force strengths (the Standard Model's states alone miss by 8.9% at the Z mass; with the far-side weak pairs the test is the strong coupling, which awaits measurement) together with gravity's strength; why the Higgs side settles, and why at 246 GeV; how the Higgs link tells the three placements apart, a single question that holds the heavy quark masses, the small tilt of quark mixing and the twist (2/9, the same open number as the electron's mass); why three generations and no more, which rests on the nothing-extra rule and on measurement (the matrix has exactly three sizes, a hint only); the matter excess, allowed but not predicted, through a start that hands its energy straight to the hidden partners (one number); dark matter's mass and amount, now that the main line has a candidate, the majoron, alongside the reading of dark matter as partly perspective; where the seeds of galaxies came from (their size and tilt are measured and not derived); where quantum probabilities come from; and whether the entanglement behind gravity and the balance behind the proton's mass are one relationship. Any new force carrier, mirror particle or fourth generation, a neutrino mass total clearly below 0.059 eV, or a neutron star well above 2.15 solar masses would break it.

  1. Quark masses. The bottom, charm and strange masses are measured inputs; only the down and up links pass strict tests. The up quark needs no lattice correction (η → 3π route), but the value of Q it uses comes from a dispersion analysis of measured decays, and its ±3% uncertainty dominates. Every relation tried for the bottom mass (including b = τ at the left–right scale, 26% off, and the 45° colour rule, which misses charm by 15–19 standard deviations) fails. The scale-independent balance angles, 52.15° (up-type) and 48.06° (down-type; 48.15° ± 0.015° with the latest lattice masses) against 45° for leptons, are the targets a derivation must meet. As shapes, the leptons are a cone and the quarks lean toward a spiral, but neither is an exact quark rule, and the mixing tilt is the amount by which the two quark cones do not share an axis. The pushes of quarks and leptons on each other's masses are real but small and close none of these gaps; any quark rule must also name the footing (the energy) on which it holds. Quark mixing belongs with these masses: the simplest mass link gives no mixing, and the small measured tilt between the up and down placements is not yet produced. The strongest lead is the far-side press: one heavy far-side state that mixes only with the down quark's single partner fits all ten quark numbers at 1.6σ with nine free numbers, which is a lead, not a result. Running the masses to the Planck energy shows the quark ladders' shapes do not change with energy, so the missing rule must act on mass ratios.
  2. Force groups. On the main, relationship-matrix picture, the strong force with three colours follows from the starting principles, and with one stated reading each the measured 8, 3 and 1 carriers, the weak hypercharges and one-handed matter follow (section 1); the strengths are measured, and “at most three” generations rests on the nothing-extra rule and on measurement. On the alternative 4 × 4 picture, kept on record, an octonionic relation matrix allows exactly three generations, but the weak force, hypercharge and left-handedness are taken from measurement. The minimal particle content also needs at least one more Higgs field to match the measured masses (Georgi and Jarlskog, 1979), and every version that passes the proton-lifetime limit either puts left-handedness in by hand or adds a field. Read two at a time, the main picture's matrix lacks the up-quark and electron partners; read three at a time (section 1), it holds them with the measured charges, and the strength test then misses by 8.9% ± 0.1% at the Z mass (two loops, standard method; 10.2% at one loop), so only the strengths remain under this heading. The gap closes if the three colourless far-side weak pairs sit at 2.4 × 108 GeV, which requires a strong coupling of 0.1178, against 0.11873 ± 0.00056 measured. Awaiting measurement: the criterion stays partly met until that measurement returns.
  3. The twist. δ = 2/9 matches the data at 0.4σ but is not derived. Rules that reproduce it for the leptons (δ = Q/3 and related forms) fail for both quark families, so it remains a measured input, 0.222222 to about one part in a million, after every route tried to derive it failed. It cannot be exactly 2/9 if the 45° balance is also exact: the two together miss the electron-to-muon mass ratio by 10 parts per million. Reading 2/9 as a fraction of a ring is withdrawn as an explanation. The twist and the electron's mass are one open number, not two.
  4. The matter–antimatter excess. Not predicted (about 4,000 times short with the lepton relation read literally); allowed in one narrow corner (section 1). With the main line's dark matter, the hot start that corner needs would make most of the dark matter hot, so the excess is now carried by a start that hands its energy straight to the hidden partners, at the cost of one number: a cold start with handover 0.0017, taken as a measured start condition. This is a lead.
  5. Shared relation matrix. The rule that both members of a pair share one relation matrix holds for leptons (it fixes the neutrino sector) but fails for quark pairs (mt/mb = 41).
  6. Newton's constant. The entanglement route fixes G in terms of the entanglement density of the medium, but the density itself has not been computed from first principles. Gravity's strength is tied to the force strengths at the grain of space, and the two form one open problem; the best simple guess misses the grain energy by a factor of 62.
  7. The Higgs side and the generations. Why the far side of the matrix settles, and at 246 GeV, is not derived, nor how the Higgs link tells the three placements apart; the heavy quark masses, the mixing tilt and the twist are this one question. The matrix's three sizes hint at, but do not derive, exactly three generations.
  8. Gravity and the proton's mass. Whether the entanglement that gives gravity and the colour balance that gives the proton's mass are one relationship is not shown.
  9. Dark matter. No state of the matrix itself is dark matter: the only chargeless state, the hidden neutrino partner, decays far too quickly, and dark matter as a response of entanglement fails published tests. With matter number never a force, the main line has a candidate without adding a state, the majoron, and reads dark matter as partly perspective (galaxies) and partly this real wave (clusters, the early universe). Its mass is not fixed (it follows mJ = 8.8 keV × (6.3 × 1010 GeV ÷ f)4 ÷ angle4) and its amount is a measured start condition; this is a lead. Dark energy's size is set by the grain and the surface together; its amount is one measured number, and no law for how it changes is derived.
  10. Dynamics of the transitioning geometry and of the initial release. Both are described qualitatively and are consistent with observation, but neither has yet been written as a dynamical equation.
  11. The solar centre. The hypothesis of a leaking central object is not excluded; helioseismology near the core is the test.
  12. The containment pressure and the QCD vacuum. The standard QCD vacuum-energy estimate is about 6.7 times larger than B. This gap is known in the literature and is not resolved here.

4. Hypotheses tested and rejected

Rejected hypotheses are reported so that the evidence is complete.

  • Quark link strengths of 3/6, 4/6 and 5/6 by analogy with the leptons miss the data by about seven standard deviations.
  • Simple fractional patterns for the quark twists fit the data they were built from but fail on independent checks.
  • Generations as three different shapes of defect in the medium fail, because the generations have identical charges and spin; the same shape in three placements works for the leptons.
  • Four quark colours, and a neutrino link of the minimal SO(10) form with no free parameters, fail.
  • Neutrino mass set by today's temperature fails (the required push differs 8000-fold between reactor and atmospheric neutrinos), and antimatter that is on average colder and hidden is excluded at the level of an equal hidden half (Cohen, De Rújula and Glashow, 1998); a small fraction in cold compact objects is allowed.
  • Proportional or opposite shifts of the balance angle for the up-type quarks, and a charge-ratio rule, do not reproduce the measured angles.
  • Mass bands among the hadrons show no clustering beyond chance; among the twelve elementary masses the clustering is suggestive (p ≈ 0.1) but not significant.
  • From the carrier matrices: the balance that would cancel the Higgs's sensitivity to high energies (needs a 313 GeV Higgs), zeros in the quark mass matrices, and equal entries all fail to give the open masses.
  • The Higgs built from top-quark pairs, six far-side parts taken as real particles (they widen the strength gap), and a rule in which the quarks share one common state all fail.
  • One shared missing variable behind every mass offset, and the tiers of the scale view as the generations, fail.
  • Dark matter as perspective alone fails for colliding clusters and the cosmic microwave background; a hot start for the majoron fails (73% of the dark matter would be hot); the settling piece as the start of the universe fails.
  • Five patterns in the size matrix, and leftover balance points as hidden masses, are at chance level; the quark ladders do not become 45° cones at any energy up to the Planck energy.
  • Fixing the quark fold's strength by making the down ladder 45° fails (best 1.38 times off).
