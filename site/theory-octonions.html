The Strangest Numbers: the Story of the Octonions

The idea that the particles of nature are written in the oldest and oddest number systems mathematics has, and the story on which TRPG is built.

Chapter 1. Four kinds of number

Children learn to count with ordinary numbers. Later they meet negative numbers, then fractions, and some meet the “imaginary” number i, whose square is −1. Put ordinary and imaginary together and you get the complex numbers, two-dimensional numbers that turn out to describe every wave and every quantum particle.

In 1843 William Rowan Hamilton, walking along a canal in Dublin, found four-dimensional numbers, the quaternions. He was so excited he carved the formula into a bridge. Weeks later his friend John Graves, and soon after Arthur Cayley, found eight-dimensional numbers, the octonions. They are strange: multiply three of them in a different grouping and you get a different answer.

Then in 1898 Adolf Hurwitz proved something astonishing. There are only four number systems where you can always divide and where size behaves sensibly: the ordinary numbers (1 dimension), the complex numbers (2), the quaternions (4) and the octonions (8). No fifth exists.

Chapter 2. Physics in the oddest numbers

For a century, octonions looked like a mathematical curiosity. Then physicists started to notice them in nature.

  • In 1973 Murat Günaydin and Feza Gürsey showed that if you fix one direction among the octonion’s seven imaginary directions, the symmetry left over is exactly the symmetry of the strong force, with three colours of quark.
  • In the 1990s Geoffrey Dixon built the particles of one generation from all four number systems multiplied together.
  • From 2014 to 2018 Cohl Furey showed that the octonions, combined with the complex numbers, contain a generation of particles with exactly the right electric charges, and the forces that act on them.
  • In 2018 Ivan Todorov and Michel Dubois-Violette found the full set of Standard Model forces inside a special three-by-three table of octonions, the exceptional Jordan algebra.
  • In 2020 Latham Boyle linked that table to the E6 pattern of grand unification. Niels Gresnigt, Adam Gillard and Tejinder Singh have pursued three generations and even mass ratios.

Chapter 3. Why this is exciting

The Standard Model’s forces and charges look like arbitrary choices. Why three colours? Why these charges? Why left-handed? In the octonion picture, they are not choices. They are forced by Hurwitz’s theorem: there is no other number system to use.

Chapter 4. What is missing

This work is careful, peer-reviewed mathematics, published in good journals by a small community. Mostly, though, it gives the pattern of particles and forces, not their numbers: the masses, the strengths, the mixing. Getting all three generations to come out naturally remains hard.

The theory at a glance

Main idea The particles and forces are the structure of the four possible number systems, especially the octonions
New ingredients none; a reading of existing mathematics
Got right three colours, the charges of a generation, the Standard Model forces, as consequences rather than choices
Not yet most masses and strengths; three generations in full

How it relates to TRPG

This is TRPG’s foundation, and TRPG credits it. TRPG’s rules (a full medium with no gaps, sizes kept) force the octonions through Hurwitz’s theorem. From there, Günaydin and Gürsey give the three colours, and Todorov and Dubois-Violette give the force pattern.

What TRPG adds:

  • a reading of the octonion table as a table of relationships seen from one point of view;
  • a three-way reading that fills in the whole generation;
  • numbers on top: the tau mass (through Koide’s rule), the down and up quarks, neutrino masses, and the strong coupling.

If TRPG’s additions turn out wrong, this mathematics still stands. If they turn out right, TRPG will have shown what this structure is for.

The people who built it

Hamilton; Graves and Cayley; Hurwitz; Jordan, von Neumann and Wigner (the exceptional Jordan algebra, 1934); Günaydin and Gürsey; Dixon; Furey; Todorov and Dubois-Violette; Boyle; Gresnigt and Gillard; Singh; Baez for making the octonions readable for everyone.
