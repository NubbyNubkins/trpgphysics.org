The Universe as Music: the Story of String Theory

The idea that every particle is a tiny vibrating string, and that gravity was hiding inside the mathematics all along.

Chapter 1. An accident in 1968

In 1968 a young Italian physicist, Gabriele Veneziano, was trying to describe how protons and neutrons bounce off each other. He found an old formula by the mathematician Leonhard Euler that fitted the data beautifully. Nobody knew why it worked.

Within two years, Yoichiro Nambu, Holger Nielsen and Leonard Susskind discovered the reason. The formula describes tiny vibrating strings. Pluck a guitar string and it can sing many notes. A particle, in this picture, is a string singing one note. A different note is a different particle.

Chapter 2. The particle nobody ordered

As a theory of protons, strings had a problem. Their notes always included one that should not be there: a particle with no mass and a spin of two. In 1974 Joël Scherk and John Schwarz, and independently Tamiaki Yoneya, realised what it was. It was the graviton, the particle of gravity.

Nobody had put gravity into string theory. It came out on its own. For many physicists that was the moment the theory stopped being a curiosity and became a candidate for everything.

Chapter 3. The extra rooms

The mathematics made a demand. Strings are only consistent if space has more dimensions than the three we know: nine in all, plus time. The extra six must be curled up so small that we never notice them, like the thickness of a garden hose seen from a mile away. It looks like a line, but an ant on it can walk around.

In 1984 Michael Green and John Schwarz showed that the theory is free of a deadly kind of inconsistency, and a wave of physicists joined. In 1995 Edward Witten showed that five different string theories were all corners of one larger theory he called M-theory, with eleven dimensions.

Chapter 4. The triumphs

  • In 1996 Andrew Strominger and Cumrun Vafa used strings to count the hidden states of a black hole, and got exactly the number that Jacob Bekenstein and Stephen Hawking had predicted from heat and disorder.
  • In 1997 Juan Maldacena discovered that a world with gravity can be exactly equivalent to a world without gravity on its boundary, like a hologram. This idea, the AdS/CFT correspondence, is now used to study things as different as hot nuclear matter and superconductors.
  • Shinsei Ryu and Tadashi Takayanagi showed in 2006 that the amount of entanglement in such a hologram equals an area in the space inside. Geometry and entanglement turned out to be two views of one thing.

Chapter 5. The problem of too many worlds

The six curled-up dimensions can be folded in an enormous number of ways, perhaps 10⁵⁰⁰ (Raphael Bousso and Joseph Polchinski, 2000). Each way gives a different universe, with different particles and different forces. String theory does not, so far, tell us which fold is ours. So it does not predict the electron’s mass, or the number of generations, or any number we have measured.

It predicted a partner for every particle (supersymmetry). The Large Hadron Collider has looked hard and found none at the energies reached.

Critics, notably Lee Smolin and Peter Woit (both 2006), argue that a theory which can fit almost any world risks fitting none in particular. Supporters reply that the mathematics has been right so often that it is pointing at something real, even if we cannot yet reach it.

Chapter 6. Following the mathematics

String theory is the boldest example in physics of following the mathematics wherever it leads. Sometimes that has worked spectacularly. Paul Dirac’s equation predicted antimatter before anyone saw it. The difference is that, for string theory, reality has not yet checked back. After more than fifty years it remains the most developed and most mathematically consistent attempt at quantum gravity, and the least tested.

The theory at a glance

Main idea Particles are vibrating strings; gravity is one of the vibrations
New ingredients six or seven extra dimensions; supersymmetry
Measured numbers in principle one length; in practice a choice among about 10⁵⁰⁰ foldings
Got right gravity appears automatically; black-hole entropy counted exactly; holography
Not yet any measured number; supersymmetry unseen

How it differs from TRPG

  • Dimensions. String theory needs extra dimensions. TRPG uses none: no extra dimensions and no hidden universes.
  • Numbers. String theory has no measured numbers yet. TRPG puts numbers on the line (strong coupling 0.1178, neutrino sum 0.0591–0.0594 eV), and they could fail.
  • Testing and scrutiny. String theory has been checked by thousands of physicists over fifty years. TRPG has not yet been peer-reviewed.
  • What they share. One version of string theory (Candelas, Horowitz, Strominger and Witten, 1985) arrives at the same E6 family of 27 that TRPG uses. And string theory’s discovery that entanglement builds geometry is in line with TRPG’s idea that gravity is a byproduct of entanglement.
